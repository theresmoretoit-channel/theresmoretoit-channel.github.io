# What Is a Radio Wave Actually Travelling Through?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Here's something that has bothered me for a surprisingly long time.

A radio signal leaves an antenna on Earth.

It crosses more than half a billion kilometres of absolutely nothing.

<!-- S02 -->
And then it arrives at a spacecraft near Jupiter.

Vanishingly faint, spread impossibly thin.

But still the same signal.

<!-- S03 -->
Every wave I can actually picture needs something to wave.

Sound needs air.

Ripples need water.

A wave on a rope needs the rope.

<!-- S04 -->
So if a radio signal travels through empty space...

what exactly is the wave waving?

<!-- S05 -->
For most of the nineteenth century, physicists had an answer they were quite happy with.

Space couldn't really be empty.

It had to be filled with something.

An invisible substance that light could ripple through.

They called it the luminiferous ether.

<!-- S06 -->
And then, in eighteen eighty-seven, two men in Cleveland went looking for it.

They didn't find it.

<!-- S07 -->
So the ether was in trouble.

But the problem wasn't.

If waves need something to wave...

what exactly was moving through empty space?

To understand how physicists got there, we need to go back a little.

<!-- S08 -->
In the early nineteenth century, electricity and magnetism looked like two completely different things.

Then something strange happened.

Hans Christian Ørsted discovered that an electric current could affect a compass needle.

Electricity and magnetism were connected.

<!-- S09 -->
And André-Marie Ampère quickly developed a mathematical description of that connection.

A current flowing through a wire creates a magnetic field around it.

Electricity could create magnetism.

<!-- S10 -->
Then came another surprise.

Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction.

A changing magnetic field could produce an electric current.

Now the connection worked in the other direction too.

<!-- S11 -->
But there was an important detail.

A steady electric current produces a steady magnetic field.

And once nothing is changing...

there is nothing new to propagate outward as a wave.

For radiation, change matters.

<!-- S12 -->
Then there was another problem.

Imagine charging a capacitor.

Electric current flows through the wires...

and charge builds up on two metal plates.

<!-- S13 -->
But between the plates...

there is a gap.

No ordinary electric current crosses it.

And yet the changing electric field still had to play a role.

Otherwise...

the picture didn't add up.

Something was missing.

<!-- S14 -->
Then James Clerk Maxwell took the next step.

He proposed that a changing electric field could have a magnetic effect...

even where no ordinary electric current was flowing.

This was the idea of displacement current.

<!-- S15 -->
Now the two sides finally fit together.

Changing electric and magnetic fields were not separate phenomena.

They were part of one electromagnetic system.

<!-- S16 -->
And that system allowed something extraordinary.

A disturbance could propagate through space.

Not through water.

Not through air.

But as a changing electromagnetic field.

<!-- S17 -->
It is tempting to imagine this as one field creating the other...

then the other creating the first...

over and over again.

That picture is useful.

But reality is a little subtler.

The electric and magnetic fields are parts of the same electromagnetic wave.

<!-- S18 -->
Maxwell's equations predicted that these disturbances should travel at a very specific speed.

So he calculated it.

The answer was approximately three hundred thousand kilometres per second.

<!-- S18b -->
Does that number seem familiar?

<!-- S19 -->
It was the speed of light.

<!-- S19b -->
And that was one of the great surprises of nineteenth-century physics.

Light itself appeared to be an electromagnetic wave.

<!-- S20 -->
But a prediction is not the same thing as an experiment.

Someone had to show that these waves actually existed.

<!-- S21 -->
In the eighteen eighties, Heinrich Hertz did exactly that.

He generated electromagnetic waves.

And detected them.

<!-- S22 -->
Maxwell's strange mathematical prediction was real.

And yes.

That Hertz.

The same unit you see when someone tells you the frequency of a radio signal.

<!-- S23 -->
At almost exactly the same time, physics was discovering something else.

The waves were real.

But the medium they were supposed to travel through...

could not be found.

<!-- S24 -->
In eighteen eighty-seven, Albert Michelson and Edward Morley were looking for evidence of that medium.

The ether.

<!-- S25 -->
But Maxwell himself did not think this meant the ether was gone.

He believed his electromagnetic theory was describing phenomena that still existed in a physical medium.

The ether was not dead yet.

<!-- S26 -->
It took decades, and a major change in the way physicists thought about space and time, before the idea of an ether became unnecessary.

The wave no longer needed an invisible mechanical substance to travel through.

<!-- S27 -->
And something else was becoming clear.

For thousands of years, there had been one kind of electromagnetic radiation humans knew very well.

Light.

<!-- S28 -->
Then the spectrum started expanding.

Infrared.

Ultraviolet.

And eventually...

radio waves.

At first, they looked like completely different phenomena.

<!-- S29 -->
Radio waves.

Microwaves.

Infrared.

Visible light.

Ultraviolet.

X-rays.

Gamma rays.

<!-- S30 -->
They are not seven different things.

Maxwell's theory, and Hertz's experiments, revealed the connection.

They are all electromagnetic radiation.

<!-- S31 -->
They differ mainly in frequency and wavelength.

And those differences give them very different properties.

But underneath...

they belong to the same electromagnetic family.

<!-- S32 -->
So when your radio receives a signal...

when your phone talks to a Wi-Fi router...

when Bluetooth connects your headphones...

or when light from the Sun reaches your eyes...

you are dealing with different parts of the same electromagnetic spectrum.

<!-- S33 -->
And humans became extremely good at using it.

We build antennas that deliberately launch electromagnetic waves.

Other antennas detect them.

<!-- S34 -->
Which raises a slightly ridiculous question.

If radio waves and visible light are both electromagnetic radiation...

could you build an antenna that transmits light?

<!-- S35 -->
Actually...

the antenna is not the difficult part.

For green light, a simple half-wave dipole would be only a few hundred nanometres long.

That is tiny.

But not impossible to manufacture.

<!-- S36 -->
The real problem is making it oscillate.

Visible light has a frequency of hundreds of trillions of cycles per second.

We can build a nanoscale antenna.

But driving it with ordinary electronics is another matter entirely.

<!-- S37 -->
And now we can return to that signal leaving Earth.

As an electromagnetic wave travels farther from its source...

it spreads out.

<!-- S38 -->
Imagine the energy from the transmitter expanding outward through space.

A little farther away, the same energy is spread over a larger area.

And farther still...

over a much larger one.

<!-- S39 -->
Nothing is necessarily "wearing out".

The wave is simply spreading.

The farther you get from the source...

the less of that energy reaches any particular area.

<!-- S40 -->
For an ideal source radiating equally in all directions...

the available power per square metre falls with the square of the distance.

Twice as far away...

only a quarter as much.

Ten times farther...

one hundred times less.

<!-- S41 -->
And yet...

with the right transmitter...

the right antenna...

and a sensitive enough receiver...

even an incredibly tiny fraction of that signal can still be detected.

<!-- S42 -->
That is how we can send signals across the planet.

Across the Solar System.

And turn tiny disturbances in an electromagnetic field into sound.

Images.

Data.

Messages.

<!-- S43 -->
And now look at what we can do with all of this.

We can describe electromagnetic fields with extraordinary precision.

We can predict how they behave.

We use them to see.

To communicate.

To understand the universe.

<!-- S44 -->
But if you keep asking why...

the original question comes back.

A water wave has water.

A sound wave has air.

A wave on a rope has a rope.

<!-- S45 -->
An electromagnetic wave has...

the electromagnetic field.

<!-- S46 -->
That is the answer.

But it may not feel like an answer.

Because now we have simply replaced one question with another.

<!-- S47 -->
What exactly is a field?

Is it a physical thing?

Or just a mathematical way of describing what happens at every point in space?

<!-- S48 -->
Modern physics goes further.

In quantum field theory, the electromagnetic field is fundamental.

What we call a photon is an excitation of that field.

And this description works extraordinarily well.

<!-- S49 -->
But describing something with extraordinary precision...

and answering what it ultimately *is*...

are not necessarily the same question.

<!-- S50 -->
Maybe that is the part I find most fascinating.

We stopped looking for the ether.

And learned how to describe electromagnetic fields so well that we built the modern world on top of them.

<!-- S51 -->
But the deeper you push the question...

the less it sounds like something from a physics textbook.

What is actually waving?

<!-- S52 -->
The answer is:

the electromagnetic field.

And then, almost immediately, comes the next question.

What exactly is a field?
