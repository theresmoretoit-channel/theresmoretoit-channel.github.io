# What Happens When the World Thaws?
**Script v0.5** (množství nad deset číslicí)

<!-- S01 -->
In 2023, scientists took a lump of mud
from 16 metres under a lake
in Siberia.
They thawed it out in a lab,
and a virus inside it
was still infectious.

<!-- S02 -->
It had been frozen
for more than 48,000 years.
And it could still infect a living cell:
an amoeba, not a human.

<!-- S03 -->
The ground it came from is thawing.
So what else is in there?
Diseases that vanished long ago?
Or something that could make
the planet even warmer?

<!-- S04 -->
Some of this is already happening.
But the biggest danger in that ground
may not be the one you're expecting.

<!-- S05 -->
The frozen ground is called permafrost:
ground that stays at or below freezing
for at least two years in a row.

<!-- S06 -->
It lies under about 15 per cent
of the land in the Northern Hemisphere.
Siberia, Alaska, northern Canada,
the ice-free edges of Greenland.

<!-- S07 -->
And it isn't just ice.
It's soil, rock and frozen water,
packed with the remains
of plants and animals.

<!-- S08 -->
In places, it reaches down
more than a kilometre.
And some of it formed
hundreds of thousands of years ago.

<!-- S09 -->
Here's what happens when it thaws.
This is Batagaika, in Yakutia.
In the 1960s, it was a gully
across a hillside stripped of its forest.

<!-- S10 -->
Today, it's a crater
almost two kilometres long.
And its walls are still crumbling back,
on average about ten metres a year.

<!-- S11 -->
Without the ice holding it together,
the ground simply slumps and collapses.
And whatever was frozen inside
meets air and water again.

<!-- S11b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S12 -->
So let's start with the scary question.
Could something in there
make us sick?

<!-- S13 -->
That virus from 2023 wasn't the first.
In 2014, the same team in France
revived one from northeast Siberia
that was about 30,000 years old.

<!-- S14 -->
By now, they've revived about a dozen.
And every single one of them
infects amoebas, not people.

<!-- S15 -->
That's on purpose.
Amoebas are the bait:
they let you fish out ancient viruses
without risking human ones.

<!-- S16 -->
So yes, something frozen
for tens of thousands of years
can still infect a living cell.
But an amoeba virus
isn't a human disease.

<!-- S17 -->
For something that can kill people,
we have to go to Yamal,
a peninsula in Arctic Russia.
Summer, 2016.

<!-- S18 -->
After years of warming,
capped by a heatwave,
the ground had thawed deeper than usual.
Then the reindeer started dying.
Of anthrax.

<!-- S19 -->
More than 2,000 of them.
Thirty-six people were infected,
and a 12-year-old boy died.

<!-- S20 -->
Anthrax spores can survive
in the soil for decades.
The last outbreak in the area
had been in 1941.

<!-- S21 -->
The leading explanation:
the thaw reached old infected carcasses,
and the spores came back to the surface.

<!-- S22 -->
But it wasn't only the thaw.
Vaccination had stopped in 2007,
and the herds had grown huge.
The thaw was the trigger,
not the whole cause.

<!-- S23 -->
Scientists do take this seriously.
Nobody knows everything
that's frozen in there.
But there's no evidence that a wave
of prehistoric diseases
is about to come out of the Arctic.

<!-- S24 -->
The bigger danger
isn't something waking up.
It's something breaking down.

<!-- S25 -->
For thousands of years,
Arctic plants grew every summer,
pulled carbon dioxide out of the air,
and died.

<!-- S26 -->
In the cold, they barely rotted.
Year after year, the carbon piled up
and froze into the ground.

<!-- S27 -->
So how much is down there?
Let's compare it with something
we already know.

<!-- S28 -->
Since 1850, by burning coal, oil and gas,
humanity has put about
500 billion tonnes
of carbon into the air.

<!-- S29 -->
The soils of the permafrost region
hold about three times as much.

<!-- S30 -->
Around 1.5 trillion tonnes.
More than the entire atmosphere
holds today.

<!-- S31 -->
More than half of it is frozen solid.
The rest lies in soil
that thaws only briefly each summer.
Either way, it's the cold
that keeps it there.

<!-- S32 -->
It won't all come out.
If emissions keep climbing,
scientists estimate that perhaps
5 to 15 per cent of it
could escape this century.

<!-- S33 -->
That sounds small.
But 10 per cent of that pile
is more than a quarter
of everything we've burned so far.

<!-- S34 -->
When the ground thaws,
ordinary soil microbes
finally get to eat.

<!-- S35 -->
Where there's plenty of oxygen,
they turn that old carbon
into carbon dioxide.
In waterlogged ground,
partly into methane.

<!-- S36 -->
Methane traps about 80 times
more heat than carbon dioxide
over 20 years,
though it breaks down
in about a decade.

<!-- S37 -->
Either way, the result is more heat.
And more heat means more thawing,
which releases more carbon.
It's called
the permafrost carbon feedback.

<!-- S38 -->
It won't happen all at once.
It's slow, uneven,
and different from place to place.

<!-- S39 -->
But once that carbon is in the air,
we can't freeze it back into the ground.

<!-- S40 -->
And there's a much faster way
to get carbon out of the ground.
Fire.

<!-- S41 -->
In 2007, lightning set fire
to the tundra of Alaska's North Slope.
The Anaktuvuk River fire burned
more than 1,000 square kilometres.

<!-- S42 -->
In that spot, the lake sediments
show no sign of fire
for about 5,000 years.

<!-- S43 -->
And that single fire released
about as much carbon
as the entire Arctic tundra
used to absorb in a whole year.

<!-- S44 -->
The fire also burned away
the plants and peat
that kept the ground insulated.
Within seven years,
a third of the burned area
had started to sink.

<!-- S45 -->
And Arctic fires are getting bigger.
In 2020, fires inside the Arctic Circle
broke the record for emissions.

<!-- S46 -->
Some of them don't even go out.
They smoulder underground all winter
and flare up again in spring.
They're called zombie fires.

<!-- S47 -->
Now put it all together.
The thaw, the microbes, the fires.

<!-- S48 -->
For thousands of years,
the Arctic tundra did one thing
with carbon.
It stored it.

<!-- S49 -->
In 2024, the yearly Arctic report
from NOAA concluded
that this is no longer true.

<!-- S50 -->
Once the fires are counted,
the tundra now releases
more carbon dioxide
than it takes in.

<!-- S51 -->
Only a little, for now.
The forests further south
still absorb more than they lose.

<!-- S52 -->
But the ground that spent millennia
pulling carbon out of the air
has started giving it back.

<!-- S53 -->
For the people who live up there,
this isn't a future problem.
The ground under their homes
is already moving.

<!-- S54 -->
By 2050, about 70 per cent
of the roads, buildings and pipelines
on permafrost will stand on ground
that could thaw near the surface.

<!-- S55 -->
So what happens when the world thaws?
Probably not a prehistoric plague.
The bigger danger is a vast store
of carbon, kept out of the atmosphere
by the cold, leaking back into it.

<!-- S56 -->
How much warming it will add
is still uncertain.
But uncertain doesn't mean small.

<!-- S57 -->
The greatest danger in the ice
isn't something waking up.
It's something breaking down.

And the less we warm the Arctic,
the more of it stays frozen.
