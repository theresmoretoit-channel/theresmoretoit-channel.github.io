<!-- S01 -->
Two genders.
Fifty-five genders.
Hundreds of genders.
Before we start counting,
what exactly goes on the list?

<!-- S02 -->
Imagine a form with three options:
woman, man, and non-binary.
Now add another option: trans woman.
Have we discovered another gender?
Or added a more specific description?

<!-- S03 -->
A trans woman can describe herself
as both trans and a woman.
Those answers overlap.
Counting the labels as separate boxes
already gives us a problem.

<!-- S04 -->
Now imagine that someone replies:
"But humans reproduce with sperm and eggs."
That's a statement about biology.
It doesn't tell us how many descriptions
belong on that form.

<!-- S05 -->
This is how an argument about a number
can hide an argument about a definition.
One person counts reproductive categories.
Another counts identities.
Someone else counts words.

<!-- S06 -->
There is a real example of the problem.
In 2014, Facebook announced
sixty custom gender options
for people using its German interface.

<!-- S07 -->
Sixty options in a profile menu.
The announcement described a way
for people to express their identities.
It wasn't the discovery
of sixty kinds of reproductive system.

<!-- S08 -->
But saying "it's just a menu"
doesn't answer everything either.
Why did people want those options?
What were the original two boxes missing?

<!-- S09 -->
To understand that,
we need to separate three things:
the body, the expectations around it,
and the person living in it.
They are connected.
They aren't interchangeable.

<!-- S10 -->
Let's start with the body.
Human sexual reproduction involves
two kinds of reproductive cell:
sperm and eggs.
These are called gametes.

<!-- S11 -->
In reproductive biology,
the male role is associated with sperm,
and the female role with eggs.
That distinction gives the categories
a biological basis.

<!-- S12 -->
It doesn't mean that every individual
must be producing those cells right now.
A boy doesn't become male at puberty.
An infertile man doesn't stop being male.

<!-- S13 -->
Equally, a woman doesn't stop being female
when she reaches menopause.
Reproductive organisation
and current fertility
are different things.

<!-- S14 -->
So the argument "some women can't
have children" doesn't, by itself,
make biological sex impossible to define.
It shows why fertility alone
is a poor test for an individual.

<!-- S15 -->
But the body has more going on
than two labels on a diagram.
Sex development involves chromosomes,
genes, gonads, hormones,
and the way tissues respond to them.

<!-- S16 -->
Usually, these produce a familiar pattern.
XY chromosomes, testes,
and typically male anatomy.
Or XX chromosomes, ovaries,
and typically female anatomy.

<!-- S17 -->
Usually isn't the same as always.
Consider a condition called
complete androgen insensitivity syndrome.
The name is a mouthful.
The mechanism matters more.

<!-- S18 -->
Someone with this condition
has XY chromosomes and develops testes.
But their cells do not respond
to androgens, including testosterone.
The signal is there.
The cells cannot receive it.

<!-- S19 -->
As a result, they are born
with typically female external genitals.
A different hormone from the testes
prevents the uterus from developing.
Chromosomes alone don't tell you
all of that anatomy.

<!-- S20 -->
This is one example of a difference
in sex development.
Intersex is an umbrella term
used for variations in sex characteristics.
It doesn't describe one uniform body type.

<!-- S21 -->
These variations don't introduce
a third kind of human gamete.
Two reproductive roles can coexist
with variation in how bodies develop.

<!-- S22 -->
Now move from the body
to the expectations around it.
Who is supposed to be tough?
Who is supposed to do the caring?
What counts as masculine or feminine?

<!-- S23 -->
These are questions about gender roles.
They concern what a society expects,
encourages, or discourages.
Those expectations can change
between places and over time.

<!-- S24 -->
A man caring for his children,
or a woman who dislikes dresses,
hasn't automatically changed gender.
Breaking a stereotype
and identifying as another gender
are different things.

<!-- S25 -->
Gender identity is a person's
internal sense of their own gender.
For some people, being a man or a woman
fits the sex they were assigned at birth.
For others, it doesn't.

<!-- S26 -->
Cisgender describes that alignment.
Transgender describes a gender identity
that differs from the sex assigned at birth.
These words describe a relationship.
They aren't names for extra gametes.

<!-- S26b -->
A definition tells us what a word means.
It doesn't explain
where gender identity comes from.

<!-- S27 -->
Non-binary is an umbrella term
for identities that aren't exclusively
man or woman.
It doesn't mean that everyone using it
has exactly the same experience.

<!-- S28 -->
Gender expression is how someone
presents themselves through clothing,
appearance or behaviour.
A skirt tells you what someone is wearing.
It doesn't tell you their chromosomes
or their gender identity.

<!-- S29 -->
So "gender" needs some context.
Are we discussing social expectations,
personal identity, or presentation?
A conversation can slide between them
without anyone noticing.

<!-- S30 -->
Now we can return to those long lists.
Imagine writing "woman" on one card
and "cisgender woman" on another.
A third card says "transgender woman".

<!-- S31 -->
We now have three cards.
But the second and third
are more specific descriptions
within the first category.
Three labels do not automatically mean
three separate genders.

<!-- S32 -->
So if someone says "fifty-five",
ask to see the list.
You can count its entries exactly.
But to count distinct genders,
you need to decide which labels overlap
and which name separate categories.

<!-- S32b -->
And there's no universally agreed rule
for where one label stops
and another begins.

<!-- S33 -->
This is also why "there are infinitely many"
needs an explanation.
An open-ended vocabulary
isn't a measurement of infinity.
It means the list hasn't been closed.

<!-- S34 -->
You might compare this with colours.
Is turquoise a separate colour,
or a kind of blue or green?
The answer depends partly
on how finely you divide your categories.

<!-- S35 -->
But that comparison has a limit.
It illustrates a problem with naming.
It doesn't establish that gender,
or biological sex,
is one smooth rainbow-shaped scale.
An analogy isn't a biological model.

<!-- S36 -->
"But people invented these words."
Yes. Money depends on human rules too.
That doesn't make your rent imaginary.
A description can matter in someone's life
without naming a new reproductive category.

<!-- S37 -->
Still, giving something a name
doesn't automatically explain it.
We can ask what a term includes,
and whether it helps answer our question.

<!-- S38 -->
That applies to short definitions too.
Take the question:
"What makes someone a woman?"
Before choosing a test,
notice which question you want it to answer.

<!-- S39 -->
If you mean an adult human female,
you are using a sex-based definition.
You then need to say what you mean
by female, and how you apply it
to the individual in front of you.

<!-- S40 -->
If you mean a gender identity,
you are asking about the person's
sense of being a woman.
Answering that question
with a chromosome count
changes the subject being measured.

<!-- S41 -->
This doesn't make every disagreement
an innocent misunderstanding.
People can understand the distinction
and still disagree about which category
should matter in a particular setting.

<!-- S42 -->
A vocabulary lesson won't settle
all of those arguments.
It can stop us treating
an answer to one question
as an automatic answer to every other one.

<!-- S43 -->
So how many genders are there?
For human reproductive roles,
we have the distinction
between sperm and eggs.
For individual sex characteristics,
we also need to describe variation.

<!-- S44 -->
For gender identities,
there isn't a single universally agreed
number of distinct categories.
Man and woman don't describe everyone,
and broader and narrower labels
can overlap.

<!-- S45 -->
For the options on a particular form,
there is an exact answer.
Count them.
Just don't mistake the design of the form
for a discovery about the whole species.

<!-- S46 -->
The strange thing isn't that we have
so many words to describe people.
It's that we expect one number
to describe bodies, identities,
and the rules we build around them.

<!-- S47 -->
Before answering "two" or "fifty-five",
finish the question.
How many... of what?
