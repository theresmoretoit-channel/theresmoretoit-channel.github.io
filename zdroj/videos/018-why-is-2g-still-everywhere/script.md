# 2G Is Ancient. So Why Are Lifts, Cars and Alarms Still Using It?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you're in a lift.

You press the button.

The doors slide shut.

The lift starts moving.

Everything is perfectly normal.

Until, one day, it isn't.

<!-- S02 -->
The lift stops between floors.

So you press the emergency button.

You expect someone to answer.

But nobody does.

<!-- S03 -->
Not because the button is broken.

Not because the lift
has no electricity.

But because the mobile network
that the emergency phone uses...

has been switched off.

<!-- S04 -->
Welcome to the surprisingly
complicated problem
of turning off 2G.

<!-- S05 -->
Because 2G isn't just something
your old Nokia used
twenty years ago.

It is still quietly sitting
inside an astonishing
number of things.

<!-- S06 -->
Things we generally expect
to work for decades.

Things like lifts.

Alarms.

Industrial equipment.

Meters.

Cars.

<!-- S07 -->
And, rather awkwardly...

emergency systems.

<!-- S08 -->
Let's start with
the obvious question.

Why are we still talking about 2G?

GSM first appeared commercially
in the early 1990s.

It is ancient.

By technology standards,
it is practically a fossil.

<!-- S09 -->
Today we have 4G.

5G.

Gigabit mobile connections.

We can stream 4K video from a phone
while sitting on a train.

<!-- S10 -->
So surely it makes sense
to get rid of
this ancient technology?

Well...

For your smartphone, probably.

For a lift?

Not so much.

<!-- S11 -->
A lift doesn't need 5G.

It doesn't need Netflix.

It doesn't need
a gigabit connection.

It doesn't even need the internet.

<!-- S12 -->
It just needs to be able
to say this.

"Help. I'm stuck between floors."

That's basically it.

<!-- S13 -->
An emergency communicator in a lift
might simply establish a voice call
with a service centre.

And GSM is perfectly capable
of doing that.

<!-- S14 -->
The same applies
to many alarm systems.

Some industrial equipment.

Some tracking devices.

Some meters.

<!-- S15 -->
They don't need
enormous bandwidth.

They need a tiny, reliable
communication channel.

And that's exactly the sort of thing
GSM was rather good at.

<!-- S16 -->
And then there was GPRS.

If you were around
in the early 2000s,
you may remember it.

GPRS was basically
mobile data over GSM.

And it was...

slow.

Very slow.

<!-- S17 -->
We are talking about
tens of kilobits per second.

Today, a teenager would probably
describe that as a network outage.

<!-- S18 -->
But some of us actually used it.

There were mobile data packages
that allowed you to use GPRS
as your internet connection.

<!-- S19 -->
Yes.

As your actual internet connection.

Some of us knew no better.

And somehow...

we survived.

<!-- S20 -->
The interesting thing is that GPRS
is still perfectly adequate
for some machines.

A device might only need
to send a few hundred bytes.

<!-- S21 -->
Something like this.

Temperature, 21.4.

Pressure, 1012.

Status, OK.

That's it.

You don't need 5G for that.

You barely need what we'd normally
call the internet.

<!-- S22 -->
And this is where
things get interesting.

2G isn't just used by old phones.

It is used by machines.

<!-- S23 -->
And machines have
a very annoying habit.

If they work...

nobody replaces them.

<!-- S24 -->
A lift can stay in service
for twenty or thirty years.

An industrial machine
can last even longer.

<!-- S25 -->
An alarm system might be installed
and then forgotten about.

A meter can sit somewhere
for fifteen years,
quietly reporting its readings.

<!-- S26 -->
And nobody is going to replace
a perfectly working device
just because the mobile industry
has moved on to another
generation of technology.

Why would they?

It works.

<!-- S27 -->
Now let's talk about cars.

Modern cars contain
an extraordinary amount
of electronics.

<!-- S28 -->
And some older cars have
emergency communication systems
based on 2G or 3G.

This includes older versions
of eCall.

<!-- S29 -->
The European emergency call system
can automatically contact 112
after a serious accident
and transmit information
about the vehicle.

That's rather useful.

<!-- S30 -->
But if the car's
communication module
only understands
an old mobile network...

then switching off that network
creates a rather strange situation.

<!-- S31 -->
The car still works.

The engine works.

The brakes work.

The airbags work.

The radio works.

<!-- S32 -->
You can drive it perfectly normally.

But its emergency
communication system
may no longer be able
to communicate.

<!-- S33 -->
The car hasn't become obsolete.

The network it depends on has.

<!-- S34 -->
Now, the obvious answer is this.

"Fine. Just replace the old module."

And sometimes that is exactly
what will happen.

<!-- S35 -->
But it isn't always that simple.

Imagine thousands of lifts.

Thousands of alarm systems.

<!-- S36 -->
Industrial equipment installed
fifteen years ago.

Vehicles that are
perfectly roadworthy.

Meters installed
across an entire country.

<!-- S37 -->
Who replaces all those devices?

The manufacturer?

The building owner?

The lift maintenance company?

The car owner?

<!-- S38 -->
And what happens if the manufacturer
doesn't even make
the device anymore?

Suddenly, a very cheap GSM modem
has become a rather expensive
engineering project.

<!-- S39 -->
Now, there is a perfectly good reason
for wanting to switch off 2G.

Radio spectrum is limited.

Operators have only so much of it.

<!-- S40 -->
Modern networks can use that spectrum
much more efficiently.

LTE and 5G can provide
vastly more capacity
and much better performance.

<!-- S41 -->
So from the operator's point of view,
keeping an entire old network
running forever
doesn't make much sense.

<!-- S42 -->
Why maintain old equipment
and dedicate spectrum
to a technology
that most people no longer use?

That's a perfectly
reasonable question.

<!-- S43 -->
But there is another question.

How much 2G do we actually need?

<!-- S44 -->
Maybe the answer isn't this.

"Keep the entire 2G network forever."

Maybe it's this.

"Keep a tiny part of it."

<!-- S45 -->
We don't need 2G to carry
millions of smartphone users.

We don't need it
for streaming video.

We don't need it
for normal mobile internet.

<!-- S46 -->
We don't even need
huge amounts of bandwidth.

We might only need
a small number of GSM channels.

Enough for voice, SMS,
basic GPRS data
and emergency systems.

<!-- S47 -->
Most of the spectrum
could still go to 4G and 5G.

The old network would become
a tiny legacy layer.

<!-- S48 -->
And it wouldn't even have to belong
to one particular operator.

It doesn't have to be Vodafone.

Or O2.

Or Deutsche Telekom.

<!-- S49 -->
Imagine there was a shared
European legacy service.

Let's call it...

EU Legacy.

<!-- S50 -->
A lift doesn't care
who owns the tower.

It just needs its old modem
to find the network
and make the call.

<!-- S51 -->
So perhaps the real problem
isn't this.

"We don't have enough capacity."

Perhaps it's this.

"We don't want to reserve
any spectrum for it."

<!-- S52 -->
And here's where it gets strange.

This isn't entirely hypothetical.

<!-- S53 -->
Regulators are aware of the problem.

In May 2026, the European Commission
published a study on exactly this.

What happens to the machines
when the old networks go.

<!-- S54 -->
And the numbers are not small.

If the networks are switched off
without coordination,
64 million vehicles across the EU
could lose eCall.

<!-- S55 -->
64 million.

So the problem isn't this.

"Somebody wants to keep
their old Nokia."

<!-- S56 -->
We're talking about infrastructure.

Safety systems.

Vehicles.

Industrial equipment.

And millions of devices
whose owners may not even realise
which mobile technology they use.

<!-- S57 -->
And what does that study recommend?

Keep at least one circuit-switched
network alive until at least 2030.

<!-- S58 -->
In other words...

keep a legacy network alive.

<!-- S59 -->
And here's the funny thing.

We've solved this sort
of problem before.

Think about the transition
from black-and-white television
to colour.

<!-- S60 -->
When colour television arrived,
we didn't say this.

"Black-and-white televisions
are obsolete. Throw them away."

<!-- S61 -->
The colour television standards
were designed so that older
black-and-white receivers
could still display
the original image.

New televisions could extract
the additional colour information.

<!-- S62 -->
One broadcast.

Old equipment still works.

New equipment
gets the new features.

That is what good backward
compatibility looks like.

<!-- S63 -->
Now, to be fair, mobile networks
are much more complicated.

GSM and LTE are not simply
two versions
of the same technology.

<!-- S64 -->
You can't just add
a little bit of LTE
to a GSM signal
and expect a 1998 Nokia
to understand it.

It won't.

<!-- S65 -->
But that doesn't mean we have to
switch the old system off overnight.

We could run both.

Until the old equipment
is finally gone.

<!-- S66 -->
And perhaps that's
the most important point.

Legacy technology isn't
necessarily bad technology.

Sometimes it's simply old technology
that is still doing its job.

<!-- S67 -->
A GSM modem doesn't become dangerous
because it is twenty years old.

A lift doesn't stop being useful
because its emergency telephone
uses GSM.

<!-- S68 -->
An industrial machine doesn't
suddenly become obsolete
because the telecom industry
has invented 5G.

<!-- S69 -->
We have a tendency to think
of technology as a sequence.

Old.

Replace.

New.

<!-- S70 -->
But real infrastructure
doesn't work like that.

Real infrastructure overlaps.

For decades.

<!-- S71 -->
And this is why
the current timetable is interesting.

Some European operators
are planning to retire 2G
around the end of this decade.

<!-- S72 -->
That sounds like a long time.

Until you remember
what we're talking about.

A smartphone from 2021
is already old by 2026 standards.

But a lift from 2011?

That's barely middle-aged.

<!-- S73 -->
An industrial machine from 2005?

It might still have
another twenty years
of useful life.

<!-- S74 -->
That's the mismatch.

Mobile networks change much faster
than the infrastructure
that uses them.

<!-- S75 -->
And perhaps the best solution isn't
some revolutionary new technology.

Maybe it's something
incredibly boring.

<!-- S76 -->
Just one question.

"Does it still work?"

Yes.

Good.

Leave it alone.

<!-- S77 -->
Technology doesn't always have to
mean destroying
the previous generation.

Sometimes the smartest thing
you can do is build the new system...

and leave a tiny door open
for the old one.

<!-- S78 -->
Because somewhere out there
is a lift.

There is probably an alarm.

There is probably
an industrial machine.

There is definitely an old car.

<!-- S79 -->
And inside all of them
there may be a little GSM modem
made fifteen years ago.

Nobody thinks about it.

Nobody talks about it.

<!-- S80 -->
It doesn't stream video.

It doesn't browse the web.

It doesn't download apps.

It has one job.

To send a tiny message
when something goes wrong.

Something like this.

"Help. I'm here."

<!-- S81 -->
And honestly...

I think that's a pretty good reason
to keep a few GSM channels alive.

<!-- S82 -->
Because the most impressive
technology isn't necessarily the one
that replaces everything
that came before.

<!-- S83 -->
Sometimes...

it's the one that lets
the old stuff keep working
until we're actually ready
to let it go.

<!-- S84 -->
2G may be ancient.

But perhaps the right question
isn't this.

"Why are we still using it?"

<!-- S85 -->
Perhaps it's this.

"What happens if we turn it off
before we're ready?"
