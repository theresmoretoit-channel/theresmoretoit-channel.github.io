<!-- S01 -->
Imagine a single teaspoon
of matter from a neutron star.

<!-- S02 -->
On Earth, it would weigh
billions of tonnes.

<!-- S03 -->
That's several times the mass
of every human being alive.
In one teaspoon.

<!-- S04 -->
Which raises two questions.
How can that much mass
fit into something so small?

<!-- S05 -->
And if you could somehow bring it here,
what would it do?

<!-- S06 -->
Start with something ordinary.
A rock.
A piece of iron.
Your hand.

<!-- S07 -->
It all feels solid.
But zoom in,
and keep zooming,
until you reach the atoms.

<!-- S08 -->
An atom has a tiny nucleus at its centre,
with electrons around it.
And in between...
almost nothing.

<!-- S09 -->
If an atom were the size of a football stadium,
the nucleus would be a pea
in the middle of the pitch.

<!-- S10 -->
And yet that pea holds
more than ninety-nine point nine per cent
of the atom's mass.

<!-- S11 -->
So ordinary matter is mostly empty space.
If you could squeeze the emptiness
out of every person on Earth,
all of humanity would fit into a sugar cube.

<!-- S12 -->
That's the first clue.
A neutron star is what happens
when matter is forced into a state
where ordinary atoms can no longer exist.

<!-- S13 -->
To see how that's possible,
we need to look inside the nucleus.

<!-- S14 -->
A nucleus is made of protons and neutrons.
And protons are positively charged.

<!-- S15 -->
Two positive charges repel each other.
Pack several protons together,
and they should push themselves apart.

<!-- S16 -->
And yet nuclei exist.
Carbon exists.
Oxygen exists.
You exist.

<!-- S17 -->
Something holds them together.
It's the strong interaction
between protons and neutrons.
At very short distances,
it overpowers the electric repulsion.

<!-- S18 -->
But its reach is tiny.
Beyond the width of a few protons,
it fades away very quickly.

<!-- S19 -->
So particles have to get extremely close
before it can grab them.
Which brings us to the squeezing.

<!-- S20 -->
A star many times heavier than the Sun
spends millions of years
fusing light elements into heavier ones.

<!-- S21 -->
It ends up with a core of iron,
heavier than the Sun
and roughly the size of the Earth.

<!-- S22 -->
Iron is a dead end.
Fusing it doesn't release energy.
So once the core gets heavy enough,
it can no longer hold itself up
against its own gravity.

<!-- S23 -->
It collapses.
In less than a second,
something the size of a planet
shrinks to the size of a city.

<!-- S24 -->
Under that pressure,
protons start capturing electrons.
Each capture turns a proton into a neutron,
and releases a ghostly particle
called a neutrino.

<!-- S25 -->
A flood of neutrinos bursts out of the core,
carrying away most of the energy,
and helping to blast the outer layers
into space in a supernova.

<!-- S26 -->
What's left behind
is no longer made of ordinary atoms.
The electrons that kept the nuclei apart
are mostly gone.

<!-- S27 -->
It's typically about twenty-five kilometres across,
made mostly of neutrons,
packed to roughly the density
of an atomic nucleus.

<!-- S28 -->
It's tempting to call it
one giant atomic nucleus.
But it isn't.

<!-- S29 -->
The crust is a lattice
of strange, neutron-heavy nuclei.
Deeper down, they merge into shapes
physicists call nuclear pasta.

<!-- S30 -->
Below that is a fluid of neutrons.
And at the very centre...
nobody knows yet.

<!-- S31 -->
What holds it up
is the pressure of this ultra-dense matter.
Partly a quantum effect,
and partly the strong interaction
pushing back at close range.

<!-- S32 -->
But beyond a certain mass,
no known pressure can stop the collapse.
The star becomes a black hole.

<!-- S33 -->
So that's how the mass fits.
Gravity crushes matter into a state
where ordinary atoms can't exist,
and packs it as tightly as a nucleus.

<!-- S34 -->
Now for the second question.
Suppose you could scoop out a teaspoon
and bring it to Earth.

<!-- S35 -->
There's a catch.
The strong interaction
isn't what holds this matter together.
On their own, neutrons don't stick.
It's the gravity of the whole star
that keeps it squeezed.

<!-- S36 -->
Take it out of the star,
and nothing would hold it in.
Its own pressure would take over,
and it would decompress violently.

<!-- S37 -->
As the matter decompressed,
it would have to turn into ordinary matter.
Even the energy difference
between a neutron and a hydrogen atom
adds up to more than the asteroid
that wiped out the dinosaurs.

<!-- S38 -->
And this isn't just a thought experiment.
In 2017, we watched something like it happen.

<!-- S39 -->
Two neutron stars,
about a hundred and thirty million light-years away,
spiralled into each other and collided.

<!-- S40 -->
Gravitational-wave detectors
felt the collision.
Then telescopes all over the world
turned to look.

<!-- S41 -->
They saw debris from the neutron stars
expanding into space,
glowing as it went.

<!-- S42 -->
As that neutron-rich matter decompressed,
it built heavy atoms.
Astronomers identified what is most likely strontium,
and models suggest gold and platinum too.

<!-- S43 -->
Some of the gold on Earth
may have been made in collisions like this.
Neutron-star matter,
released from the pressure.

<!-- S44 -->
But there's an even stranger question
hiding in that teaspoon.

<!-- S45 -->
Not why it's so dense.
Why it weighs anything at all.

<!-- S46 -->
Every neutron and every proton
is made of three smaller particles,
called quarks.

<!-- S47 -->
So you'd expect a proton
to weigh as much as its three quarks.
Let's add them up.

<!-- S48 -->
About one per cent.
The quarks account for
roughly one per cent of the proton.

<!-- S49 -->
The other ninety-nine per cent
comes from energy.

<!-- S50 -->
The energy of quarks moving
at nearly the speed of light,
and of the gluon fields
binding them together.

<!-- S51 -->
Einstein showed that energy has mass.
E equals m c squared.
Inside a proton,
that's where almost all the mass comes from.

<!-- S52 -->
You may have heard
that the Higgs field gives particles their mass.
It does give quarks theirs.
But the quarks' own mass
is only about one per cent of the proton.

<!-- S53 -->
So the matter in your body
is mostly massive
because of the energy of the fields
that bind it together.

<!-- S54 -->
And the same goes for the teaspoon.
Billions of tonnes,
and almost all of it
is the energy of the strong interaction.

<!-- S55 -->
Using supercomputers,
physicists can calculate the proton's mass
from quantum chromodynamics,
and the result agrees with experiment.

<!-- S56 -->
But that calculation starts
from a few numbers
the theory itself doesn't predict.

<!-- S57 -->
How strong the strong interaction is.
How heavy the quarks are.
Those have to be measured,
and then put in by hand.

<!-- S58 -->
The Standard Model of particle physics
contains around twenty such numbers.
If the universe had a settings menu,
these would be the sliders.

<!-- S59 -->
Take the speed of light.
Exactly 299,792,458 metres per second.
That number looks fundamental.

<!-- S60 -->
But its exact value
depends on the units we choose.
Since 1983, the metre has been defined
using the speed of light.

<!-- S61 -->
Measure in feet,
or in some alien unit,
and the number changes completely.
The speed itself matters enormously.
The number is a fact about our ruler.

<!-- S62 -->
But some numbers have no units at all.
And those are different.

<!-- S63 -->
The most famous one
is the fine-structure constant.
It sets how strongly electric charges
interact with light.

<!-- S64 -->
It helps set how big atoms are,
all that empty space from the beginning,
and the strength of chemical bonds.

<!-- S65 -->
Its value is about 1/137.
Metres, feet or alien units,
everyone gets the same number.

<!-- S66 -->
We can measure it
with extraordinary precision.
But we still don't know
why its value is what it is.

<!-- S67 -->
Some calculations suggest
that changing certain constants
could leave a universe with no stable stars,
or no complex chemistry.

<!-- S68 -->
Is that luck?
Is there a deeper theory that fixes them?
Or are we one universe among many?
Nobody knows.

<!-- S69 -->
Think about that teaspoon again.
Billions of tonnes of crushed matter.

<!-- S70 -->
To explain it,
we went from atoms to nuclei,
from nuclei to neutrons,
from neutrons to quarks and fields.

<!-- S71 -->
And at the bottom of all those layers,
we found a handful of numbers
our best theories simply take as given.

<!-- S72 -->
Even if a deeper theory explains them one day,
we'll have a new question.
Why that theory?

<!-- S73 -->
Maybe the universe has no settings menu.
Maybe it does.
Or maybe...
we just haven't found it yet.

<!-- S74 -->
So here's a question for you.
If physicists one day derive 1/137
from something deeper,
would that answer the question,
or just move it one level down?
Let me know in the comments.
