<!-- S01 -->
Imagine your computer
starts behaving strangely.
A program freezes.
Something crashes.
What do you do?
You open the logs.

<!-- S02 -->
You check what happened before the crash.
You look for the error message.
And if you're lucky,
you find the exact line responsible.

<!-- S03 -->
Now imagine your own body
doing the same thing.
You wake up with a headache.
You feel exhausted.
Your stomach hurts.

<!-- S04 -->
Something is clearly wrong,
but the only message you get is this.
Error. Something went wrong.
Root cause: unknown.

<!-- S05 -->
And the really strange part?
It's your body.
Surely, you're the system administrator.
Your brain adjusts your heartbeat,
regulates your temperature,
and talks to your immune system.

<!-- S06 -->
Yet you can't open a terminal and ask
what happened to me last night?
So why can't you read your own logs?

<!-- S07 -->
In 1995, the British Medical Journal
described a builder
who had jumped down onto a nail.
Fifteen centimetres long.
It went straight through his boot.

<!-- S08 -->
The smallest movement of that nail hurt
so much that doctors gave him fentanyl
and a sedative before pulling it out.

<!-- S09 -->
Then they took off the boot.
The nail had passed between his toes.
His foot was completely unharmed.

<!-- S10 -->
Ten years later, in Colorado,
a construction worker's nail gun backfired.
He felt a heavy blow,
and then a toothache.

<!-- S11 -->
For six days, he took painkillers
and iced his face.
Then he went to a dentist,
and an X-ray showed why.

<!-- S12 -->
A ten-centimetre nail,
through the roof of his mouth
and into his brain.
Surgeons took it out,
and he was expected to recover fully.

<!-- S13 -->
One man had no injury at all,
and pain that needed a strong opioid.
The other had a nail in his brain,
and a toothache.

<!-- S14 -->
So your body's error messages
aren't just vague.
They can be wrong in both directions.
But that isn't because
nothing is being recorded.

<!-- S15 -->
One nerve, the vagus,
connects your brain to your heart,
your lungs and your gut.
And by the usual estimate,
around eighty percent of its fibres
don't carry orders down.
They carry reports up.

<!-- S16 -->
Sensing your own insides even has a name.
Interoception.
Some of it reaches you.
You feel hungry.
You notice your heart racing.
Most of it never does.

<!-- S17 -->
You can test this right now.
Without touching your wrist or your neck,
try to count your heartbeats.

<!-- S18 -->
In the lab version of this test,
most people count too few.
And that's an organ
thumping away inside your chest.

<!-- S19 -->
Part of the reason may be capacity.
Every day, your body replaces
about three hundred and thirty billion cells,
most of them blood cells.

<!-- S20 -->
And one recent estimate
puts human thought and action
at about ten bits per second.
By the same estimate,
your senses take in around a billion.

<!-- S21 -->
So even with every signal on a screen,
you couldn't keep up.
The terminal is too narrow.
But capacity alone doesn't explain
which few lines get through.

<!-- S22 -->
People who keep huge computer systems
running have a rule about this.
It's in Google's book
on site reliability, in five words.
Every page should be actionable.

<!-- S23 -->
A page is an alert
that wakes up an engineer.
If nobody can do anything about it,
it shouldn't wake anybody up.

<!-- S24 -->
Now look at what your body pages you for.
Hunger. Eat.
Thirst. Drink.
A full bladder. Find a bathroom.
A hand on a hot stove. Move.

<!-- S25 -->
In 1979, the pain researcher Patrick Wall
argued that pain has more in common
with hunger and thirst
than with seeing or hearing.
A demand for action, not a measurement.

<!-- S26 -->
Now look at what stays silent.
High blood pressure usually has no symptoms.
About a third of adults aged thirty
to seventy-nine have it,
and nearly half of them don't know.

<!-- S27 -->
In one large American study,
more than forty-five percent
of first heart attacks
were only discovered later, on an ECG.
Whatever those people felt at the time,
nobody took it for a heart attack.

<!-- S28 -->
For nearly all of human history,
nobody could do anything
about their blood pressure.
No action, so perhaps, no alert.
That's a hypothesis.
Nobody can rerun evolution to check it.

<!-- S29 -->
There's a second rule,
and the physician Randolph Nesse
named it after a smoke detector.
A false alarm costs you some burnt toast.
A missed one costs you the house.

<!-- S30 -->
So defences like pain, fever and vomiting
may be tuned to go off far too often.
Seen that way, the builder's body
wasn't malfunctioning.
A nail through your boot
is exactly when you want
the alarm first and the facts later.

<!-- S31 -->
And sometimes,
the alarm watches the wrong number.
When you hold your breath,
that desperate urge comes mainly
from carbon dioxide building up,
not from oxygen running out.

<!-- S32 -->
So if your oxygen falls
while carbon dioxide keeps leaving,
that alarm barely goes off.
Pilots are trained for this,
because some people feel euphoric,
and may not know they're in trouble.

<!-- S33 -->
In 2005, Helios Flight 522
climbed with its cabin pressure falling.
A warning horn sounded,
and the crew seem to have taken it
for a different warning.

<!-- S34 -->
A ground engineer on the radio even asked
about the pressurisation panel.
The captain replied by asking
where the cooling breakers were.
Then the crew stopped answering.
A hundred and twenty-one people died.

<!-- S35 -->
Now imagine that alarm hardly working.
Around a thousand people worldwide
have a condition
once called Ondine's curse.

<!-- S36 -->
Their automatic breathing barely responds
to changes in oxygen and carbon dioxide.
Some need a ventilator
every time they fall asleep.
Some need one all the time.

<!-- S37 -->
And even when an alert does fire,
it can carry the wrong address.
A heart attack can show up as pain
in your arm, your back, or your jaw.
Something cold on the roof of your mouth
hurts in your forehead.

<!-- S38 -->
The best-known explanation
is a shared line.
Nerves from your organs and your skin
meet on the same neurons in your spinal cord.
Two machines behind one address.

<!-- S39 -->
The brain sees a signal on that line,
and blames the skin,
because that's where signals
usually come from.

<!-- S40 -->
None of this means you should ignore it.
Pain can be bad at saying where and why.
New or unexplained chest pain
is still a reason to get help, now.

<!-- S41 -->
Then there's the oddest gap of all.
Brain tissue itself
has no pain receptors.
Surgeons can operate on awake patients
once the scalp and the membranes are numb.

<!-- S42 -->
That headache you woke up with
isn't your brain hurting.
It comes mostly from the tissues around it.
The organ reading the alerts
can't raise that one about itself.

<!-- S43 -->
So is there any log at all?
There is.
There are several...
You've just never been shown them.

<!-- S44 -->
Your blood keeps a partial record
of the viruses you've met.
In 2015, researchers built a test
that checks a drop of blood
for antibodies against
two hundred and six species of virus.
The average person showed about ten.

<!-- S45 -->
Your hair grows about a centimetre a month,
and it traps traces of a stress hormone,
cortisol, as it grows.
A few centimetres
can hint at the last few months.

<!-- S46 -->
Your DNA keeps a tally too.
Smoking a pack a day for a year
leaves an estimated hundred and fifty extra
mutations in each lung cell.

<!-- S47 -->
And one log you can check yourself.
A serious illness
can briefly interrupt your nail growth,
and leave a groove across the nail.
It takes about six months to grow out.

<!-- S48 -->
So the logs exist.
They just were never written for you.
An antibody is there for your immune system.
A hormone is there for the cells it reaches.
The reader was never the part of you
that's watching this video.

<!-- S49 -->
That's why we had to build
the debugger on the outside.
An ECG records your heart.
An EEG records your brain.
Blood tests, scanners, smartwatches.

<!-- S50 -->
And each one sees only a part.
A number can be perfectly accurate
and still be misleading.
A hundred beats a minute
is perfectly normal after climbing stairs.
Sitting quietly,
it might mean something else.

<!-- S51 -->
So let's go back to where we started.
Surely, you're the system administrator.
You aren't.

<!-- S51b -->
You never had root access.

<!-- S52 -->
What you call you
is closer to one unprivileged process,
with a tiny dashboard
built to make you act,
not to tell you what's going on.

<!-- S53 -->
You can measure your body.
You can study your own brain.
But you have to do it with instruments,
the way you'd study a stranger.

<!-- S54 -->
And perhaps the strangest thing
about being human
is that the machine
trying to understand itself
is also the machine
doing the understanding.

If you could see all of it,
every marker, every day, in real time,
would you actually want to?
