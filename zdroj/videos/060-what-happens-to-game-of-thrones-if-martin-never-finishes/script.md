<!-- S01 -->
In August 2017, at a steam fair in Dorset,
a six-and-a-half tonne steamroller
drove over a computer hard drive.

<!-- S02 -->
Then they put what was left of it
through a steam-powered stone crusher.

<!-- S03 -->
The drive belonged to Terry Pratchett.
It held up to ten unfinished novels.

<!-- S04 -->
Nobody will ever read them.

<!-- S05 -->
This wasn't vandalism.
It was exactly what Pratchett wanted.
He had left instructions to do it.

<!-- S06 -->
But look at who actually did it.

Pratchett was already dead.
Somebody else started the engine.

<!-- S07 -->
George R. R. Martin has been writing
The Winds of Winter for fifteen years.

He has said that if he dies first,
the book simply won't be finished.

<!-- S08 -->
That sounds like an instruction.

But an instruction needs somebody
to carry it out.

<!-- S09 -->
And that somebody won't be him.

<!-- S10 -->
Martin writes on a DOS machine
running WordStar 4.0.

He keeps two computers.
One for email. One that only writes.

<!-- S11 -->
He has said there are around
eleven hundred pages of the book on it.

That part isn't a rumour.
He has repeated it publicly for years.

<!-- S12 -->
What nobody outside that room knows
is everything else.

Older drafts.
Chapters with two different endings.
Scenes he wrote and then abandoned.

<!-- S13 -->
Notes about where a character ends up,
three hundred pages later.

<!-- S14 -->
So the first question isn't
who finishes the book.

<!-- S15 -->
Who decides what happens to the files?

<!-- S16 -->
That question has been answered before.

Franz Kafka left instructions
with his friend Max Brod.

Burn everything. Unread.

<!-- S17 -->
Brod didn't.

<!-- S18 -->
The Trial exists because of that.
So does The Castle.

Every novel Kafka wrote
is a disobeyed instruction.

<!-- S19 -->
Brod had told him to his face
that he would refuse.

Kafka left him the papers anyway.

<!-- S20 -->
Nobody asks the manuscript.
They ask whoever is holding it.

<!-- S20b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S21 -->
Martin has already answered
part of this question.

He answered it in 1992.

<!-- S22 -->
That year a truck left Santa Fe
carrying his papers to Texas.

Some of the earliest pages
of A Game of Thrones were in it.

<!-- S23 -->
Still on dot matrix paper,
with the perforated edges attached.

<!-- S24 -->
Today that archive
runs to over two hundred boxes.

Manuscripts. Letters. Notes.
Board games. Replica swords.

<!-- S25 -->
Most of it is open
to any researcher who asks.

His private correspondence is closed.

<!-- S26 -->
And the rule for the closed part
is written down.

To see it, you have to present
a letter of permission.

<!-- S27 -->
A letter from Martin.

<!-- S28 -->
It is a rule that will one day
be impossible to satisfy.

<!-- S29 -->
The door doesn't lock when he dies.

<!-- S30 -->
It just stops having anyone behind it.

<!-- S31 -->
There is a third answer,
and it has been used before.

Publish the mess.

<!-- S32 -->
Christopher Tolkien spent decades
editing his father's unfinished papers.

Not into a finished novel.
Into twelve volumes of drafts.

<!-- S33 -->
Versions that contradict each other.
Notes in the margin. Dead ends.

<!-- S34 -->
Vladimir Nabokov asked for his last
manuscript to be destroyed.

His son published it instead,
as photographs of the index cards.

<!-- S35 -->
You can see the handwriting.
You can see the crossings-out.

Nobody there pretended to be the author.
They published what was on the page.

<!-- S36 -->
Now picture that in Texas.

A historian opens a box
and finds something labelled
The Winds of Winter, draft seven.

<!-- S37 -->
A chapter with three different endings.

A note in the margin saying:
this doesn't work.

<!-- S38 -->
Another one saying: try again.

And a later version
that contradicts them both.

<!-- S39 -->
For a historian, that's a treasure.

For anyone who just wants to know
how the story ends,
it's torture.

<!-- S40 -->
And then there is the fourth answer.

Finish it.

<!-- S41 -->
When Martin is asked about this,
he reaches for one comparison.

The Mystery of Edwin Drood.

<!-- S42 -->
Charles Dickens died in 1870,
halfway through a murder mystery.

He had told his biographer how it ends.

He had not written it down.

<!-- S43 -->
Three years later,
a printer in Vermont finished it.

He said Dickens had asked him to.

<!-- S44 -->
At a seance.

<!-- S45 -->
A note appeared,
signed with Dickens's name.

The printer said he wrote the rest
of the book word for word,
exactly as it came to him.

<!-- S46 -->
He included a preface
that Dickens had supposedly dictated.

<!-- S47 -->
Decades later, Arthur Conan Doyle
went through it hunting for the fake.

He checked the spelling.
He checked it for Americanisms.

<!-- S48 -->
He found almost none.

<!-- S49 -->
The manner was right, he decided.
The trick of thought was right.

It just sounded, he said,
like Dickens gone flat.

<!-- S50 -->
But look at the shape of it.

A reconstruction that passes
every test anyone knows how to run.

And underneath it,
a story nobody would call Dickens.

<!-- S51 -->
That was 1873.

<!-- S52 -->
Now imagine the same thing
with much better tools.

Software could read every draft
on that DOS machine.

Line them up. Date them.
Find the contradictions.

<!-- S53 -->
And work out which ending
he was moving towards.

<!-- S54 -->
It wouldn't produce one book.

<!-- S55 -->
Change how you read one vague note
and you get a different ending.

Trust the later drafts more
and you get another.

<!-- S56 -->
You could generate hundreds of them.
Thousands.

<!-- S57 -->
So which one is the real one?

Version 37? Version 814?

<!-- S58 -->
And there would be pressure
to pick one.

Publishers would want it.
Fans would want it.

The people who own the rights
would have every reason to want it.

<!-- S59 -->
And somewhere in the world
there would be a writer saying...

I can finish this.

<!-- S60 -->
There's one more detail
that makes all of this worse.

We already know part of the ending.

<!-- S61 -->
In 2013, Martin sat down
with the television producers
and told them how it ends.

<!-- S62 -->
Not the whole thing.

A handful of destinations.

What happens to Hodor.
What happens to Shireen.
That Bran ends up on the throne.

<!-- S63 -->
Think of the story as a map.

He gave them some of the places
it has to arrive at.

He didn't give them the road.

<!-- S64 -->
The series had to build its own.

It cut characters.
It merged storylines.

<!-- S65 -->
Which means a reconstruction
could hit every single destination
and still be a completely different book.

<!-- S66 -->
Because there is one thing
no amount of material can replace.

<!-- S67 -->
A living author can throw away
three hundred pages.

He can decide a character survives.
He can rewrite the whole ending.

<!-- S68 -->
He can look at your reconstruction
and say...

No.
That isn't what I meant.

<!-- S69 -->
That is the real difference.

Not that the book is unfinished.

<!-- S70 -->
That the author can still say no.

<!-- S71 -->
Once he's gone, the manuscript
stops being instructions.

<!-- S72 -->
It becomes evidence.

<!-- S73 -->
Evidence of what he was trying to do.

Not a description of what he'd have done.

<!-- S74 -->
There is one last answer,
and it's the quietest of all.

The estate does exactly what he asked.

Nothing gets finished.
Nothing gets published.

<!-- S75 -->
The files are kept, and sealed.

It's his work.
He gets to say.

<!-- S76 -->
But look at it once more.

Even that is somebody else's decision.

<!-- S77 -->
Pratchett's estate said yes,
and started the steamroller.

<!-- S78 -->
Brod said no,
and that is why we have Kafka.

<!-- S79 -->
Christopher Tolkien published
the contradictions too.

<!-- S80 -->
The printer in Vermont
didn't ask anybody.

<!-- S81 -->
Martin has said what he wants.

<!-- S82 -->
But saying what you want
is not the same as deciding.

The files might not survive him.

The archive will.
So will the theories.

<!-- S83 -->
He can still have the last word.
He just has to finish it himself.

<!-- S84 -->
Otherwise it belongs to whoever
opens the archive

<!-- S85 -->
and asks for a letter
that nobody can write.
