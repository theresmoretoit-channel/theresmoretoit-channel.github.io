# Who Runs the Earth?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine that tomorrow,
something unexpected happens.

One country invades another.

And we say:

"Enough.
This is against the rules.
Stop."

<!-- S02 -->
But...

who gets to order them to stop?

Who is actually
above the countries?

<!-- S03 -->
The answer is surprisingly simple.

Nobody.

<!-- S04 -->
There is the United Nations.

There are international courts,
treaties,
and countless
international organisations.

<!-- S05 -->
But there is no world government
with its own police and military
that can simply say:

"This country has broken the rules.
And now we are going to stop it."

<!-- S06 -->
And that is why international politics
is, in a sense, anarchic.

Not because the world
is necessarily chaotic.

<!-- S07 -->
But because there is no higher authority
above sovereign states,
with a monopoly on the use of force.

<!-- S08 -->
And that may be one of the biggest
problems in the world today.

<!-- S09 -->
Imagine an ordinary person.

They can't simply say:

"I don't like this law,
so I won't follow it."

<!-- S10 -->
If they break the law,
there is a police force.

If they have a dispute,
there is a court.

<!-- S11 -->
And if someone attacks another person,
the state can use force to stop them.

<!-- S12 -->
Why?

Because the state has a monopoly
on the legitimate use of force.

<!-- S13 -->
We don't have to agree with every law.

But we all know that there is someone
who ultimately has the authority
to enforce it.

<!-- S14 -->
And that is what makes it possible
for millions of people to live together
under one system.

<!-- S15 -->
Now let's ask the same question
at the international level.

Imagine a small country
attacks its neighbour.

Who comes and arrests
its prime minister?

Nobody.

<!-- S16 -->
China invades another country.

Who sends the police?

Nobody.

<!-- S17 -->
The United States
violates an international treaty.

Who can order the United States:

"Stop. Fix this immediately."

<!-- S18 -->
Again, there is no world police force
with authority over the United States.

Of course, there are
international institutions.

<!-- S19 -->
There are courts.

There is the United Nations.

There are sanctions.

There are diplomatic
and military coalitions.

<!-- S20 -->
But ultimately, these mechanisms
depend on the countries themselves.

And that creates a strange situation.

<!-- S21 -->
Countries create the rules,
but countries are also
the main enforcers of those rules.

<!-- S22 -->
At first, it sounds simple.

Let's create a world government.

Every country gives it
some of its powers.

<!-- S23 -->
We create a world parliament.

A world court.

A world police force.

And a world military.

<!-- S24 -->
When one country causes trouble,
the world government intervenes.

Problem solved.

Except...

<!-- S25 -->
which country would agree to that?

Imagine the world government
tells the United States:

"Your military
cannot cross this border."

<!-- S26 -->
And the United States says:

"No."

<!-- S27 -->
What happens next?

If the world government
has no force of its own,
the order is just a suggestion.

<!-- S28 -->
If it does have force,
then it has to be capable
of using that force
even against the most powerful
countries on Earth.

<!-- S29 -->
And that is the real problem.

For a world government to work,
it would actually have to be
above the countries.

<!-- S30 -->
And here we get to
an interesting historical detour.

After the collapse of the Soviet Union,
the world entered a period
when there was one clearly
dominant power.

<!-- S31 -->
The United States.

It had an enormous economy.

An enormous military.

Military bases around the world.

Powerful allies.

<!-- S32 -->
And the ability to project force
almost anywhere on the planet.

Some people therefore started talking
about an American unipolar world.

<!-- S33 -->
But there is an important difference.

The United States
was not a world government.

It was a hegemon.

<!-- S34 -->
It had an extraordinary ability
to influence other countries.

But it did not have
universal legal authority over them.

<!-- S35 -->
And more importantly:

Who had the authority
to control the United States?

Nobody above it.

<!-- S36 -->
And that reveals a fundamental problem
with every hegemon.

If we create one power
strong enough to stop everyone else...

who stops that power?

<!-- S37 -->
Let's imagine that we really
wanted to build one.

We could create something like
a Federation of Earth.

<!-- S38 -->
Countries would still exist.

Brazil would still be Brazil.

France would still be France.

Japan would still be Japan.

<!-- S39 -->
But some powers would be transferred
to a higher level.

For example:
wars between countries,
weapons of mass destruction,
international aggression,
certain global environmental problems,
and perhaps the protection
of global commons.

<!-- S40 -->
The Federation would have its own courts.

Its own police.

And most importantly:

its own armed forces.

<!-- S41 -->
Not an army that countries lend to it
when they feel like it.

An actual military
belonging to the Federation of Earth.

<!-- S42 -->
Imagine that one country
invades its neighbour.

The world government declares:

"This is aggression."

<!-- S43 -->
And instead of:

"We are considering
another round of sanctions..."

the response is:

"Withdraw your forces
within forty-eight hours."

<!-- S44 -->
And if they don't?

World forces begin
pushing the aggressor back.

No months of negotiations.

No waiting to see
which countries will join.

<!-- S45 -->
No:

"We couldn't reach an agreement
in the Security Council."

There would simply be
a higher authority.

<!-- S46 -->
Just as a state
can tell an individual:

"You cannot do this."

And if they don't,
the police intervene.

<!-- S47 -->
Because a world government
would have enormous power.

And that immediately raises
another question:

What happens if it goes wrong?

<!-- S48 -->
Today, one government
can become authoritarian.

People can leave.

They can overthrow it.

Other countries
can provide a counterbalance.

There can be democratic allies.

<!-- S49 -->
But if there is only
one world government...

where do you escape from it?

<!-- S49b -->
Do we all move to Mars?

<!-- S50 -->
So a world government
would need something
that may be even more important
than its military.

Extremely strong checks and balances.

<!-- S51 -->
The ideal system might therefore
look more like a federation.

The global level would have
only clearly defined powers.

Countries would keep most
of their existing responsibilities.

<!-- S52 -->
Brazil would still decide
how its schools work.

France would still decide
how its roads are built.

Japan would still run
its healthcare system.

<!-- S53 -->
But if one country wanted to start
a war against its neighbour,
that would no longer be
entirely its own business.

<!-- S54 -->
Because a war between two countries
can threaten the entire planet.

And that could be the boundary
between national and global authority.

<!-- S55 -->
And this is, I think,
the most interesting part.

Maybe a world government will not appear
because one day everyone decides:

"A world government would be nice."

<!-- S56 -->
History suggests something different.

Major political changes often happen
because a problem appears
that is larger than the political units
dealing with it.

<!-- S57 -->
Imagine that one day
we detect something strange.

There is no doubt.

It is an artificial signal.

And it is coming
from another star system.

<!-- S58 -->
It isn't noise.

It isn't a natural phenomenon.

Someone is out there.

<!-- S59 -->
And they send us a message.

What do we do?

Who answers?

<!-- S60 -->
The President of the United States?

The President of China?

The Secretary-General
of the United Nations?

The European Union?

India?

Or everyone at once?

<!-- S61 -->
They probably won't ask
who runs one country.

They might ask:

"Who represents planet Earth?"

<!-- S62 -->
And suddenly, we have a problem.

We don't have anyone.

<!-- S63 -->
Maybe first contact wouldn't create
a world government overnight.

But it could create
enormous pressure for coordination.

<!-- S64 -->
We would need
a common diplomatic protocol.

Joint scientific teams.

A common defence strategy.

<!-- S65 -->
And above all,
someone who could say:

"This is the official position
of humanity."

<!-- S66 -->
Suddenly, the question:

"Why should we have
some kind of world authority?"

would have a very practical answer.

Because someone has to speak for Earth.

<!-- S67 -->
We don't have to wait
for an extraterrestrial civilisation.

The same pressure could come
from another global threat.

<!-- S68 -->
An asteroid on a collision course
with Earth.

A global pandemic.

A technological catastrophe.

Or something we cannot
even predict today.

<!-- S69 -->
The more interconnected
the world becomes,
the more problems there will be
that no individual country
can solve on its own.

<!-- S70 -->
And maybe one day we will realise
that we have two choices.

Either we have two hundred countries
trying to negotiate
every time something happens.

<!-- S71 -->
Or we create
a higher level of government
for problems that affect everyone.

<!-- S72 -->
Thousands of years ago,
humans mostly lived
in small communities.

Then came cities.

Then kingdoms.

Then nation-states.

Then international organisations.

<!-- S73 -->
And today, we are dealing with problems
that are genuinely planetary.

Maybe the next step isn't:

"Get rid of countries."

<!-- S74 -->
Maybe it is:

"Add another layer above them."

<!-- S75 -->
Just as towns didn't disappear
when countries emerged.

There would simply be
another level above them.

<!-- S76 -->
That is far from certain.

A world government could potentially
end wars between countries.

It could be much more effective
at dealing with global problems.

<!-- S77 -->
But it would also create
the most powerful political institution
in human history.

<!-- S78 -->
And if we designed it badly...

we could create something much worse
than the world we have today.

<!-- S79 -->
So perhaps the real question isn't:

"Do we need a world government?"

It is:

<!-- S80 -->
"Can we ever build a world government
that is powerful enough
to stop countries,
but weak enough
that it cannot enslave humanity?"

<!-- S81 -->
And that is a hell of a problem.

<!-- S82 -->
Today we live in a strange situation.

Inside countries,
we have laws, courts, and police.

But between countries,
there is still something
resembling anarchy.

<!-- S83 -->
Countries can negotiate.

They can sign treaties.

They can create organisations.

They can impose sanctions.

<!-- S84 -->
But there is still nobody above them
with the ultimate authority to say:

"Enough."

<!-- S85 -->
Maybe it will stay that way
for hundreds of years.

Or maybe something will happen
that changes it within a single decade.

<!-- S86 -->
Maybe a catastrophe.

Maybe a technological breakthrough.

Maybe a common threat.

Or maybe...

<!-- S87 -->
one day, someone will answer
our message.

<!-- S88 -->
And humanity will realise
that to the rest of the universe,
we are not the United States,
or China,
or Europe,
or any of the countries
we spend our lives arguing about.

<!-- S89 -->
We are simply...

Earth.

<!-- S90 -->
And for the first time,
we may have to answer the question:

"Who speaks for humanity?"
