<!-- S01 -->
In the early 2000s, mobile operators
were selling data at a price
that sounds absurd today.

<!-- S02 -->
Not mobile internet.
Text messages.

<!-- S03 -->
A single text message carried
at most 140 bytes.

<!-- S04 -->
At ten cents a message,
one megabyte of texts
would cost you more than seven hundred dollars.

<!-- S05 -->
And people paid it.
Billions of times a day.

<!-- S06 -->
So how did a tiny side feature
of the mobile network
become a gold mine?

<!-- S07 -->
And why was every message
limited to exactly 160 characters?

<!-- S08 -->
Let's start with that number,
because it has two parents.

<!-- S09 -->
In 1985, a German engineer
called Friedhelm Hillebrand
sat down at a typewriter.

<!-- S10 -->
He typed out ordinary sentences
and questions,
and counted the characters.

<!-- S11 -->
Almost all of them
fit into 160.

<!-- S12 -->
He checked postcards and telex messages too.
Most of those were shorter than 160 as well.

<!-- S13 -->
So the first parent is human.
160 characters was enough
to say most things people actually say.

<!-- S14 -->
The second parent is the network.

<!-- S15 -->
Engineers were designing GSM,
the new European standard
for digital mobile phones.

<!-- S16 -->
And a phone doesn't only carry your voice.
It constantly exchanges small messages
with the network.

<!-- S17 -->
I'm here.
I've moved to another cell.
A call is coming in.

<!-- S18 -->
That's called signalling.
And most of the time,
those channels sit almost empty.

<!-- S19 -->
So the idea was modest.
What if short text messages
could ride along in that spare capacity?

<!-- S20 -->
But a signalling message is small.
The room left for the text
was 140 bytes.

<!-- S21 -->
140 bytes is 1120 bits.

<!-- S22 -->
Simple text doesn't need
eight bits per character.
GSM used seven.

<!-- S23 -->
And 1120 divided by seven
is exactly 160.

<!-- S24 -->
It wasn't a coincidence.
The first design had room
for only about 146 characters.
So the engineers stretched it
until 160 fitted.

<!-- S25 -->
There's a catch, though.
Seven bits only give you
a hundred and twenty-eight characters.

<!-- S26 -->
That covers English,
and a handful of accented letters.

<!-- S27 -->
But not the Czech ř.
Not the Polish ł,
the Hungarian ő,
or the Cyrillic alphabet.

<!-- S28 -->
Use one of those,
and the phone switches to an encoding
with sixteen bits per character.

<!-- S29 -->
And the same 140 bytes
now hold only 70 characters.

<!-- S30 -->
One letter could cut your message
by more than half.

<!-- S31 -->
Which is why, for years,
millions of people wrote their texts
without accents at all.

<!-- S32 -->
The service itself was called
SMS.
Short Message Service.

<!-- S33 -->
And nobody expected it
to become the way people talk.

<!-- S34 -->
It was a small extra
built into the network.
Something like a pager:
a short note for someone on the move.

<!-- S35 -->
On the 3rd of December 1992,
the first text message was sent
across a British network.

<!-- S36 -->
It said:
"Merry Christmas."

<!-- S37 -->
It was typed on a computer,
not a phone.

<!-- S38 -->
The phone that received it
could display the message.
But it had no way to send one back.

<!-- S39 -->
Getting a message to someone
worked like a post office.

<!-- S40 -->
Your phone handed the text
to a server in the network,
the SMS Centre.

<!-- S41 -->
If the recipient's phone was switched off,
the message waited.

<!-- S42 -->
Out of coverage?
It waited.

<!-- S43 -->
And when the phone came back,
the network tried again.

<!-- S44 -->
Engineers call it store and forward.
No internet,
no account,
no app.
Just a mobile signal.

<!-- S45 -->
Then phones learned to send texts,
and people discovered something
the designers hadn't really planned for.

<!-- S46 -->
A phone call needs
both people at the same time.

<!-- S47 -->
A text doesn't need that.

<!-- S48 -->
You could send
"Where are you?"
and carry on with your day.

<!-- S49 -->
The other person could answer
in a minute,
or in an hour.

<!-- S50 -->
And on a keypad
with three or four letters on each key,
people got astonishingly fast.

<!-- S51 -->
The phone stopped being
just a telephone.
It became a personal messaging device.

<!-- S52 -->
And that's where the gold mine opened.

<!-- S53 -->
Think about what a text message
costs the network.

<!-- S54 -->
The towers were already there.
The signalling channels were already there.

<!-- S55 -->
And each message was
at most a hundred and forty bytes.

<!-- S56 -->
But customers didn't pay for bytes.
They paid per message.

<!-- S57 -->
Or for a bundle
of a few hundred messages a month.

<!-- S58 -->
Prices varied a lot
from country to country.

<!-- S59 -->
In much of Europe,
you paid for every single text.

<!-- S60 -->
In the United States, texting caught on later,
and you often paid to receive a text, too.

<!-- S61 -->
But the basic arithmetic
was the same almost everywhere.

<!-- S62 -->
A tiny amount of data,
sold as a separate service.

<!-- S63 -->
And sold billions of times a day.

<!-- S64 -->
For operators, it became
one of the most profitable things
they had ever sold.

<!-- S65 -->
And then it got a little strange.

<!-- S66 -->
Some operators offered free texting
from their own websites.
Type a number, type a message, send.

<!-- S67 -->
Programmers noticed.
If a website can send a text,
a program can use that website.

<!-- S68 -->
Soon there were apps
that sent texts from your computer,
through the operators' own gateways.

<!-- S69 -->
The service people paid for
on their phones
was sometimes free one window away.

<!-- S70 -->
But 160 characters had another limit.

<!-- S71 -->
What if you wanted to send a picture?

<!-- S72 -->
Enter MMS.
Multimedia Messaging Service.

<!-- S73 -->
Pictures, sound,
short videos,
longer text.

<!-- S74 -->
And here's the clever part.
An MMS didn't travel like a text.

<!-- S75 -->
First, your phone received
a small hidden message.
Something like:
there's a picture waiting for you.

<!-- S76 -->
Then the phone went online
and downloaded it.

<!-- S77 -->
Which meant MMS needed
mobile data.

<!-- S78 -->
It needed the right settings
from your operator.

<!-- S79 -->
And it had size limits
that squeezed photos
into a blurry little rectangle.

<!-- S80 -->
It was often expensive.
And sometimes,
it simply didn't arrive.

<!-- S81 -->
MMS was the awkward teenager
between the text message
and everything that came next.

<!-- S82 -->
And what came next
was the internet on your phone.

<!-- S83 -->
Once phones had a reliable data connection,
a message didn't need the operator's
messaging service at all.

<!-- S84 -->
It could travel as ordinary data.

<!-- S85 -->
Apps like WhatsApp
and Facebook Messenger
sent texts, pictures, voice messages
and videos the same way.

<!-- S86 -->
The economics of messaging flipped.

<!-- S87 -->
Instead of one message,
one charge,

<!-- S88 -->
thousands of messages
became a rounding error
inside your data plan.

<!-- S89 -->
The gold mine didn't run dry.
It was simply bypassed.

<!-- S90 -->
But SMS didn't disappear.

<!-- S91 -->
Because it still has one trick
that most internet messengers don't.

<!-- S92 -->
Mobile signal: yes.
Wi-Fi: no.
Mobile data: no.

<!-- S93 -->
Even then, SMS still works.

<!-- S94 -->
That's why it carries
the things that can't wait
for a good connection.

<!-- S95 -->
Login codes.
Bank alerts.
Delivery updates.

<!-- S96 -->
And the occasional
"Call me."

<!-- S97 -->
The mobile industry did try
to give the old system a modern successor.

<!-- S98 -->
It's called RCS.
Rich Communication Services.

<!-- S99 -->
Typing indicators.
Read receipts.
Group chats.
Proper photos.
Reactions.

<!-- S100 -->
Basically, everything people
had been getting from messaging apps
for years.

<!-- S101 -->
But RCS needs an internet connection.

<!-- S102 -->
So when the data is gone,
the most modern system steps aside,
and the phone can fall back
to the oldest one.

<!-- S103 -->
A message designed in the 1980s,
squeezed into the gaps of a signalling channel,
limited to 160 characters.

<!-- S104 -->
It became a gold mine.

<!-- S105 -->
It was bypassed by the internet.

<!-- S106 -->
And it's still the thing
your phone reaches for
when nothing else works.

<!-- S107 -->
RCS has one more twist, though.

<!-- S108 -->
For years,
the best-known phone in the world
simply didn't speak it.

<!-- S109 -->
But that's a story for another video.

<!-- S110 -->
So here's a question for you.
Was the 160-character limit
a handicap texting survived,
or part of the reason it caught on?
Let me know in the comments.
