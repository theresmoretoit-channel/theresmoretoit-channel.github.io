<!-- S01 -->
Send a message from one iPhone
to another,
and it shows up in a blue bubble.

<!-- S02 -->
Send the same message
from an iPhone to an Android phone,
and the bubble is green.

<!-- S03 -->
Same words.
Same app.
Different colour.

<!-- S04 -->
For years, that colour came
with real problems.
Blurry videos.
Broken group chats.

<!-- S05 -->
In 2024, the iPhone finally learned
the same modern messaging standard
Android phones use.

<!-- S06 -->
And the bubbles
are still green.

<!-- S07 -->
So why?

<!-- S08 -->
Here's the first surprise.
Green wasn't invented
to mark Android users.

<!-- S09 -->
When the iPhone launched in 2007,
every text you sent on it was green.

<!-- S10 -->
Every single one.
Even between two iPhones.

<!-- S11 -->
Blue only arrived in 2011,
with a new service
called iMessage.

<!-- S12 -->
So green isn't the odd colour out.
Green is the original.

<!-- S13 -->
Blue is the exception.

<!-- S14 -->
And the colour doesn't describe
the phone on the other end.

<!-- S15 -->
It describes the road
the message travelled.

<!-- S16 -->
To see why that matters,
we need to look at those two roads.

<!-- S17 -->
The old road is the mobile network.

<!-- S18 -->
SMS for text,
MMS for pictures and video.

<!-- S19 -->
Practically every phone speaks them,
because they belong to the mobile network,
not to the phone makers.

<!-- S20 -->
But it's an old road.
An SMS holds a hundred and sixty characters.

<!-- S21 -->
And an MMS video is squeezed
until it fits the operator's size limit,
often about a megabyte, or less.

<!-- S22 -->
The new road is the internet.

<!-- S23 -->
iMessage sends your message
as ordinary data,
through Apple's own servers.

<!-- S24 -->
No operator in the middle.
No size limits from the flip-phone era.

<!-- S25 -->
Read receipts.
Typing indicators.
Full-quality photos.
Group chats that actually work.

<!-- S26 -->
But Apple's servers
only talk to Apple devices.

<!-- S27 -->
So when an iPhone sent a message
to anything else,
it couldn't take the new road.

<!-- S28 -->
It fell back to the old one.

<!-- S29 -->
Blue means Apple carried it.

<!-- S30 -->
Green means someone else did.

<!-- S31 -->
That's the whole technical answer.
And it would be a boring story,
if it ended there.

<!-- S32 -->
It doesn't.

<!-- S33 -->
Because falling back to the old road
made the conversation worse.

<!-- S34 -->
Not just for the Android user.
For everyone in the chat.

<!-- S35 -->
Add one Android phone
to a group of iPhones,
and the whole group dropped to MMS.

<!-- S36 -->
Videos arrived as a smudge.
Reactions arrived as text.
"Liked an image."

<!-- S37 -->
And for the person
everyone was waiting on,
it looked like their phone
was the problem.

<!-- S38 -->
In the United States,
this became a social thing.

<!-- S39 -->
In 2022, the Wall Street Journal
reported that American teenagers
dreaded the green bubble.

<!-- S40 -->
Some said they'd been singled out,
even ostracised, for switching.

<!-- S41 -->
The same year,
a survey of American teens found
that eighty-seven per cent
owned an iPhone.

<!-- S42 -->
And in 2024,
when the US government sued Apple
for monopolising smartphones,

<!-- S43 -->
the green bubble
was in the complaint.

<!-- S44 -->
So this wasn't a niche complaint.
In America, the colour mattered.

<!-- S45 -->
But here's the strange part.

<!-- S46 -->
In most of the world,
it barely registered.

<!-- S47 -->
Ask someone in Germany,
Brazil or India
about green bubbles,
and you may get a blank look.

<!-- S48 -->
And a big part of the reason
goes back to the price of a text message.

<!-- S49 -->
In much of Europe,
texts often cost money, one by one.

<!-- S50 -->
So when smartphones arrived,
people jumped to apps
that sent messages over data.

<!-- S51 -->
Above all, WhatsApp.

<!-- S52 -->
And WhatsApp looks the same
on an iPhone and on Android.

<!-- S53 -->
No blue.
No green.
No fallback.

<!-- S54 -->
In the United States,
texting had become cheap,
and often unlimited.

<!-- S55 -->
So there was little reason
to leave the phone's built-in app.

<!-- S56 -->
Americans kept texting,
and more and more of them
did it on an iPhone.

<!-- S57 -->
The green bubble problem took hold
where texting was cheap
and iPhones were common.

<!-- S58 -->
So why didn't Apple just
put iMessage on Android?

<!-- S59 -->
Inside Apple,
people did suggest it.

<!-- S60 -->
Emails that came out in a court case
show executives discussing the idea
in 2013.

<!-- S61 -->
But one executive argued
that iMessage on Android
would make it easier for iPhone families
to give their kids Android phones.

<!-- S62 -->
iMessage wasn't just a feature.
It was a reason to stay.

<!-- S63 -->
And in 2022, when someone told Apple's CEO
he couldn't send his mother certain videos,
because she had an Android phone,

<!-- S64 -->
the answer was:
"Buy your mom an iPhone."

<!-- S65 -->
But Apple isn't the only reason
this took so long.

<!-- S66 -->
Android had no single answer
to iMessage.

<!-- S67 -->
Google launched one messaging app
after another.
Talk. Hangouts. Allo.

<!-- S68 -->
And retired them, one after another.

<!-- S69 -->
The real successor to SMS
was supposed to be RCS.
Rich Communication Services.

<!-- S70 -->
A standard designed by the mobile industry
in the late 2000s.

<!-- S71 -->
But RCS depended on operators
to switch it on,
and for years, many didn't.

<!-- S72 -->
In the end, Google ran much of it
on its own servers.

<!-- S73 -->
And then, in November 2023,
Apple made an announcement.

<!-- S74 -->
The iPhone would support RCS.

<!-- S75 -->
Apple didn't say why now.

<!-- S76 -->
The timing lined up with
a European competition investigation.

<!-- S77 -->
Others pointed to China,
where new phones were being pushed
to support RCS.

<!-- S78 -->
A few weeks later,
a small app called Beeper Mini
let Android phones send real iMessages.

<!-- S79 -->
Apple shut it out
within days.

<!-- S80 -->
Blue stayed Apple's.
But in 2024, RCS arrived.

<!-- S81 -->
iPhones and Android phones
could finally share
read receipts,
typing indicators,
and proper photos.

<!-- S82 -->
In 2026, Apple and Google
began rolling out encryption
for those messages,
first as a beta,
and only with some operators.

<!-- S83 -->
So the road between the two
is finally paved.

<!-- S84 -->
Which brings us back
to the question.

<!-- S85 -->
Why is the bubble still green?

<!-- S86 -->
Because RCS is not iMessage.

<!-- S87 -->
It's a newer, better road,
but it isn't Apple's road.

<!-- S88 -->
Blue is reserved
for messages Apple carries
itself.

<!-- S89 -->
So the colour never really meant
"this phone is worse".

<!-- S90 -->
It meant
"this message isn't ours".

<!-- S91 -->
And that's the quiet change
behind the whole story.

<!-- S92 -->
SMS belonged to the operators,
and so it worked with everyone.

<!-- S93 -->
The messengers that replaced it
belong to the companies
that make the phones and the apps.

<!-- S94 -->
And a company that owns the road
gets to decide
who's allowed to use it.

<!-- S95 -->
And what colour you are
when you do.

<!-- S96 -->
So here's a question for you.
If every bubble turned
the same colour tomorrow,
would anyone actually switch phones?
Let me know in the comments.
