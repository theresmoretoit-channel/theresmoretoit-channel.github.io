<!-- S01 -->
In January 1642,
King Charles the First of England
walked into the House of Commons.
Armed soldiers waited at the door.

<!-- S02 -->
He had come to arrest five members
of Parliament, for treason.

<!-- S03 -->
But the five had been warned,
and they were already gone.
The king asked the Speaker
where they were.

<!-- S04 -->
The Speaker knelt, and said:
"I have neither eyes to see,
nor tongue to speak in this place,
but as this House is pleased
to direct me."

<!-- S05 -->
The king left empty-handed.
Behind him, the members shouted one word:
"Privilege! Privilege!"

<!-- S06 -->
The problem that day is older
than any modern constitution.
What happens when those in power
can arrest the people
who are meant to check them?

<!-- S07 -->
One answer democracies came up with
is parliamentary immunity.

<!-- S08 -->
Which raises a harder question.
How do you protect politicians
from persecution...
without putting them above the law?

<!-- S09 -->
Start with the obvious danger.
The government wants to pass
a controversial law,
and the vote is going to be close.

<!-- S10 -->
It controls the police.
So the night before the vote,
a few opposition members get arrested.

<!-- S11 -->
The government doesn't need
to win the argument.
It only needs to remove the people
who were going to vote against it.

<!-- S12 -->
That's the first thing immunity is for.
Parliament has to be able to work
without the government
deciding who gets to show up.

<!-- S13 -->
Forty-seven years
after the king came for the five,
England wrote a rule
into its Bill of Rights.

<!-- S14 -->
Speech and debate in Parliament
"ought not to be impeached or questioned
in any court or place out of Parliament."

<!-- S15 -->
Remember that wording.
It will come back.

<!-- S16 -->
The second danger is quieter.
Say Parliament passes a law
that costs a big company
ten million dollars.

<!-- S17 -->
You voted for it.
The company knows exactly who you are,
and it can afford
far more lawyers than you.

<!-- S18 -->
If it could sue you personally
for that vote,
your question would no longer be
"Is this law good for the country?"

<!-- S19 -->
It would be:
"What will this vote cost me?"

<!-- S20 -->
That's why many democracies
protect legislators
from legal consequences
for how they vote, or what they say
in the chamber.

<!-- S21 -->
Notice who that really protects.
Not the politician. The institution.
Voters need their representatives
to vote without fear.

<!-- S22 -->
But there's a second, very different
kind of immunity,
and this is where the argument starts.

<!-- S23 -->
There's a big difference between saying
"You can't punish me for how I voted"...

<!-- S24 -->
...and saying
"You can't prosecute me,
because I'm a member of Parliament."

<!-- S25 -->
The first protects the job.
The second protects the person,
even for things that have nothing
to do with the job.

<!-- S26 -->
Democracies disagree sharply
about how much of the second one to allow.

<!-- S27 -->
Take the United States.
The Constitution says members of Congress,
for any speech or debate in either House,
"shall not be questioned
in any other place."

<!-- S28 -->
Sound familiar?
It's the English Bill of Rights,
carried across the Atlantic.

<!-- S29 -->
American courts read the clause broadly.
It covers votes, committee work, reports,
the whole business of making laws.

<!-- S30 -->
But that's where it stops.
A senator who commits an ordinary crime
can be investigated and charged
like anyone else.
Nobody has to ask Congress first.

<!-- S31 -->
The American answer, roughly:
protect the lawmaking.
Not the lawmaker.

<!-- S32 -->
Britain, where it all started,
goes the same way.
A British MP accused of an ordinary crime
has no general immunity from prosecution.

<!-- S33 -->
Across Europe, the rules vary enormously,
from almost nothing
to protection from arrest and prosecution.

<!-- S34 -->
The Czech Republic goes further.
Its Constitution protects votes
and speeches too.

<!-- S35 -->
But it adds a second layer.
A member of Parliament
can't be criminally prosecuted
without the consent
of their own chamber.

<!-- S36 -->
For any crime,
even one that has nothing
to do with Parliament.
And if the chamber says no,
the prosecution is blocked
for as long as the mandate lasts.

<!-- S37 -->
There is a real argument for this.
A government could invent charges
to get rid of an opposition leader.

<!-- S38 -->
Even an investigation can be a weapon,
if the police and prosecutors
answer to the wrong people.
The chamber's vote is meant to catch that.

<!-- S39 -->
But it creates an obvious problem.
The people deciding
whether a politician can be prosecuted
are politicians themselves.

<!-- S40 -->
Which brings us to Andrej Babiš.
A billionaire businessman,
and, since December 2025,
the Czech Prime Minister.

<!-- S41 -->
For years, he has faced charges
in what's known as the Stork's Nest case.

<!-- S42 -->
Prosecutors say a farm
and conference resort
obtained about two million euros
in EU funding meant for small businesses
by falsely presenting the company
as a small, independent business.
Babiš denies any wrongdoing.

<!-- S43 -->
Each time he was elected to Parliament,
the case needed
the Czech lower house's consent again.
And three times, in 2017, 2018 and 2022,
it said yes.

<!-- S44 -->
The case went to court.
He was acquitted twice,
and twice the appeal court
overturned the acquittal.

<!-- S45 -->
In June 2025, it went further.
It bound the lower court
to find both defendants guilty,
based on the evidence already heard.

<!-- S46 -->
Then, in October 2025,
his party won the election.
A new mandate meant a new vote.
This time, his coalition had the majority.

<!-- S47 -->
In March 2026, a hundred and four MPs
voted against allowing the prosecution.
Eighty-one voted for it.
One abstained.

<!-- S48 -->
That vote did not declare him innocent.
It wasn't a verdict at all.

<!-- S49 -->
It means that for the rest of this term,
the courts cannot go on with his case.
When his mandate ends, it can resume.

<!-- S50 -->
And the same case produced
an almost perfect comparison.

<!-- S51 -->
Babiš wasn't the only defendant.
His former close aide, Jana Nagyová,
was charged alongside him.

<!-- S52 -->
She is a member of Parliament too,
of the European Parliament.
In April 2025, it voted
to lift her immunity.

<!-- S53 -->
After the Czech vote,
the two cases were split,
and hers went on without him.

<!-- S54 -->
In May 2026, the court found her guilty
of subsidy fraud.
A three-year suspended sentence,
and a fine of half a million Czech crowns,
about twenty thousand euros.
In August, the appeal court upheld it.

<!-- S55 -->
That tells us nothing about
how Babiš's own case would have ended.

<!-- S56 -->
But it shows exactly what immunity can do.
Same case. Two parliaments.
One said yes.
The other said no.

<!-- S57 -->
So, why do politicians have immunity?

<!-- S58 -->
Not because they matter
more than anyone else.
A parliament has to be able
to vote against the powerful,
the government included,
without politicians being punished
for doing their job.

<!-- S59 -->
That part is easy to defend.
The hard part is everything beyond it.

<!-- S60 -->
When the accusation has nothing
to do with Parliament,
should a politician still be protected?
For how long?
And who gets to decide?

<!-- S61 -->
Charles the First showed
why Parliament needs protection
from those in power.

<!-- S62 -->
The Stork's Nest shows
what can happen when that protection
depends on politicians themselves.

<!-- S63 -->
So the real question isn't
whether politicians should have immunity.
It's what exactly
that immunity should protect.

<!-- S64 -->
Your vote?
Your speech?
Your right to oppose the government?

<!-- S65 -->
Or yourself...
when you're accused of an ordinary crime?

<!-- S66 -->
Democracy needs politicians
who are free to make hard decisions.
It also needs politicians
who answer to the law.

<!-- S67 -->
Finding the line between those two
is the real problem of immunity.
