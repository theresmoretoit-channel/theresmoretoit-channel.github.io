<!-- S01 -->
Stop eating for sixteen hours,
says the internet,
and something switches on inside you.

<!-- S02 -->
Your cells start taking out the trash.
Old, damaged parts get broken down
and recycled.
A kind of built-in repair mode.

<!-- S03 -->
It even has a scientific name.
Autophagy.
Greek for "self-eating".

<!-- S04 -->
And in 2016, the scientist
who worked out how it happens
won a Nobel Prize.

<!-- S05 -->
But where does that sixteen
actually come from?
And has anyone ever measured it
in a human being?

<!-- S06 -->
Follow the number back,
and the trail gets thin very quickly.

<!-- S07 -->
Sixteen hours is familiar
from a meal schedule.
Sixteen hours without food,
then eight hours to eat.

<!-- S08 -->
One guide to it, from 2010,
promoted it for training and fat loss.
The word autophagy isn't in it.

<!-- S09 -->
The link came later.
One popular fasting guide says
autophagy "typically begins
around the sixteen-hour mark".

<!-- S10 -->
As its source, it links to a study.
A study on mice,
which went without food
for twenty-four or forty-eight hours.

<!-- S11 -->
That study never measured sixteen hours.
And it wasn't done on people.

<!-- S12 -->
Autophagy doesn't start
when you stop eating.
It's running right now in your cells,
at a low background level.

<!-- S13 -->
Fasting can turn it up.
But up from what, by how much,
and after how long in a human?
That's the real question.

<!-- S14 -->
Your cells are full of machinery
that wears out.
Proteins fold the wrong way.
Clumps build up.
Mitochondria, the cell's power plants,
get damaged.

<!-- S15 -->
Small things are handled
by the proteasome,
a molecular shredder
that destroys proteins one by one.

<!-- S16 -->
Autophagy can take apart
an entire mitochondrion.
The proteasome can't do that on its own.

<!-- S17 -->
It starts with a membrane.
A thin sheet forms around the cargo
and keeps growing until it closes.

<!-- S18 -->
The result is a bag with a double wall,
called an autophagosome.
Next, it fuses with a lysosome.

<!-- S19 -->
A lysosome is the cell's stomach.
A sealed, acidic sac
full of digestive enzymes.

<!-- S20 -->
The cargo is broken down
into its building blocks,
and those go back out into the cell
to be used again.

<!-- S21 -->
The lysosome's discoverer,
Christian de Duve,
gave this process its name in 1963.

<!-- S22 -->
How much does a body depend on it?
Newborn mice show it clearly.

<!-- S23 -->
At birth, the food supply
from the mother is cut off,
and autophagy surges
in tissues across the body.

<!-- S23b -->
Mice born without one key autophagy gene
look normal.
They die within a day.

<!-- S24 -->
So this isn't an emergency mode
waiting for you to skip a meal.
It's maintenance,
and it never stops.

<!-- S25 -->
Then where does the fasting idea
come from?
Partly from yeast.

<!-- S26 -->
In the early 1990s, Yoshinori Ohsumi
took yeast cells that couldn't digest
anything in their recycling chamber,
and starved them.

<!-- S27 -->
Within an hour,
little bubbles of cell contents
began to pile up inside.
After three hours,
they almost filled the chamber.

<!-- S28 -->
Starvation had turned the recycling up.
And because digestion was blocked,
the bubbles had nowhere to go.
Remember that trick.

<!-- S29 -->
From those yeast, he found
fifteen genes the process depends on.
That's what the Nobel Prize was for.
Genes, in yeast.
Not a fasting schedule.

<!-- S30 -->
The famous fasting results
came later, from mice.
In a 2010 study,
young male mice went without food
for one or two days.

<!-- S31 -->
In their brain cells,
the researchers counted
far more autophagosomes.
After two days, in one type of neuron,
about four times as many.

<!-- S32 -->
That's real data.
But a mouse burns about seven times
more energy per gram of body
than a human does.

<!-- S33 -->
In one lab study, adult mice lost
about fourteen percent of their weight
in a single day without food.

<!-- S34 -->
So for a mouse,
a day or two without food
isn't a long fast.
It's an extreme one.

<!-- S35 -->
And results from different organs
and methods don't always agree.
One mouse study found no response
in the brain at all.
Another, six years later,
found a strong one.

<!-- S36 -->
Even in mice, the answer depends
on where you look, and how.
Nobody can turn those days
into a human countdown.

<!-- S37 -->
So what about people?
The first problem is simple.
You can't look inside
a living person's organs and watch.

<!-- S38 -->
Researchers can take blood,
or a small piece of muscle.
That's about it.

<!-- S39 -->
And counting isn't enough.
More autophagosomes could mean
the cell is recycling faster...

<!-- S40 -->
or that the exit is blocked
and they're piling up,
just like in Ohsumi's yeast.

<!-- S41 -->
What matters is the whole flow,
from bag to breakdown.
Scientists call it flux.
Measuring it in people is new,
and so far mostly limited to blood.

<!-- S42 -->
The results so far are small and mixed.
After a twelve-hour overnight fast,
blood cells recycled no faster
than an hour after a protein meal.

<!-- S43 -->
Eighteen-hour fasts for four days
raised the activity of one autophagy gene
in blood by about a fifth.
That's a signal from a gene,
not a measurement of the recycling.

<!-- S44 -->
The largest study followed
a hundred and twenty-one people
for six months,
fasting for twenty hours,
three times a week.

<!-- S45 -->
Within the fasting group,
the change wasn't significant.
A comparison added after the fact
did find a difference at six months,
partly because autophagy drifted down
in the standard-care group.

<!-- S46 -->
One of the field's leading researchers,
Guido Kroemer, was asked directly.
Does a sixteen-hour fast
induce autophagy in any human tissue?

<!-- S47 -->
His answer was three words.
"We don't know."

<!-- S48 -->
That's the honest state of things.
Not "it doesn't happen".
We simply don't know yet.

<!-- S49 -->
So should we even want more of it?
In animals, the case looks strong.

<!-- S50 -->
Mice that lose autophagy in the brain
develop movement problems,
and their neurons fill with clumps
of protein.

<!-- S51 -->
Turn it up, and some animals
live longer.
Mice engineered to overproduce Atg5
lived about seventeen percent longer.

<!-- S52 -->
In humans, there's no evidence yet
that boosting autophagy
makes anyone live longer.

<!-- S53 -->
And then there's cancer.

<!-- S54 -->
Mice with only one working copy
of a key autophagy gene
develop tumours more often.
Autophagy helps keep cancer
from starting.

<!-- S55 -->
But once a tumour is established,
it can use autophagy to survive.
Pancreatic tumours rely on it heavily,
and in mice, blocking it
made them shrink.

<!-- S56 -->
Autophagy isn't good or bad.
It's a tool, and what it does
depends on whose side it's working for.

<!-- S57 -->
So what really happens
after sixteen hours without food?

<!-- S58 -->
Fasting does change the fuel
your body uses.
Over time, it relies more
on fat and ketones.

<!-- S59 -->
But that's a gradual shift,
not a universal countdown.
And it doesn't tell us
when autophagy changes.

<!-- S60 -->
Inside your cells,
the recycling that never stopped
keeps going.
Maybe a little faster, in some tissues.
By how much, nobody can tell you yet.

<!-- S61 -->
So when a timeline promises
that repair begins at a precise hour,
it knows more than the science does.

<!-- S62 -->
Your cells don't stay alive
by keeping everything forever.

<!-- S63 -->
Sometimes, staying alive
means knowing what to throw away.
