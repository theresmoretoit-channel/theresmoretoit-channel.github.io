<!-- S01 -->
In the early 1970s,
every time you boarded a passenger plane,
your chance of dying on that flight
was about one in three hundred
and fifty thousand.

<!-- S02 -->
Today, it's about one in fourteen million.

<!-- S03 -->
In a single lifetime,
flying has become roughly forty times safer.

<!-- S04 -->
And it's not because planes stopped breaking.
Engines still fail.
Sensors still freeze.
Pilots still make mistakes.

<!-- S05 -->
So what actually changed?

<!-- S06 -->
Almost every rule on a modern flight
exists for the same reason.

<!-- S07 -->
Somebody, somewhere, died.

<!-- S08 -->
But that alone doesn't explain it.
People die on roads too,
and roads haven't become
anything like forty times safer.

<!-- S09 -->
So the real question isn't
whether aviation learns from its crashes.
It's how it manages to learn so well.

<!-- S10 -->
On the thirtieth of October 1935,
Boeing showed the US Army its new bomber.
The Model 299.

<!-- S11 -->
Four engines, retractable landing gear,
and far more switches and settings
than any pilot was used to.

<!-- S12 -->
Seconds after takeoff,
it climbed steeply, stalled, and crashed.
Two of the five men on board died.

<!-- S13 -->
The pilots weren't careless.
They had simply forgotten to release a lock
that held the controls in place
on the ground.

<!-- S14 -->
A newspaper called it
"too much airplane for one man to fly".

<!-- S15 -->
The answer wasn't more training.
It was a card.
A short list of things to check
before takeoff, and before landing.

<!-- S16 -->
The Army bought the bomber anyway.
It became the B-17,
and its crews flew it with checklists.

<!-- S17 -->
Today, a captain with twenty thousand hours
still reads a list
to make sure the landing gear is down.

<!-- S18 -->
Not because they don't know how to fly.
But because knowing what to do
isn't the same as doing it,
every single time.

<!-- S19 -->
In 1952, Britain's de Havilland Comet
became the first jet airliner
to carry paying passengers.
It flew higher and faster than any airliner before it,
above most of the weather.

<!-- S20 -->
Then, in early 1954,
two Comets broke apart in mid-air,
within three months of each other.
No storm. No collision. No warning.

<!-- S21 -->
Every Comet was grounded.
Investigators put a complete one
into a giant tank of water,
and pressurised the cabin
and released it, over and over.

<!-- S22 -->
After the equivalent
of about three thousand flights,
the fuselage tore open.

<!-- S23 -->
The crack had started at the corner
of an opening in the fuselage.
Every flight stretched the metal there,
a little, until it gave way.
Metal fatigue.

<!-- S24 -->
From then on, every new airliner
had to be designed against metal fatigue,
and its makers had to show
that it would hold.

<!-- S25 -->
There was a second lesson in the Comets,
and it came from a young Australian scientist
who'd been asked to help
work out what had gone wrong.

<!-- S26 -->
David Warren noticed
that the investigators could only guess.
Nobody knew what the crew had seen or said
in their last moments.

<!-- S27 -->
So he built a device that recorded
the instruments and the voices in the cockpit,
and would survive the crash.

<!-- S28 -->
We still call it the black box.
It's actually bright orange,
so it can be found in the wreckage.

<!-- S29 -->
And once investigators could hear
what went on inside a cockpit,
they found problems
that weren't in the machine at all.

<!-- S30 -->
On the twenty-seventh of March 1977,
two Boeing 747s were on the same runway
at Tenerife, in the Canary Islands.
The fog was so thick
that neither crew could see the other.

<!-- S31 -->
The KLM jet began its takeoff roll.
The Pan Am jet was still on the runway,
taxiing towards it.

<!-- S32 -->
The KLM captain believed
he'd been cleared to take off.
He hadn't.

<!-- S33 -->
The tower had said "OK",
and then "stand by for takeoff".
But the second half was lost,
because another radio call
came in on top of it.

<!-- S34 -->
In the cockpit, the flight engineer asked,
"Is he not clear, that Pan American?"
The captain answered, "Oh, yes."
And kept going.

<!-- S35 -->
Five hundred and eighty-three people died.
It's still the deadliest accident
in the history of aviation.

<!-- S36 -->
Two things changed.
The first was a single word.

<!-- S37 -->
Today, pilots and controllers
only say "takeoff"
when an aircraft is actually cleared
to take off.
Before that, it's a "departure".

<!-- S38 -->
The second change took longer.
The KLM captain was the head
of the airline's training for the 747.
His engineer had doubts, and voiced them.
Once.

<!-- S39 -->
Out of Tenerife, other crashes like it,
and years of research at NASA,
came a new kind of training,
known as Crew Resource Management.

<!-- S40 -->
The captain is still in charge.
But a first officer saying,
"I think that's wrong,"
isn't challenging authority.
They're doing their job.

<!-- S41 -->
And sometimes, that teamwork
is all a crew has left.

<!-- S42 -->
On the nineteenth of July 1989,
United Flight 232, a DC-10,
was cruising over Iowa
when its tail engine blew apart.

<!-- S43 -->
Fragments tore through the tail
and drained all three hydraulic systems.
The pilots could no longer move
a single control surface.

<!-- S44 -->
Three separate systems
were meant to make that impossible.
But all three ran through
the same small part of the tail.

<!-- S45 -->
The crew found one way left to steer.
More power on one wing engine,
less on the other.

<!-- S46 -->
A DC-10 instructor,
travelling as a passenger,
came up to the cockpit
and worked the throttles for them.

<!-- S47 -->
They reached the airport at Sioux City.
The landing was a disaster.
The plane cartwheeled and broke apart.

<!-- S48 -->
And yet a hundred and eighty-four
of the two hundred and ninety-six
people on board survived.

<!-- S49 -->
Captain Al Haynes later said
a big part of the reason
was the way the four of them worked together.
The training that came out of crashes
like Tenerife.

<!-- S50 -->
By then, computers could fly a plane
more smoothly than any pilot.
That made flying safer.
But it created a new kind of problem.

<!-- S51 -->
On the first of June 2009,
Air France Flight 447 was flying
from Rio de Janeiro to Paris,
at night, over the Atlantic.

<!-- S52 -->
Ice crystals blocked the sensors
that measure airspeed.
The autopilot switched off,
and suddenly the pilots were flying by hand,
in the dark, in turbulence.

<!-- S53 -->
The pilot at the controls
pulled the nose up.
The plane climbed, slowed,
and stalled.

<!-- S54 -->
The stall warning sounded
again and again.
For more than three minutes,
the plane fell towards the sea
with its nose still pointing up.

<!-- S55 -->
Two hundred and twenty-eight people died.
The recorders were found almost two years later,
almost four kilometres under the sea.

<!-- S56 -->
The lesson was uncomfortable.
Automation makes flying safer...
but pilots still have to understand
what it's doing,
and be ready when it stops.

<!-- S57 -->
So far, every lesson in this story
has cost lives.

<!-- S58 -->
But the biggest reason flying is so safe
is that most of its lessons don't.

<!-- S59 -->
On the first of December 1974,
a TWA jet descended too early
on its approach to Washington Dulles
and flew into a ridge.
Ninety-two people died.

<!-- S60 -->
Six weeks earlier,
a United crew had made the same mistake,
on the same approach.
They caught it just in time,
and reported it.

<!-- S61 -->
But only United's own pilots were told.
The warning never reached TWA.

<!-- S62 -->
So in 1976, America set up
a reporting system
run by NASA, not by the regulator.

<!-- S63 -->
Pilots, controllers and mechanics
could report their own mistakes,
and in most cases,
the report couldn't be used to punish them.

<!-- S64 -->
It has received more than two million reports.
Close calls, confusing charts,
a checklist that was easy to misread...
each one a lesson,
learned without anyone getting hurt.

<!-- S65 -->
And when something does go wrong,
the investigation has one purpose,
written into international rules.
To prevent the next accident.
Not to decide who's to blame.

<!-- S66 -->
That's the part that's easy to miss.
People only tell you about their mistakes
if they're not afraid
of what happens next.

<!-- S67 -->
Safety experts like to picture this
as slices of Swiss cheese.
The design, the checklist,
the crew, the training.
Every slice has holes in it.

<!-- S68 -->
An accident only happens
when the holes in every slice line up.
And every crash, and every report,
adds a slice, or makes a hole smaller.

<!-- S69 -->
But that system is run by people too.
And it can be weakened from the inside.

<!-- S70 -->
In October 2018,
a brand-new Boeing 737 MAX
crashed into the sea off Indonesia,
minutes after takeoff.

<!-- S71 -->
Less than five months later,
a second one crashed in Ethiopia.
Three hundred and forty-six people died
in the two crashes.

<!-- S72 -->
In both, new software
kept pushing the nose down,
because of a single faulty sensor.
Many of the pilots flying the MAX
hadn't been told the software was there.

<!-- S73 -->
The evening before the first crash,
another crew had faced the same problem
on the same aircraft.
They switched the system off.
The plane flew again the next morning.

<!-- S74 -->
Investigators found failures
far from the cockpit.
In how the aircraft was designed,
how it was certified,
and how much of that checking
the regulator had left to the manufacturer.

<!-- S75 -->
The slices of cheese were all still there.
Some had just been made thinner.

<!-- S76 -->
The MAX was grounded
for around twenty months.
And the lesson went back into the system,
like every lesson before it.

<!-- S77 -->
When several crashes make the headlines,
it can feel as if flying
is getting more dangerous.
But a single year is too short
to reveal a trend.
Aviation safety is measured in decades.

<!-- S78 -->
So why is flying so safe now?
Not because planes stopped failing,
or pilots stopped making mistakes.

<!-- S79 -->
But because every failure,
and every near miss,
is taken apart, written down,
and shared with everyone who flies.

<!-- S80 -->
None of it is there by accident.
It's a collection of lessons.
And some of them are written
in the names of people
who never made it home.

<!-- S81 -->
We didn't learn to fly safely
by never making mistakes.
We learned by refusing
to make the same mistake twice.

<!-- S82 -->
So here's a question for you.
Aviation learned to treat mistakes
as information, not as something to hide.
Where else do you think we need that most?
Let me know in the comments.
