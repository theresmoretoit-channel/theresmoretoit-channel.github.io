<!-- S01 -->
In 2013, Microsoft did something
unusual for a software giant.
It wrote a YouTube app
for its own phones,
because Google wouldn't.

<!-- S02 -->
Microsoft's version
didn't show Google's adverts,
and Google soon wanted it gone.

<!-- S03 -->
So Microsoft rebuilt it,
adverts and all.
Google blocked that one too.

<!-- S04 -->
Think about that for a moment.
The biggest software company in the world
couldn't put the most popular video site
on its own phone.

<!-- S05 -->
And Windows Phone
was not a half-hearted project.
Microsoft had the money,
a design that reviewers loved,
and Nokia, at the time
the biggest phone maker on Earth.

<!-- S06 -->
At its best, in 2013,
about three smartphones
in every hundred sold
ran Windows Phone.

<!-- S07 -->
In 2015, Microsoft wrote off
seven point six billion dollars
on the phone business
it had bought from Nokia,
and announced up to
seven thousand eight hundred job cuts.

<!-- S08 -->
Two years later,
it quietly gave up.

<!-- S09 -->
So what killed Windows Phone?

<!-- S10 -->
Let's treat it like a case.
There are five suspects.
Each one had a motive,
and some of them have an alibi.

<!-- S11 -->
Suspect number one: time.

<!-- S12 -->
In January 2007,
Steve Ballmer,
Microsoft's chief executive,
was asked about the brand-new iPhone.

<!-- S13 -->
He laughed at it.
Five hundred dollars,
even with a phone contract,
and no keyboard.
Business customers, he said,
wouldn't want it.

<!-- S14 -->
That wasn't a silly view at the time.
Microsoft had been selling
smartphone software since 2002,
and people who did business on a phone
wanted a keyboard.

<!-- S15 -->
The iPhone sold anyway,
and Android followed.
Windows Phone 7 reached the shops
in October 2010,
three years and nine months
after the iPhone was first shown.

<!-- S16 -->
Guilty?
Not so fast.
Android was late too.
The first Android phone came out
twenty-one months
after the iPhone was shown,
and Android won.

<!-- S17 -->
So being late can't be the whole story.
Keep it in mind, though.
It comes back.

<!-- S18 -->
Suspect number two:
the phone itself.

<!-- S19 -->
The first Windows Phone
had no copy and paste,
and apps from other companies
couldn't keep running in the background.

<!-- S20 -->
That sounds bad, until you remember
that the first iPhone
had no copy and paste either,
and no app store for a whole year.

<!-- S21 -->
And people liked the design.
Big, clean type.
Square tiles on the home screen
that showed your messages,
your missed calls and your photos
without opening anything.

<!-- S22 -->
Windows Phone wasn't perfect.
But it wasn't a bad phone.
And that distinction matters.

<!-- S23 -->
Suspect number three,
and the one everybody names:
the apps.

<!-- S24 -->
Instagram came to Windows Phone
in late 2013,
three years after the iPhone,
and labelled as a beta.

<!-- S25 -->
Snapchat never came at all.
And when people used
unofficial Snapchat apps instead,
Snapchat blocked them.

<!-- S26 -->
For many owners,
the gap was closer to home.
Their bank had no app.
Neither did their train company.
And the game their friends played wasn't there.

<!-- S27 -->
Microsoft knew.
It paid developers.
It gave away phones.
In some cases,
its own engineers wrote the app for them.

<!-- S28 -->
So that's our answer, right?
No apps.
Case closed.

<!-- S29 -->
Not quite.
"No apps" isn't a cause.
It's the thing that needs explaining.

<!-- S30 -->
Why would a developer skip
a platform from Microsoft,
with Nokia behind it?

<!-- S31 -->
Imagine you run a small studio.
An app costs money to build,
and more to keep up to date.
On a platform
with eighty percent of users,
that money comes back.
On one with three percent,
it usually doesn't.

<!-- S32 -->
Where would you spend it?

<!-- S33 -->
In 2017, a Microsoft vice president,
long the public face of Windows Phone,
said much the same.
They had paid developers,
they had written apps for them,
but there were too few users
for most companies to invest.

<!-- S34 -->
So the apps weren't there
because the users weren't there.
And the users weren't there
because the apps weren't.

<!-- S35 -->
That's a loop.
And a loop doesn't tell us
who started it.
Let's keep going.

<!-- S36 -->
Suspect number four: Nokia.
Or rather, one man at Nokia.

<!-- S37 -->
In 2010, Nokia was still
the biggest phone maker in the world.
Its smartphones ran its own system,
called Symbian,
and Symbian was losing ground fast
to the iPhone and Android.

<!-- S38 -->
That September,
Nokia hired a new boss, Stephen Elop.
He came straight from Microsoft.

<!-- S39 -->
In February 2011,
he sent his staff a memo.
Nokia, he wrote,
was standing on a burning platform.

<!-- S40 -->
Three days later,
Nokia announced
that its future smartphones
would run Windows Phone.

<!-- S41 -->
The first of them arrived
nine months later.
Until then,
Nokia kept selling Symbian phones,
on a system Nokia had just told the world
it was leaving.
Sales of those phones collapsed.

<!-- S41b -->
Nokia even had a new system of its own,
called MeeGo.
Its only phone, the N9,
got excellent reviews.
Nokia dropped MeeGo anyway.

<!-- S41c -->
In 2013, Microsoft agreed to buy
Nokia's phone business,
in a deal worth
about seven billion dollars,
and Elop went back to Microsoft with it.

<!-- S42 -->
To a lot of people,
that looked like a plan.
A Microsoft man walks into Nokia,
ties it to Windows,
and hands it back cheap.
A Trojan horse.

<!-- S43 -->
There's no evidence it was a plan.
But a bet doesn't have to be a conspiracy
to be a costly mistake,
and for Nokia, it was.

<!-- S44 -->
Still, did it kill Windows Phone?
Because of that bet,
the biggest phone maker of 2011
was making Windows phones,
and reviewers praised their cameras and design.

<!-- S45 -->
And with all of that,
Windows Phone reached three percent.

<!-- S46 -->
Nokia made a risky bet, and lost.
But whatever was killing Windows Phone
was stronger than Nokia.

<!-- S47 -->
Suspect number five: Google.

<!-- S48 -->
We've seen the YouTube fight.
In 2012, a Google manager was asked
why Google wasn't making its apps
for Windows Phone.
His answer was simple.
Google would go where the users are,
and they weren't there.

<!-- S49 -->
Google also gave Android
to phone makers for free.
Microsoft charged them
a licence fee for every phone,
until 2014.

<!-- S50 -->
And here's the strange part.
Android wasn't free for everyone.
Microsoft held patents
that it said Android used,
and most big Android phone makers
paid Microsoft for every phone they sold.

<!-- S51 -->
In one year,
Samsung alone paid Microsoft
about a billion dollars.

<!-- S52 -->
Microsoft was making good money
from its rival's success.

<!-- S53 -->
So did Google kill Windows Phone?
Look at its reason again.
Not enough users.
That's the same reason
every other developer gave.

<!-- S54 -->
Google didn't start the loop.
But it had no reason
to help a rival break out of it,
and it didn't.

<!-- S55 -->
Five suspects,
and none of them quite did it.
Which means we've missed one.

<!-- S56 -->
Suspect number six:
Microsoft.

<!-- S57 -->
Before Windows Phone,
Microsoft had Windows Mobile,
with thousands of apps
written for it.

<!-- S58 -->
Windows Phone 7 couldn't run any of them.
Developers had to start again,
and rewrite their apps.

<!-- S59 -->
Then, in April 2012,
the Lumia 900 went on sale in America.
Nokia's big flagship.

<!-- S60 -->
Ten weeks later,
Microsoft announced Windows Phone 8,
and said the Lumia 900
would never get it.

<!-- S61 -->
Nor would any other phone
already on sale.
Old apps would still run
on the new phones,
but new apps wouldn't run on the old ones.

<!-- S62 -->
This time, it was the users
who had to start again,
with a new phone.

<!-- S63 -->
In 2015, Windows 10 Mobile arrived,
with yet another way of building apps.
Older apps still worked,
but new ones were increasingly
written for the new system,
and many phones sold with Windows Phone 8
were never offered the upgrade.

<!-- S64 -->
Three resets in five years.
Not every one broke everything.
But each one told someone,
users, developers, or both,
the same thing.
Whatever you put in here,
start again.

<!-- S65 -->
To see why that was fatal,
we need to go back to 1999.

<!-- S66 -->
That year, an American judge
wrote down why no rival
could catch Windows on the PC.

<!-- S67 -->
He called it
the applications barrier to entry.

<!-- S68 -->
People bought Windows
because the programs they needed
ran on it.
Developers wrote for Windows
because that's where the people were.

<!-- S69 -->
Any new system had to climb that wall
from the outside,
with no programs and no people.

<!-- S70 -->
We've looked at how that wall
made Windows on the PC.
On phones, the same machine
ran the other way.

<!-- S71 -->
By 2010, the loop was already spinning
for the iPhone and Android.
This time,
Microsoft was the one outside the wall.

<!-- S72 -->
That's why being late mattered.
Every month, the wall got higher.
Android had been late too,
but it started climbing
while the wall was still low,
and it was free for phone makers.

<!-- S73 -->
And that's why the resets hurt so much.
On a wall like that,
you can't afford to stop climbing.
Every reset gave users a reason to wait,
and developers a reason to hold off.

<!-- S74 -->
So who killed Windows Phone?

<!-- S75 -->
Not Nokia, on its own.
Not Google.
Not really the missing apps.

<!-- S76 -->
It was the same wall
that made Windows untouchable.
Microsoft knew that wall
better than anyone.
It had made its fortune on the inside.
On phones, it never found a way over,
and every reset made the climb harder.

<!-- S77 -->
In October 2017,
Microsoft said it would keep fixing
Windows on phones,
but new features and new hardware
were no longer the focus.
Support ended in December 2019.

<!-- S78 -->
Microsoft didn't leave your phone, though.
Outlook, Word, Excel and Teams
all run on the iPhone and on Android.

<!-- S79 -->
If you can't get over the wall,
you can still sell things
to the people inside it.

<!-- S80 -->
So here's a question for you.
Was there ever room
for a third kind of phone?
Or was Windows Phone
doomed from the start?
Let me know in the comments.
