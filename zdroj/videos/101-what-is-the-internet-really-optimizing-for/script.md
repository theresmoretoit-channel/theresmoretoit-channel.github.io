<!-- S01 -->
Imagine spending hours
on an educational video.

Researching the subject.
Checking the facts.
Writing the script.
Creating original visuals.

<!-- S02 -->
Then imagine making a short video,
uploading it to TikTok,
and watching it get hundreds of views
within a few hours.

<!-- S03 -->
Meanwhile, the carefully researched
long video struggles to find anyone.

<!-- S04 -->
Of course, a single example
proves very little.
The short might simply be more appealing.
The algorithm might have found
an audience for one and not the other.

<!-- S05 -->
But the contrast raises a question.
What exactly is the internet rewarding?

<!-- S06 -->
If the answer were simply quality,
or effort,
the online world might look
very different.

<!-- S07 -->
So perhaps the problem isn't
the people making the content.
Perhaps it's the system deciding
what millions of people get to see.

<!-- S08 -->
And that system has a problem
it can never fully solve.
It can't see what a video is worth.

<!-- S09 -->
It can't watch a careful explanation
and a pointless stunt,
and decide which one
is more valuable to humanity.

<!-- S10 -->
What it can see is what people do.
Whether they click. How long they watch.
Whether they skip, like, or share.

<!-- S11 -->
Every one of those numbers is a clue
about what people value.
None of them is the value itself.
And trouble starts
when the clue becomes the goal.

<!-- S11b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S12 -->
YouTube found that out early.
For years, its recommendations
leaned heavily on clicks.

<!-- S13 -->
But a click only proves
that a title or a thumbnail worked.
Not that the video did.

<!-- S14 -->
So in 2012, YouTube shifted its weight
towards watch time instead.

<!-- S14b -->
Four years later, its own engineers
explained why in a research paper.
Ranking by clicks, they wrote,
often promotes deceptive videos
that people don't finish.
In a word, clickbait.

<!-- S15 -->
Watch time is a much better clue.
It's harder to fake.
But it still isn't quality.

<!-- S16 -->
Think about how a video earns it.
First, it has to stop you leaving.
A video about nuclear fusion
needs a few minutes to get good.
It has to set up the problem, the physics,
and why anyone should care.

<!-- S17 -->
A video of someone dropping
a watermelon off a roof
needs no introduction.

<!-- S18 -->
No background knowledge.
Nothing to remember.
Not long to wait.

<!-- S19 -->
The watermelon doesn't win
because it matters more.
It wins because it's easier to consume.

<!-- S20 -->
And a feed makes that advantage
enormous.
Picture yourself scrolling.

<!-- S21 -->
A scientist begins with a definition
and a graph about the climate.
You could learn something.
But you'd have to put in some effort first.

<!-- S22 -->
Swipe. A dog falls asleep sitting up.
Instant reward.

<!-- S23 -->
Swipe. An absurd stunt.
Swipe. An argument.
Swipe. Something unbelievable.

<!-- S24 -->
When the next video is one swipe away,
trying something new costs nothing.
And so does leaving.

<!-- S25 -->
Short videos can be brilliant,
and long ones can be terrible.
Length isn't the point.

<!-- S26 -->
The point is that a creator
now has a few seconds
to convince you to stay.
A complicated idea may need time
to become interesting.

<!-- S27 -->
A shocking image is interesting
before you even know what it is.
Guess which one has the easier job.

<!-- S28 -->
And why would a platform care
how long you stay?
Because most of them are paid
by advertisers,
and advertisers pay for attention.

<!-- S29 -->
So picture two ways
to spend twenty minutes.
A careful explanation
of a scientific discovery.
Afterwards, something finally makes sense.

<!-- S30 -->
Or a few hundred unrelated clips.
Some funny. Some irritating.
Most forgotten by the next one.

<!-- S31 -->
To the platform,
both look like engagement.
Only one of them
taught you something.

<!-- S32 -->
Economists have a name for this.
Goodhart's law.
When a measure becomes a target,
it stops being a good measure.

<!-- S33 -->
Clicks were a fair sign of interest...
until titles were written to win them.

<!-- S34 -->
Watch time is a fair sign of engagement...
but it can't tell
whether the time was well spent.

<!-- S35 -->
And then there's the third clue.
Reactions.
A comment can mean a post
matters to people.
Or that it made them furious.

<!-- S36 -->
In 2018, Facebook changed its feed
to favour posts that got
comments and reactions.

<!-- S37 -->
Its own researchers later warned
that the change was rewarding outrage.
And publishers complained
that their most divisive posts
were the ones that spread.

<!-- S38 -->
One political party in Poland
told Facebook that, by its own estimate,
its posts had gone
from about half negative
to about eighty per cent negative.

<!-- S39 -->
And it said why.
Because of the change to the feed.

<!-- S40 -->
That's Goodhart's law
at full strength.
The metric didn't just measure
what people posted.
It changed what they posted.

<!-- S41 -->
So is it the audience?
Are people simply getting stupider?
That would be too easy.
And probably wrong.

<!-- S42 -->
Humans have always loved spectacle.
Romans had gladiators.
Crowds gathered for public executions.
Newspapers learned long ago
that scandal sells.

<!-- S42b -->
Even the famous claim that our
attention span is now shorter
than a goldfish's
seems to come from nowhere.

<!-- S42c -->
It appeared in a 2015 report
from Microsoft,
and nobody has found the study behind it.
Or the goldfish.

<!-- S43 -->
What has changed is the scale
and the convenience.
A broadcaster used to choose
what was on tonight.

<!-- S44 -->
Now a system picks the next thing
for you, every few seconds,
based on what you just did.

<!-- S45 -->
You no longer search for entertainment.
Entertainment searches for you.

<!-- S46 -->
And if the easy option wins
just a little more often,
across millions of people,
repeated thousands of times...
that becomes a cultural force.

<!-- S47 -->
The question isn't whether
people are stupid.
It's whether the environment
lets them use their intelligence.

<!-- S48 -->
A low view count doesn't prove
a video is good.
A high one doesn't prove it's bad.

<!-- S49 -->
But if popularity gets confused
with value,
the easiest things to consume
become the easiest things to find.
And everything else has to fight
just to be noticed.

<!-- S50 -->
So what's the answer?
Ban short videos?
Blame people for watching silly clips?

<!-- S51 -->
No.

<!-- S51b -->
Sometimes you want astrophysics.
Sometimes you want a cat
behaving like a broken robot.
Both are fine.

<!-- S52 -->
The problem is when one system
becomes the main doorway
to what we see and learn,
and the number it chases
isn't quite what we value.

<!-- S53 -->
Platforms do know this.
YouTube asks viewers in surveys
to rate videos from one to five stars,
and tries to reward time
people say was well spent.

<!-- S54 -->
That's a better clue.
But it's still a guess about what you value,
made by a system
that's paid by your attention.

<!-- S55 -->
At the very least, we can stop
pretending that the most popular content
must be the best content.

<!-- S56 -->
Because those are two different questions.
What do people want to watch right now?
And what do we want
to still be there ten years from now?

<!-- S57 -->
We need jokes and discoveries,
ten-second distractions
and ideas that take an hour.
The goal isn't to get rid of the fun.
It's to keep the slow and the difficult
from disappearing beneath it.

<!-- S58 -->
There's a stranger way
to look at all this.
The universe is thirteen point eight
billion years old,
and our galaxy alone has
hundreds of billions of stars.

<!-- S59 -->
Yet we've found no sign of anyone else.
That's the Fermi paradox.
Where is everybody?

<!-- S60 -->
Maybe civilisations destroy themselves.
Maybe the distances are too great.
Maybe life is just rare.

<!-- S61 -->
Or maybe they invent smartphones.
And recommendation algorithms.
And spend the rest of their existence
watching increasingly ridiculous videos.

<!-- S62 -->
Their spaceships stay unfinished.
Their telescopes gather dust.

<!-- S63 -->
And somewhere, around a distant star,
an entire civilisation is watching
someone drop a thousand rubber ducks
into a swimming pool.

<!-- S64 -->
There's no evidence that this
explains the silence of the universe.
But the unsettling part is that
the algorithm wouldn't have to be evil.

<!-- S65 -->
It wouldn't need to hate science.
It would only need to be very good
at doing what it was built to do.

<!-- S66 -->
We built the greatest
knowledge-sharing machine in history.

<!-- S67 -->
Now we have to decide whether to use it
to understand the universe...

<!-- S68 -->
or simply to pass the time
until the next video begins.
