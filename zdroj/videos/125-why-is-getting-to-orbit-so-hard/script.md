<!-- S01 -->
In July 1969, the rocket that carried
the crew of the first Moon landing
weighed about two thousand
nine hundred tonnes on the launch pad.

<!-- S02 -->
Eight days later, what came back to Earth
was a capsule with three people inside.
It weighed just under five tonnes.

<!-- S03 -->
About one part in six hundred
of that rocket came home.
So where did the rest go?

<!-- S04 -->
Most of it was propellant:
fuel, and the oxygen to burn it.
More than two thousand tonnes of it
burnt in under three minutes.

<!-- S05 -->
And here's the strange part.
Much of it wasn't there
to lift the spacecraft.
It was there to lift more propellant.
To keep things simple,
we'll call all of it fuel from here on.

<!-- S06 -->
Imagine you want a rocket to go faster.
The obvious answer is more fuel.

<!-- S07 -->
But fuel has mass.
Before the rocket can burn it,
it has to carry it,
and speed it up along with everything else.

<!-- S08 -->
So the extra fuel needs fuel of its own.
And that fuel needs fuel too.

<!-- S09 -->
At some point, adding fuel
stops solving the problem...
and starts becoming the problem.

<!-- S10 -->
This problem has an equation,
and it's more than a century old.

<!-- S11 -->
In 1903, a schoolteacher
from the Russian town of Kaluga,
Konstantin Tsiolkovsky,
published the formula
that every rocket still has to obey.

<!-- S12 -->
He wasn't the first to do the maths.
In 1813, a British mathematician,
William Moore, had worked out
how a rocket speeds up as it loses mass,
for military rockets.
Tsiolkovsky used it
to work out how we might reach space.

<!-- S13 -->
Here it is.

<!-- S13b -->
On the left is delta-v:
the change in speed the rocket can achieve.

<!-- S14 -->
On the right, first, the exhaust speed.
A rocket has nothing to push against.
It moves by throwing mass backwards,
and the faster it throws it,
the more momentum each kilogram carries off,
and the more the rocket gets back.

<!-- S15 -->
Then the ratio of two masses:
the rocket full of fuel at the start,
and what's left when the fuel is gone.
Engines, empty tanks, structure...
and the payload you actually wanted to send.

<!-- S16 -->
And wrapped around that ratio,
a logarithm.
That's the part that makes it so hard.

<!-- S17 -->
Take two rockets with the same engine.
The first starts four times heavier
than it will be with its tanks empty.
The second, eight times heavier.

<!-- S18 -->
Twice the mass ratio.
You'd expect twice the speed.
It gets about fifty percent more.

<!-- S19 -->
Double it again, from eight to sixteen,
and you get only about a third more.
Every extra tonne of fuel buys less speed
than the tonne before it.

<!-- S20 -->
Turn that around, and it looks worse.
Each extra kilometre per second
doesn't add to the rocket's starting mass.
It multiplies it.

<!-- S21 -->
So how much speed does orbit take?
In low Earth orbit, a spacecraft travels
at about seven point eight kilometres
per second, sideways.

<!-- S22 -->
On the way up, the rocket also fights
gravity and the air.
Altogether, it needs around
nine point four kilometres per second.

<!-- S23 -->
Now give it the best chemical engine,
burning hydrogen and oxygen.
And let's be generous:
assume it performs as it would in a vacuum,
with exhaust at four and a half
kilometres per second.

<!-- S24 -->
Put those numbers into the equation,
and the rocket has to start
about eight times heavier than it ends.

<!-- S25 -->
A hundred tonnes on the launch pad...
and only about twelve tonnes
allowed to reach orbit.

<!-- S26 -->
And those twelve tonnes aren't payload.
They're the engines, the empty tanks,
the whole structure that held the fuel.
Whatever's left after that
is the satellite.

<!-- S27 -->
In real rockets, that leftover is small.
A Falcon 9 weighs about
five hundred and fifty tonnes at launch.
Its maximum payload to low orbit
is around twenty-three tonnes.
Up to about four percent.

<!-- S28 -->
So rockets that reach orbit
shed stages or other hardware
on the way up.

<!-- S29 -->
That's where the rest of the Saturn V went.

<!-- S30 -->
Its first stage burned
for less than three minutes,
then fell away into the Atlantic.
Five engines and enormous empty tanks...
that the rest of the flight
no longer had to carry.

<!-- S31 -->
The second stage took it almost to orbit,
and fell away too.
The third finished the job,
and later pushed the spacecraft
towards the Moon.

<!-- S32 -->
Each stage does part of the work,
and then stops being dead weight.
The stages above it never have to carry
its empty tanks and engines.

<!-- S33 -->
That's why so many rockets look like
several rockets stacked on top of each other.
They are.

<!-- S34 -->
But staging doesn't beat the rocket equation.
Every stage obeys it on its own.
It just stops us paying
for the same empty tank
over and over again.

<!-- S35 -->
Apollo kept doing it all the way.
The lander left its lower half on the Moon.
Its upper half brought the astronauts back up,
and was cast off in lunar orbit.

<!-- S36 -->
The service module was dropped
just before re-entry.
What came home was the one piece
that had to.

<!-- S37 -->
So why not just build a better engine?
Remember the exhaust speed in the equation.
The faster the exhaust,
the less fuel you need.

<!-- S38 -->
Chemical rockets can't go much faster.
Their exhaust speed is limited
by the energy released in chemical reactions.

<!-- S39 -->
Ion engines throw their propellant out
several times faster.
NASA's Dawn probe used them
to change its own speed
by more than eleven kilometres per second.

<!-- S40 -->
But their push was tiny.
About the weight of a sheet of paper
resting on your hand.

<!-- S41 -->
At full power, Dawn would have needed
four days to reach sixty miles an hour.
An engine like that
can't lift anything off the ground.

<!-- S42 -->
To leave the ground, you need brute force.
To travel efficiently, you need fast exhaust.
No engine we've ever flown
is good at both.

<!-- S43 -->
Which leaves one more question.
We've been treating that
nine point four kilometres per second
as a fixed price.
But the price is set by Earth.

<!-- S44 -->
A heavier planet pulls harder.
Escaping it takes more speed...
and in the rocket equation,
more speed doesn't add to the bill.
It multiplies it.

<!-- S45 -->
In 2018, the German researcher Michael Hippke
did the calculation
for a rocky planet ten times Earth's mass,
and about one point seven times as wide.

<!-- S46 -->
To escape Earth,
a rocket needs about eleven kilometres
per second.
There, it would need about twenty-seven.

<!-- S47 -->
In his simplified single-stage rocket,
the mass ratio jumps from about twenty-six
to about two thousand seven hundred.

<!-- S48 -->
A mission like Apollo, he estimated,
would need a rocket of around
four hundred thousand tonnes.
More than a hundred Saturn Vs.

<!-- S49 -->
His calculation suggests that chemical rockets
could still escape a planet like this.
But on heavier rocky worlds,
a chemical rocket's fuel needs
could become prohibitive.

<!-- S49b -->
It works the other way, too.
Mars has about a tenth of Earth's mass.
To escape it, a rocket needs
only about five kilometres per second.

<!-- S49c -->
In the same simplified calculation,
the mass ratio drops from about twenty-six
to about four.

<!-- S50 -->
The rocket equation never forbids anything.
It only names the price.

<!-- S51 -->
On Earth, that price is a rocket
that throws away almost all of itself
to bring one small capsule home.
On a heavier world, it might be a price
nobody would ever choose to pay.

<!-- S52 -->
So here's a question for you.
Do you think intelligent life
on a much heavier planet
could ever reach space?
Let me know in the comments.
