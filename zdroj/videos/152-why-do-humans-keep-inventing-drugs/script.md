<!-- S01 -->
More than twelve thousand years ago,
in what is now Utah,
someone sat by a fire
and left behind a few tobacco seeds.

<!-- S02 -->
About eight thousand years ago,
in the Andes,
people were chewing coca leaves
with a pinch of powdered lime.

<!-- S03 -->
And about nine thousand years ago,
in a village in China,
people were brewing a drink
from rice, honey and fruit.

<!-- S04 -->
These people never met.
They didn't share a language,
a religion, or even a continent.

<!-- S05 -->
And yet each of them found a plant
that changes how a human brain works.
And kept coming back to it.

<!-- S06 -->
Nobody knows when humans first did this.
And these are just three examples.
Opium in the Bronze Age Mediterranean.
Cannabis in the mountains of Central Asia.

<!-- S07 -->
So why?
Why does every human society,
sooner or later,
find a way to change its own mind?

<!-- S08 -->
And there's a stranger question
hiding underneath.
Why does a plant contain a molecule
that fits a human brain at all?

<!-- S09 -->
A coffee bush doesn't care
whether you're awake.
A tobacco plant has never met a smoker.

<!-- S10 -->
The answer is that these molecules
weren't made for us.
They're weapons.

<!-- S11 -->
Plants can't run away
from the animals that eat them.
So many of them fight with chemistry.

<!-- S12 -->
In 1984, a researcher in Boston
mixed ground coffee and tea leaves
into the food of caterpillars.

<!-- S13 -->
They ate less, trembled,
and stopped growing.
At higher doses,
they were dead within a day.

<!-- S14 -->
Nicotine is so good at this
that farmers sprayed tobacco water
on their crops for centuries.
And some of today's most widely used
insecticides are modelled on it.

<!-- S15 -->
Even cocaine seems to protect
the coca plant.
At the levels found in its leaves,
it scrambles the nerves
of the insects that chew them.

<!-- S16 -->
And here's why the weapon
works on us too.
An insect's nervous system and yours
run on many of the same chemicals.

<!-- S17 -->
Dopamine. Serotonin. Acetylcholine.
Insects use them too.
Some of these signalling systems
have been around
for hundreds of millions of years.

<!-- S18 -->
So when a plant evolves a molecule
that jams an insect's nerves,
it often jams ours as well.

<!-- S19 -->
The same molecule can have
very different effects
in different animals.
Dose matters. So does biology.

<!-- S20 -->
The caffeine in your coffee
is the same defensive chemical
that helps protect the plant.
Just in a very different dose.

<!-- S21 -->
And plants don't only use
these chemicals as weapons.

<!-- S22 -->
Coffee and citrus flowers
put a little caffeine in their nectar.
Not enough to harm a bee.
Just enough to change it.

<!-- S23 -->
In 2013, researchers found
that bees given caffeine
were three times as likely
to remember a flower's scent
a day later.

<!-- S24 -->
Plants were already using chemistry
to influence their visitors
long before we came along.

<!-- S25 -->
But that only explains
why these molecules work.
It doesn't explain why we want them.

<!-- S26 -->
A poison should taste like a warning.
So why would a brain
go looking for it?

<!-- S27 -->
To answer that,
scientists had to look inside the brain.
And for a long time, the best map they had
was the drugs themselves.

<!-- S28 -->
In 1973, three laboratories,
working independently,
found receptors in the brain
that morphine locks onto.

<!-- S29 -->
Which raised an obvious question.
Why would a human brain
have a lock for a poppy?

<!-- S30 -->
Two years later,
researchers in Aberdeen found the key.
The brain makes its own opioids.

<!-- S31 -->
We now call them endorphins.
The name means "the morphine within".

<!-- S32 -->
Cannabis led to the same discovery.
First a receptor its active compound fits,
then, in 1992, the brain's own version.

<!-- S33 -->
Its discoverers named it anandamide,
after the Sanskrit word for bliss.

<!-- S34 -->
And nicotine gave its name
to a whole family of receptors,
in your muscles as well as your brain.

<!-- S35 -->
They don't need to invent
a new system inside your brain.
They hijack systems
that were already there.

<!-- S36 -->
Caffeine blocks a signal
that builds up the longer you're awake,
the one that makes you feel tired.

<!-- S37 -->
Nicotine imitates a messenger
the brain uses for attention.

<!-- S38 -->
And cocaine stops the brain
from clearing away dopamine,
so it keeps working
for longer than it should.

<!-- S39 -->
Dopamine is often called
the pleasure chemical.
It isn't, really.

<!-- S40 -->
It's closer to a teacher.
Among other things,
it helps the brain learn
what's worth doing again.

<!-- S41 -->
So a substance that pushes on it
doesn't just feel good.
It can teach the brain
to treat it as something worth repeating.

<!-- S42 -->
Then why didn't evolution
protect us from that?

<!-- S43 -->
With one substance,
it seems to have done the opposite.

<!-- S44 -->
About ten million years ago,
our ape ancestors picked up
a single mutation
in a gene that breaks down alcohol.

<!-- S45 -->
It made them about forty times better
at breaking it down.

<!-- S46 -->
One explanation is fruit.
Around that time, our ancestors
were spending more time on the ground,
where ripe fruit falls and ferments.

<!-- S47 -->
Our ancestors may have gained
an advantage from eating fermented fruit,
millions of years
before anyone brewed a drop.

<!-- S48 -->
And not everyone who tries a drug
gets caught by it.

<!-- S49 -->
In the early 1990s,
a large American survey estimated
how many people who had tried a substance
went on to become dependent on it.

<!-- S50 -->
For cannabis, about nine per cent.
For alcohol, about fifteen.
For heroin, twenty-three.

<!-- S51 -->
And the highest of all
wasn't heroin or cocaine.
It was tobacco.
Nearly a third.

<!-- S52 -->
So the risk depends on the substance,
and on the person.
Genes, mental health, stress,
and how often it's used
all change the odds.

<!-- S53 -->
And chemistry still isn't
the whole story.

<!-- S54 -->
The most widely used drug on Earth
is caffeine.
And hardly anyone drinks coffee
for the thrill.

<!-- S55 -->
They drink it to get through a morning.
To stay awake on a night shift.
To feel normal.

<!-- S56 -->
That's what a lot of drug use is.
Not chasing a high,
but getting away from something.
Pain. Fear. Exhaustion.
A memory you'd rather not have.

<!-- S57 -->
Some cultures used psychedelic plants
in ceremonies,
where an altered state
meant contact with something sacred.

<!-- S58 -->
Others used alcohol to mark a wedding,
or to turn strangers into friends.

<!-- S59 -->
And which of these substances
a society accepts
isn't decided by chemistry at all.

<!-- S60 -->
By the fifteenth century,
coffee was spreading
through Sufi communities in Yemen,
where it kept people awake
for night prayers.

<!-- S61 -->
In 1511, the governor of Mecca
banned it.
The ban didn't last.

<!-- S62 -->
In 1675, King Charles the Second
tried to shut down
every coffee house in England.
He backed down
before the order even took effect.

<!-- S63 -->
And in the seventeenth century,
the Pope threatened to excommunicate
anyone who used tobacco
in the churches of Seville.

<!-- S64 -->
Today, coffee is breakfast.
And tobacco,
which, in that survey,
hooked more of its users than heroin,
is sold at the petrol station.

<!-- S65 -->
For most of history,
all of this came straight from plants.
You chewed the leaf.
You drank the brew.
You smoked the dried flower.

<!-- S66 -->
Then, in the early 1800s,
a young German pharmacist
pulled a single compound out of opium.

<!-- S67 -->
He named it after Morpheus,
the god of dreams.
Morphine.

<!-- S68 -->
For the first time,
one of opium's active compounds
could be isolated,
purified and measured.

<!-- S69 -->
That changed medicine.
And it changed the risk.

<!-- S70 -->
Around 1860, a German chemist
did the same with coca.
A coca leaf is typically
less than one per cent cocaine.
Purified, it's a hundred times
more concentrated.

<!-- S71 -->
And in August 1897,
a chemist at Bayer
prepared two compounds,
eleven days apart.

<!-- S72 -->
One became aspirin.
The other became heroin.

<!-- S72b -->
Heroin had actually been made
more than twenty years earlier.
But Bayer turned it into a medicine,
and sold it for coughs.

<!-- S73 -->
And increasingly,
chemists went beyond extraction.
They began modifying molecules,
and creating compounds
no plant had ever made.

<!-- S74 -->
Some became essential medicines.
Modern anaesthetics. Antidepressants.
Painkillers far stronger than morphine.

<!-- S75 -->
Being new doesn't make a molecule
more dangerous.
What matters is what it does,
how much of it you take,
and how.

<!-- S76 -->
But it did open a loophole.
Laws usually ban specific molecules.
Change one small piece of a banned one,
and you may get something similar
that, legally, doesn't exist yet.

<!-- S77 -->
At the end of 2024,
the European Union's drugs agency
was monitoring more than a thousand
new psychoactive substances.
Forty-seven of them were reported
for the first time that year.

<!-- S78 -->
When one is banned,
another can appear.
Faster than anyone can study
what it does.

<!-- S79 -->
And now the question is turning around.
It's no longer just
what nature happens to contain.
It's what we want our brains to do.

<!-- S80 -->
Since 2023, specially authorised
psychiatrists in Australia
have been allowed to prescribe psilocybin,
the compound in magic mushrooms,
for depression that nothing else helped.

<!-- S81 -->
Millions of people take pills
to sleep, to focus,
or to feel less afraid.

<!-- S82 -->
The same molecule can be a medicine
when it's used to treat a patient,
or an illegal drug
when it's possessed or sold unlawfully.

<!-- S83 -->
The line between a drug, a medicine
and a morning coffee
isn't written in the molecule.
We draw it.
And we keep moving it.

<!-- S84 -->
So, why do humans
keep inventing drugs?

<!-- S85 -->
Partly, we don't.
The first ones were invented by plants,
as weapons against insects.

<!-- S86 -->
They work on us
because our brains are built
from the same ancient parts.

<!-- S87 -->
And they pull us back
because they push on the system
that decides what's worth repeating.

<!-- S88 -->
Everything else,
the coffee houses, the ceremonies,
the laboratories,
is what a curious species does
once it notices that.

<!-- S89 -->
We didn't just invent drugs.
We found out that the world is full
of chemicals that can change
what it feels like to be human.

<!-- S90 -->
And once you've found that out,
it's very hard to stop looking.

<!-- S91 -->
So here's a question for you.
If a pill could make you sharper,
calmer or braver,
with no side effects at all,
would that still be a drug?
Let me know in the comments.
