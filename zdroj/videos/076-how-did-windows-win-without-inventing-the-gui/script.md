# How Did Windows Win Without Inventing the GUI?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Windows. Icons. Menus. A mouse.

For most people,
that is simply what a computer looks like.

And most of them met it through Microsoft.

<!-- S02 -->
But when the first Windows appeared,
in 1985,
almost none of that was new.

Other companies had already built it.

<!-- S03 -->
So here is the real question.

If Microsoft didn't invent
the graphical interface,
how did Windows end up
on most of the world's desks?

<!-- S04 -->
To answer that,
we have to look at everyone
who got there first...

and ask why they didn't win.

<!-- S06 -->
In 1968, in San Francisco,
a researcher called Douglas Engelbart
sat on a stage in front of
about a thousand people.

<!-- S07 -->
For ninety minutes,
he showed them a computer
controlled with a small wooden box
on wheels.

A mouse.

<!-- S08 -->
Text on his screen linked to other text.

And a colleague thirty miles away
was editing the same document with him.

<!-- S10 -->
Engelbart showed where computing could go.

But it was a research system,
on one large, expensive computer.

Nobody could buy it.

<!-- S12 -->
Five years later,
a few people came much closer.

<!-- S13 -->
At Xerox PARC,
a research centre in California,
a team built a machine called the Alto.

The first ones ran in 1973.

<!-- S14 -->
The Alto had a screen
where every single dot
could be controlled separately.

So it could show different fonts.

Pictures.

And text that looked
the way it would on paper.

<!-- S15 -->
It had a mouse.

It had windows that overlapped.

It had menus that popped up
where you clicked.

<!-- S16 -->
And it wasn't a lab curiosity.

Xerox built around two thousand of them.

People used them every day,
to write, to draw,
to send each other email.

<!-- S17 -->
So by the mid-1970s,
a large part of the modern desktop
already existed.

A decade before Windows.

<!-- S17b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S18 -->
So why aren't we all
using Xerox computers?

<!-- S19 -->
The Alto itself was never sold
as a product.

But in 1981,
Xerox did release a commercial machine
built on the same ideas.

The Star.

<!-- S20 -->
The Star had a desktop on the screen.

Documents.

Folders.

A printer.

An in-tray for mail.

<!-- S21 -->
The idea that your screen
looks like an office desk
went on sale here.

<!-- S22 -->
And each Star workstation
cost more than sixteen thousand dollars.

<!-- S23 -->
That was more than many people
earned in a year.

And Xerox sold it to offices
as part of a whole networked system,
not as a single computer.

<!-- S24 -->
It was enormously influential.

It was a commercial failure.

<!-- S25 -->
Xerox had the ideas first.

It didn't have a machine
ordinary people could afford.

<!-- S26 -->
Which brings us to Apple.

<!-- S27 -->
In 1979,
Steve Jobs and a group of Apple engineers
visited Xerox PARC.

<!-- S28 -->
Xerox let them in on a deal.

It got to buy a stake in Apple
before the company went public.

<!-- S29 -->
What they saw changed
what Apple was building.

But Apple didn't simply copy it.

It hired people from PARC,
and its own engineers
spent years reworking the ideas.

<!-- S30 -->
The first result was the Lisa,
in 1983.

A graphical interface.
A mouse. Windows. Menus.

<!-- S31 -->
And a price of nearly
ten thousand dollars.

The Lisa failed too.

<!-- S32 -->
Then, in 1984,
came the Macintosh.

<!-- S33 -->
Smaller. Simpler.

About two and a half thousand dollars.

And launched with one of the most
famous adverts ever shown
during a Super Bowl.

<!-- S34 -->
This was the graphical interface
as a consumer product.

<!-- S35 -->
So if anyone was going to win,
surely it was Apple?

<!-- S36 -->
There was one catch.

If you wanted a Macintosh,
you had to buy it from Apple.

<!-- S37 -->
Apple controlled both
the hardware and the software.

<!-- S38 -->
That made the Mac polished
and consistent.

It also meant that
the Mac could only spread
as fast as Apple could sell it.

<!-- S39 -->
And to see why that mattered,
we have to leave the graphical interface
for a moment...

and go back to 1980.

<!-- S40 -->
That year,
IBM was building its first
personal computer.

And it needed an operating system.

<!-- S41 -->
It went to a small company called Microsoft.

<!-- S42 -->
Microsoft didn't have one.

So it licensed an existing system
from another Seattle company,
bought it outright the following year,
and adapted it for IBM.

<!-- S43 -->
On IBM's machines,
it was called PC DOS.

<!-- S44 -->
And here is the detail
the whole story turns on.

<!-- S45 -->
IBM's contract did not stop Microsoft
from licensing the same system
to other manufacturers.

<!-- S46 -->
Microsoft kept the right to sell it
to anyone else...

as MS-DOS.

<!-- S47 -->
At first, that didn't seem
to matter very much.

Who else would need it?

<!-- S48 -->
The IBM PC came out in 1981.

And IBM had built it
mostly from parts anyone could buy.

<!-- S49 -->
The one piece rivals couldn't legally copy
was a small program IBM had written
for a chip at the heart of the machine.

<!-- S50 -->
In 1983,
a company called Compaq
started selling a computer
that ran the same software
as an IBM PC.

<!-- S51 -->
Its engineers had recreated
that small program from scratch,
without copying IBM's code.

<!-- S52 -->
Others did the same.

Within a few years,
dozens of companies were selling
IBM-compatible PCs.

<!-- S53 -->
They all competed with each other.

On price.

On speed.

On features.

<!-- S54 -->
And almost all of them
ran Microsoft's operating system.

<!-- S55 -->
So by the time Windows arrived,
Microsoft already had something
Xerox and Apple didn't.

<!-- S56 -->
It wasn't a better interface.

It was a place on a huge number of computers
made by companies it didn't own.

<!-- S57 -->
Windows 1.0 came out in 1985.

<!-- S58 -->
And honestly...
it wasn't very good.

<!-- S59 -->
Its windows couldn't even overlap.

They were laid out side by side,
like tiles.

<!-- S60 -->
But it ran on top of DOS,
on the PCs people already had.

You didn't need a new computer.

You didn't need to throw away
your old programs.

<!-- S61 -->
Windows 2.0, in 1987,
added overlapping windows.

<!-- S62 -->
And in 1988,
Apple sued Microsoft.

<!-- S63 -->
Apple claimed Windows copied
the look and feel of the Macintosh.

<!-- S64 -->
But in 1985,
Apple had signed an agreement
that let Microsoft use parts of
the Macintosh's visual design.

<!-- S65 -->
The court found that most of what
Apple was complaining about
was covered by that licence...

or couldn't be protected at all.

<!-- S67 -->
Nobody could own the idea
of a window on a screen.

<!-- S68 -->
The case dragged on for six years.

And while it did,
the real contest
was being decided somewhere else.

<!-- S69 -->
In 1990,
Microsoft released Windows 3.0.

<!-- S70 -->
It was finally good enough.

And it could run on
the huge number of PCs
that were already out there.

<!-- S71 -->
PC makers started installing it
on new computers before they were sold.

<!-- S72 -->
And Microsoft made sure
that was the easy choice.

<!-- S73 -->
Under some of its licences,
a manufacturer paid Microsoft
for every computer it shipped
with a certain processor...

whether that computer came with
Microsoft's software or not.

<!-- S74 -->
So installing a rival system
meant paying twice.

<!-- S75 -->
In 1994,
the United States government
challenged those contracts,
and Microsoft agreed to stop.

<!-- S76 -->
But by then,
something much harder to stop
was already running.

<!-- S77 -->
Software companies wrote programs
for the platform with the most users.

<!-- S78 -->
Users bought the platform
with the most programs.

<!-- S79 -->
Every new Windows user
made Windows more worth writing for.

Every new program
made Windows more worth buying.

<!-- S80 -->
And once that loop gets going,
it is very hard for anyone else
to get in.

<!-- S81 -->
In 1995,
Microsoft released Windows 95.

<!-- S82 -->
A Start button.

A taskbar.

Long file names.

<!-- S83 -->
And a launch so loud
that people queued at midnight
to buy an operating system.

<!-- S84 -->
Microsoft said it sold seven million copies
in the first five weeks.

<!-- S86 -->
So how did Windows win
without inventing the graphical interface?

<!-- S87 -->
Not by being first.

Engelbart showed the way in 1968.

Xerox made it work in the 1970s.

Apple put it in people's homes in 1984.

<!-- S88 -->
And not by being the best.

For years,
Windows was clearly behind the Mac.

<!-- S89 -->
Windows won because Microsoft had
spent a decade getting its software
onto other people's computers.

<!-- S90 -->
When the graphical interface
finally became something everyone wanted,
Microsoft was already there.

<!-- S91 -->
Nobody invented the graphical interface
in a single moment.

It was built in steps,
by many people,
over twenty years.

<!-- S92 -->
Microsoft's contribution
wasn't the idea.

It was getting the idea
onto most of the desks on the planet.
