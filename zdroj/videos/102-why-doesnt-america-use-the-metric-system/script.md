Then the radio says

<!-- S05 -->
Lunch is weighed in pounds.

<!-- S06 -->
Meanwhile, almost every other country

<!-- S07 -->
So why doesn't America

<!-- S08 -->
Here's the strange part.
In a way, it already has.

<!-- S09 -->
Since 1959, an American inch

twenty-five point four millimetres.
Not roughly. Exactly.

<!-- S10 -->
A pound is exactly zero point
four five three five nine two three seven
kilograms.

<!-- S11 -->
And the yard has been defined

since 1893.

<!-- S12 -->
So American units aren't

They're the metric system

It's a bit like announcing

<!-- S15 -->
Let's start with a name.
People often call American units
imperial.
They aren't.

<!-- S16 -->
The colonists brought
English units with them.
Feet. Pounds. Gallons. Miles.
Then America became independent.

<!-- S17 -->
In 1824, Britain tidied up
its own units into a new system,
the imperial system.
America was no longer listening.

<!-- S18 -->
So the two drifted apart.
An American gallon is about
three point eight litres.
A British one,
about four and a half.

<!-- S19 -->
America doesn't use the imperial system.
It uses something older.

<!-- S20 -->
Meanwhile, in the 1790s,
France was building something new.

<!-- S21 -->
A system where everything
comes in tens.
A thousand metres make a kilometre.
A thousand grams make a kilogram.

<!-- S22 -->
Converting means
moving a decimal point.
Simple, elegant, boring.
Exactly what measurement should be.

<!-- S23 -->
And some Americans loved the idea.
In 1790, Thomas Jefferson proposed
a decimal system of his own.
Congress shelved it.

<!-- S24 -->
Oddly, America had already
gone decimal with its money.
A hundred cents to a dollar.
Britain only did that in 1971.

<!-- S25 -->
There's even a famous story
that pirates stopped America
going metric.

<!-- S26 -->
In 1794, a French scientist
sailing to America with
standards of length and weight
was captured by privateers.
He died in prison.

<!-- S27 -->
It's a great story.
It's just not the reason.
Nobody in Congress
was waiting for that ship.

<!-- S28 -->
In fact, metric became legal
in the United States in 1866.
And in 1875, America was one
of seventeen countries
to sign the Treaty of the Metre.

<!-- S29 -->
So America was never against metric.
It just never stopped using
what it already had.

<!-- S30 -->
Because once a unit settles in,
it stops being maths.
It becomes infrastructure.

<!-- S31 -->
A carpenter buys tools in inches.
A builder draws houses in feet.
A factory cuts parts to fit them.
A school teaches children to measure.

<!-- S32 -->
Every one of those
depends on all the others.
Changing the unit means coordinating
a change across the whole network.

<!-- S33 -->
And it gets into your head.
If you grew up with miles,
you know a mile is
about a twenty-minute walk.
It doesn't feel like a unit.
It's just a mile.

<!-- S34 -->
Tell an American their house
is a hundred and sixty-seven
square metres, and they'll say fine...
but how big is that?

<!-- S35 -->
One thousand eight hundred
square feet.
That number means something.

<!-- S36 -->
By the 1970s, most of the world
had switched.
And America finally decided to try.

<!-- S37 -->
In 1975, President Gerald Ford
signed the Metric Conversion Act.
It set up a Metric Board
to coordinate the change.

<!-- S38 -->
And there was a catch.
The change was voluntary.

<!-- S39 -->
Nobody abolished the mile.
The government essentially said,
metric is probably a good idea.
And America said...
no, thanks.

<!-- S40 -->
Some places did try.
Interstate 19 in Arizona
got its distance signs in kilometres.

<!-- S40b -->
Those signs are still there today.

<!-- S41 -->
But to many people,
it felt like being told
that their mile was obsolete.
Their gallon. Their pound.
Their Fahrenheit.

<!-- S42 -->
And that's the strongest argument
against switching.
Not that metric is worse.
It isn't.

<!-- S43 -->
It's that the old system
works well enough
for the people who use it.

<!-- S44 -->
Politicians noticed that telling voters
their units were obsolete
wasn't a winning strategy.
In 1982, the Reagan administration
shut the Metric Board down.

<!-- S45 -->
That was the end of the big plan.
But not the end of metric.

<!-- S46 -->
American scientists use kilograms.
Doctors dose in milligrams.
Soldiers measure distance in kilometres,
and call them klicks.
Fizzy drinks come in two-litre bottles.

<!-- S47 -->
Because international trade
doesn't care about tradition.
If you build cars or computers
for the global market,
metric units are hard to avoid.

<!-- S48 -->
So America runs two systems at once.
Metric where it's useful.
Traditional units where people
are attached to them.

<!-- S49 -->
And running two systems
has a price.

<!-- S50 -->
In 1999, NASA lost
the Mars Climate Orbiter.

<!-- S51 -->
Software written by the contractor
reported thruster impulses
in pound-force seconds.
NASA's navigators expected
newton seconds.

<!-- S52 -->
So every thruster firing looked
more than four times weaker
than it really was.
For months, the error piled up.
The spacecraft came in far too low,
and was lost.

<!-- S53 -->
The units were the cause.
People did notice something was off.
In nine months of flight,
nobody tracked it down.

<!-- S53b -->
The irony is that NASA
had made metric its preferred system
back in 1991.
The spacecraft's own specification
called for newton seconds.

<!-- S54 -->
A computer doesn't care
about tradition.
It won't stop and ask
which units you meant.

<!-- S55 -->
So are Americans being stupid?
No.

<!-- S56 -->
Nobody thinks in tens naturally.
We think in whatever we grew up with.

<!-- S57 -->
A French child learns
a hundred centimetres to a metre.
An American child learns
twelve inches to a foot.
Neither finds it strange.
Until they travel.

<!-- S58 -->
Imagine being told that from tomorrow,
kilometres are illegal.
Distances are now in furlongs.

<!-- S59 -->
Furlongs aren't hard to learn.
You'd still be furious,
because you'd have to relearn
everything you already know.

<!-- S60 -->
That's the real answer.
Not that the American system is better.
It's that changing an established system
has an enormous switching cost.

<!-- S61 -->
But other countries paid it.
So how did they do it?

<!-- S62 -->
At midnight on the twentieth of January
2005, every speed limit in Ireland
switched to kilometres per hour.
Nearly sixty thousand new signs,
put up in advance,
took effect at once.

<!-- S63 -->
Australia went metric in the 1970s,
industry by industry, with deadlines.
Its road signs changed in 1974.
Within about a decade,
it was essentially done.

<!-- S64 -->
And then there's Britain.

<!-- S65 -->
Britain decided to go metric in 1965.
Schools switched.
Most packaged food switched.
Then in 1980, the government
abolished its Metrication Board.

<!-- S66 -->
And Britain stopped halfway.
Petrol by the litre.
Road signs in miles.
Beer by the pint.

<!-- S67 -->
So before anyone in Britain
laughs at Americans...
British speed limits are in
miles per hour too.

<!-- S68 -->
In each of these stories,
the countries that finished
set a date and kept to it.
The ones that left it to choice
ended up with a mixture.

<!-- S69 -->
And America's one serious attempt

<!-- S69b -->
So the signs aren't the hard part.
Ireland switched over in a single night.
Australia changed a whole country
in about ten years.

<!-- S69c -->
The hard part is someone
naming the day the mile ends.
And no American president
has ever set that date.

<!-- S70 -->
So why doesn't America
use the metric system?

<!-- S71 -->
In many ways, it already does.
In its labs, its hospitals, its army,
and in the definition of every inch.

<!-- S72 -->
What it never really changed
is everyday life.
The road signs. The recipes.
The weather forecast.

<!-- S73 -->
Changing those would take a date

and forty million people keeping to it.

So the radio still says
