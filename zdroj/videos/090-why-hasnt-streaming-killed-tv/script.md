# Why Hasn't Streaming Killed TV?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine it's 2026.
You turn on the television.
It's half past seven in the evening.

<!-- S02 -->
First, a few minutes of commercials.
Then the news.
Then more commercials.
Then the weather.

<!-- S03 -->
Finally, at eight o'clock,
the film you actually wanted to see begins.
It's two hours long.

<!-- S04 -->
Except...
commercial break.
Another commercial break.
And another one.

<!-- S05 -->
A two-hour film
now finishes at quarter to eleven.
And you have to get up at six.
So somewhere around ten, you give up.
You never find out how it ends.

<!-- S06 -->
And that's not even the weirdest part.
You didn't choose the film.
You didn't choose when it started.
You can't pause it.
You can't rewind it.

<!-- S07 -->
And if you miss the beginning?
Too bad.
Wait until somebody decides
to show it again.

<!-- S07b -->
Of course, you could have it recorded.
People have done that for decades.
First with a VCR and a timer,
today with a set-top box.
Then you fast-forward through the ads.

<!-- S07c -->
But then you're no longer watching it
on the broadcaster's schedule.
You've turned it into something
you can watch the way you want.

<!-- S08 -->
The broadcast itself
is still linear television.
And honestly...
who would choose to watch like this in 2026?

<!-- S09 -->
Well...
millions of people still do.

<!-- S10 -->
In the United States, in May 2025,
streaming finally overtook
broadcast and cable combined.
44.8 percent of TV viewing
against 44.2.

<!-- S11 -->
Overtook. Barely.
After nearly twenty years of Netflix,
YouTube and everything else,
the old way of watching
still holds almost half of TV viewing.

<!-- S12 -->
So why hasn't streaming killed it?
The answer has less to do with television
than you might think.

<!-- S12b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S13 -->
Start with the basic idea.
A broadcaster has a transmitter.
The transmitter sends out a signal.
And every television in the area
can pick it up.

<!-- S14 -->
One transmission.
Millions of receivers.
The broadcaster doesn't have to know
who is watching,
or even how many.

<!-- S15 -->
It's an incredibly efficient way
to distribute information.
But it has one unfortunate consequence.
Everybody gets the same thing,
at the same time.

<!-- S16 -->
Broadcasting doesn't ask every viewer
what they want.
It asks one question:
what should everyone receive?

<!-- S17 -->
The broadcaster decides what's on,
and when it starts.
The viewer decides only one thing:
whether to watch.

<!-- S18 -->
For most of television's history,
that wasn't really a problem.
There wasn't much of an alternative.

<!-- S19 -->
Then the Internet happened.

<!-- S20 -->
Instead of
"here is what we're transmitting,
take it or leave it",
you get
"what do you want to watch?"

<!-- S21 -->
You choose the programme.
You choose when.
Pause it. Rewind it.
Pick the audio language.
Turn on subtitles.
Stop halfway and come back tomorrow.

<!-- S22 -->
And this isn't really about commercials.
It's about the direction of control.

<!-- S23 -->
Broadcasting pushes content towards you.
Streaming lets you pull it
towards yourself.

<!-- S24 -->
So for a film on a Tuesday night,
streaming simply wins.
But now change one thing.

<!-- S25 -->
Instead of a random film,
it's the World Cup final.
Ten million people want to see it.
And they all want to see it right now.

<!-- S26 -->
For a transmitter,
that's no harder than one viewer.
It sends the same signal either way.
Ten million receivers don't cost
ten million times as much.

<!-- S27 -->
That's the whole trick of broadcasting.
One to many.

<!-- S28 -->
The Internet works differently.
The network has to deliver the stream
to a huge number of viewers,
using servers, caches and network capacity
all the way to their screens.
And every extra viewer
needs a stream of their own.

<!-- S29 -->
Engineers have become
remarkably good at this.
But the underlying problem doesn't go away.

<!-- S30 -->
A huge number of people
are asking the network for the same thing
at the same moment.
And when it's happening right now,
nobody can download it in advance.

<!-- S31 -->
Sport.

<!-- S31b -->
Breaking news.

<!-- S31c -->
Election night.
Things millions of people want
at the same moment.

<!-- S32 -->
And that's exactly
what broadcasting is almost
ridiculously good at.

<!-- S33 -->
Which suggests
we've been mixing up two different things.

<!-- S34 -->
Television is a medium.
Broadcasting is a way of delivering it.

<!-- S35 -->
A television channel doesn't need
an antenna on the roof anymore.
It can come by cable.
By satellite.
Over the Internet.

<!-- S36 -->
In Europe in 2024,
more than half of pay-TV subscriptions
already came over broadband.

<!-- S37 -->
But the old-fashioned aerial
was still in use
in about two homes in five.

<!-- S38 -->
So the old way is declining.
But it's hardly gone.

<!-- S39 -->
And streaming didn't even get rid of
commercials.
Many services now offer cheaper plans
with advertising.
Apparently, even the Internet
couldn't escape that.

<!-- S40 -->
But traditional television
has one more trick.
And it sounds strange
after everything we've just said.

<!-- S41 -->
Sometimes people don't want to choose.

<!-- S42 -->
You sit down on the sofa.
You're tired.
You don't know what you want.
And you have thousands of options.

<!-- S43 -->
You scroll.
And scroll.
Twenty minutes later,
you're still choosing.

<!-- S44 -->
Traditional television is different.
You turn it on,
and something is already playing.
Somebody else has chosen for you.

<!-- S45 -->
Nobody says radio is broken
because you can't pick every song.
That's not what radio is for.

<!-- S46 -->
Linear television can work the same way.
It's not interactive.
It's not personalised.
And that's precisely the point.

<!-- S47 -->
And there's one more situation
where broadcasting is hard to beat.

<!-- S48 -->
Imagine a major Internet outage.
Or a natural disaster.
Or mobile networks so overloaded
that nothing gets through.

<!-- S49 -->
A transmitter with backup power
doesn't need every viewer to have
a fast Internet connection.
If you can receive the signal,
you can receive the information.
Even a battery radio will do.

<!-- S50 -->
That's one reason many governments
and public broadcasters
still keep the transmitters running.
Broadcasting isn't just
an old way of watching sitcoms.
It's a remarkably robust way
of reaching an entire country.

<!-- S51 -->
So, has streaming killed television?

<!-- S52 -->
For watching a film whenever you like?
Pretty much.
Why wait until eight o'clock?
Why sit through the interruptions?
Why not watch the rest tomorrow?

<!-- S53 -->
But for sending the same thing
to millions of people at once,
broadcasting suddenly looks
much less obsolete.

<!-- S54 -->
In fact, it looks almost elegant.
One transmitter.
One signal.
Millions of receivers.

<!-- S55 -->
We often assume old technology disappears
when something newer comes along.
But radio didn't disappear
because of podcasts.
Books didn't disappear
because of e-books.

<!-- S56 -->
The old technology moves
into a different role.
The one it does best.

<!-- S57 -->
So the real question isn't
whether television is dead.
It's whether we need everyone
to see the same thing at the same time.

<!-- S58 -->
For a two-hour film?
Probably not.

<!-- S59 -->
For the World Cup final?
Maybe.

<!-- S60 -->
For an emergency?
Absolutely.

<!-- S61 -->
And that's why, even in 2026,
you can still turn on a television
and find the same old thing waiting.

<!-- S62 -->
A signal.
A schedule.
A bunch of commercials.
And millions of other people
watching exactly the same thing.

<!-- S63 -->
Not because we haven't invented
anything better.
But because sometimes,
sending one thing to everyone
is still very hard to beat.
