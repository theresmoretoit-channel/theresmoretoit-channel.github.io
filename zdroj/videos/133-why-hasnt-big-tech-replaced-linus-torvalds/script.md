<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you run one of the biggest
technology companies on Earth.

<!-- S02 -->
Thousands of engineers.
Billions of dollars.

<!-- S03 -->
And one of the most important pieces
of your infrastructure
is controlled by a man
who doesn't work for you.

<!-- S04 -->
You can't fire him.
You can't overrule him.

<!-- S05 -->
When your engineers want to change
that software, they send him their work,
and wait for his answer.

<!-- S06 -->
That man is Linus Torvalds.
And the software is Linux.

<!-- S07 -->
Google does this.
So do Microsoft, Intel, Amazon and Meta.

<!-- S08 -->
So why hasn't Big Tech replaced him?

<!-- S09 -->
Linux began as a student's hobby
in Helsinki in 1991.
Today, it runs Android phones,
cloud servers, and all five hundred
of the world's fastest supercomputers.

<!-- S10 -->
The latest release brought
almost nineteen thousand changes,
from about three thousand developers
and more than two hundred companies.

<!-- S11 -->
So how many of those changes
did Linus write himself?

<!-- S12 -->
Nine.

<!-- S13 -->
And eight of them
just changed the version number.

<!-- S14 -->
In two weeks, he merged
around two hundred and forty batches
of changes, each one sent to him
by someone he trusts.

<!-- S15 -->
He's in charge
because the code ends up with him.

<!-- S16 -->
And that's exactly the part
a company like Google
could simply walk away from.

<!-- S17 -->
Linux is open source.
Anyone can take the code
and make their own version.

<!-- S18 -->
It's called a fork,
and you don't need Linus's permission.

<!-- S19 -->
Google did exactly that.

<!-- S20 -->
Not a whole new Linux.
It took the kernel,
the core that talks to the hardware,
and kept its own branch of it
for Android.

<!-- S21 -->
In December 2009, Android's code
was removed from the main kernel.

<!-- S22 -->
Greg Kroah-Hartman,
one of the senior maintainers,
explained why:
no one cared about the code,
so it was removed.

<!-- S23 -->
So Android's kernel drifted away.

<!-- S24 -->
Chip makers and phone makers
added their own changes on top.

<!-- S25 -->
By Google's own account,
up to half the code
in a phone's kernel
could come from outside the main kernel.

<!-- S26 -->
And every security fix
found in Linux
had to be carried over
into each of those versions.

<!-- S27 -->
Google's documentation calls that
"prohibitively expensive".

<!-- S28 -->
A new kernel release could take
up to eighteen months
to reach a phone.

<!-- S29 -->
Two years after deleting
Android's code,
Greg put it back.

<!-- S30 -->
His note said:
"Turns out I was wrong,
we want these in the tree."

<!-- S31 -->
And since Android 12,
phones that launch on a newer kernel
must use one common kernel from Google,
the Generic Kernel Image.

<!-- S32 -->
It's still Linux
with Android's own changes on top.
But it stays close to the main version,
because that's where the fixes are.

<!-- S33 -->
Making your own version is easy.
Keeping it alive is the expensive part.

<!-- S34 -->
That's why companies send their code
to the main kernel.

<!-- S35 -->
Not as a favour.
Once it's in,
everyone else helps look after it.

<!-- S36 -->
But Linus can't read
nineteen thousand changes.
Nobody could.

<!-- S37 -->
Linux is split into areas.
Networking. Memory. File systems.
Graphics drivers. Each processor family.

<!-- S38 -->
Each area has its own maintainers.
The list of them has around
three and a half thousand entries.

<!-- S39 -->
A change goes first
to the right maintainer.

<!-- S40 -->
They review it, argue about it,
ask for fixes,
and collect what's ready.

<!-- S41 -->
Then they send it up.

<!-- S42 -->
At the top,
more than a hundred of them
send their collections to Linus.

<!-- S43 -->
Linux is built everywhere.
But it comes together in one place.

<!-- S44 -->
Now, the companies
sending all that code
aren't charities.

<!-- S45 -->
These companies compete
with each other every day.

<!-- S46 -->
Would Google let Microsoft decide
what goes into the software
its servers run on?

<!-- S47 -->
Linus doesn't work for any of them.

<!-- S48 -->
So who does pay him?

<!-- S49 -->
The Linux Foundation.

<!-- S50 -->
And the Foundation isn't a charity
standing outside the corporate world.

<!-- S51 -->
It's an association of companies.

<!-- S52 -->
Google, Microsoft, Intel,
Meta, Amazon and others
pay for its top membership,
and each of them
gets a seat on its board.

<!-- S53 -->
The Foundation says
it pays Linus
so that he can stay independent.

<!-- S54 -->
That's a stated purpose,
not a published guarantee.

<!-- S55 -->
The Foundation hosts
more than a thousand projects.
The kernel gets about three percent
of its budget.

<!-- S56 -->
So the companies aren't putting
one of their own at the top.

<!-- S57 -->
They're supporting a place
where they can work together,
without any one of them in charge.

<!-- S58 -->
But does Linus really
have the final word?

<!-- S59 -->
Here's what that looks like.

<!-- S60 -->
In 2024, Linux got
a new file system called bcachefs,
a new way of storing files on a disk.

<!-- S61 -->
Every new version has a rule.
New features come in
during the first two weeks.
After that, only fixes.

<!-- S62 -->
Its developer kept sending
large changes after those two weeks.

<!-- S63 -->
In August 2024, Linus wrote:

<!-- S64 -->
"At some point,
'fix something' just turns into development.
And this is that point."

<!-- S65 -->
The arguments went on for another year.

<!-- S66 -->
In 2025, Linus took bcachefs
out of the kernel.

<!-- S67 -->
It still exists,
as a separate add-on.
Just not in the shared version.

<!-- S68 -->
Linus has power
because the people who send him code
keep trusting him.

<!-- S69 -->
So what happens when Linus is gone?

<!-- S70 -->
We already know a little.

<!-- S71 -->
In September 2018, Linus stepped away.

<!-- S72 -->
He apologised for attacking people
in his emails,
and said he needed time
to change how he behaved.

<!-- S73 -->
Greg Kroah-Hartman,
the same maintainer
from the Android story,
took over
and finished the next version, 4.19.

<!-- S74 -->
Linux kept going.
A few weeks later, Linus came back.

<!-- S75 -->
Since 2026, there's even a written plan.

<!-- S76 -->
If Linus can't continue,
the senior maintainers start talking
within seventy-two hours
about who takes over.

<!-- S77 -->
It doesn't name a successor.
It describes how to find one.

<!-- S78 -->
So Linux can survive without Linus.

<!-- S79 -->
What it can't easily replace
is the trust that put him
at the centre in the first place.

<!-- S80 -->
So why hasn't Big Tech
replaced Linus Torvalds?

<!-- S81 -->
Because what they'd be replacing
isn't a programmer.

<!-- S82 -->
It's the one place
where all their competing code
comes together,
looked after by someone
none of them owns.

<!-- S83 -->
Linus doesn't own the kingdom.
Everyone just agrees that, for now,
he's the right person to guard it.

<!-- S84 -->
So here's a question for you.
When Linus does step down,
should one person
get the final word again,
or should it be a group?
