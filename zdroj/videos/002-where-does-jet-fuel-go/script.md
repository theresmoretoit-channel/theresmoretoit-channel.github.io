# Where Does the Fuel Go When a Plane Dumps It?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Every so often, the same headline appears.

A plane took off, something went wrong,
and it had to come straight back.

<!-- S02 -->
But first, it dumped twenty tonnes of fuel.

Twenty thousand kilograms of kerosene,
released deliberately into the sky.

<!-- S03 -->
Which leaves two questions.

Where does it actually go?

And if the problem is too much fuel,
why not simply burn it?

<!-- S04 -->
Picture it.

A large airliner has just lifted off.

A few minutes into the climb,
something goes wrong.

<!-- S05 -->
An engine problem.
A gear that will not retract.

The crew turns back.

Which sounds simple. Turn around, land.

<!-- S06 -->
Except the aircraft took off eight minutes ago,
so the tanks are still almost full.

And here the numbers stop being comfortable.

<!-- S07 -->
A seven four seven may take off
at three hundred and ninety-seven tonnes.

It may land at two hundred and eighty-six.

<!-- S08 -->
That gap is over a hundred tonnes.

A hundred tonnes of aircraft that is legal in the air,
and not legal on the runway.

<!-- S09 -->
On a normal flight this never comes up.

The aircraft burns fuel for ten hours
and gets lighter on its own.

<!-- S10 -->
But turn back fifteen minutes after departure,
and none of that has happened.

It is still carrying nearly everything it left with.

<!-- S11 -->
Which invites an obvious objection.

Why not build the thing stronger?

Heavier undercarriage, stiffer wings.
Then it could land at any weight.

<!-- S12 -->
Engineers could.

But a stronger structure means more material.

More material means more mass.

More mass means more fuel to carry it.

<!-- S13 -->
And every kilogram of it flies on every flight
the aircraft ever makes, for decades,

to be useful on the rare day it turns back.

<!-- S14 -->
Which does not mean it cannot land heavy.

An overweight landing is a certified procedure.

If the aircraft needs to be down now, it lands.

<!-- S15 -->
But if there is time,
it is better to arrive at a sensible weight.

And the heaviest thing an aircraft
can throw away is fuel.

<!-- S16 -->
So where do twenty thousand kilograms of kerosene go?

Not, as it turns out, into anybody's garden.

<!-- S17 -->
Look at where the fuel leaves.

Not a hatch in the belly.
Not a pipe near the engines.

The nozzles sit out near the wingtips.

<!-- S18 -->
As far from everything hot
as the airframe allows.

Remember that. It becomes important later.

<!-- S19 -->
And they are not simply holes.

They atomise, spraying fuel into a slipstream
moving at hundreds of kilometres an hour.

What leaves is a mist.

<!-- S20 -->
Then there is altitude.

Fuel is normally jettisoned
no lower than about eighteen hundred metres.

And that number is not arbitrary.

<!-- S21 -->
Modelling by the American toxic substances agency
found that above roughly fifteen hundred metres,

more than ninety-eight percent evaporates
before reaching the ground.

<!-- S22 -->
Ground sampling under real dumps found no liquid fuel.

The kerosene does not vanish.
It stays in the atmosphere.

But it does not fall as rain.

<!-- S23 -->
Which handles the first question.

Now the second.

The aircraft has four engines
whose entire purpose is destroying fuel.

So why not just burn it?

<!-- S24 -->
Because engines burn fuel
at the rate engines burn fuel.

A seven four seven in cruise
gets through ten to eleven tonnes an hour.

<!-- S25 -->
So to burn off a hundred tonnes
you would fly for about ten hours.

And you cannot instruct four jet engines
to eat five times faster.

<!-- S26 -->
Which is where I had
what felt like a very good idea.

We already spray fuel out of the wings.

So set fire to it.

<!-- S27 -->
Fuel streaming from the wingtips,
and behind the aircraft, an enormous flame.

Or, to use the technical term,
a flying flamethrower.

<!-- S28 -->
So why does nobody do this?

Here is the part I did not expect.

Somebody did.

<!-- S29 -->
The F one eleven was a swing-wing bomber,

and its fuel dump outlet happened to sit
between the two engines.

<!-- S30 -->
Which meant that if you dumped fuel
and lit the afterburners at once,

the fuel ignited.

A flame dozens of metres long.

<!-- S31 -->
The Australian air force turned it
into a signature airshow display.

It had no operational purpose.
It was never a weight reduction system.

<!-- S32 -->
It was an accident of where somebody put a pipe.

And nobody else ever took it up.

<!-- S33 -->
That flame existed because a fuel outlet
sat next to an afterburner.

On an airliner the nozzles are at the wingtips,
deliberately, so that never happens.

<!-- S34 -->
The choice that makes dumping safe
is the same one that makes burning impossible.

And nobody wants to hear:
we have activated the emergency flamethrower.

<!-- S35 -->
So the fuel stays unlit.
But we still need the weight gone.

What else is on board?

Luggage. Two hundred passengers,
twenty kilograms each.

<!-- S36 -->
Four tonnes.

Against a hundred tonne problem,
that is a rounding error.

We would open the cargo doors in flight
to solve four percent of it.

<!-- S37 -->
So, not the suitcases.

Which leaves one remaining source of mass.

The passengers.

Obviously, we are not going to throw people
out of an aircraft.

<!-- S38 -->
This is a civilised society. We have parachutes.

The computer decides the aircraft
is twelve tonnes too heavy,

and assumes eighty kilograms per passenger.

<!-- S39 -->
A message appears on the captain's display.

Recommendation: remove
one hundred and fifty passengers.

Selected at random, of course. For fairness.

<!-- S40 -->
Congratulations. You have been enrolled
in the Emergency Weight Reduction Programme.

Good afternoon. I understand
most of you were expecting Frankfurt.

<!-- S41 -->
Instead, a parachute course.
It lasts four minutes.

This is a parachute. This is the cord.

If something goes wrong, use the other parachute.

<!-- S41b -->
And then the cabin door opens.

Good luck.

<!-- S42 -->
So the fuel goes overboard instead.

Except. And this is the part
almost nobody mentions.

Most airliners cannot dump fuel at all.

<!-- S43 -->
The seven three seven cannot.
The A three twenty cannot.

Neither can the seven five seven, the A two twenty,
or essentially every regional jet.

<!-- S44 -->
They have no jettison system whatsoever.

And that is not an oversight.

It is written into the certification rules.

<!-- S45 -->
An aircraft needs a jettison system
only if it cannot meet its climb requirements

after a fifteen minute flight at maximum weight.

<!-- S46 -->
Narrowbodies can.

They are light enough, and their engines have
enough margin, to climb away safely even when full.

So nobody fits one.

<!-- S47 -->
Which means that for the aircraft
most of us actually fly on,

the answer to where the dumped fuel goes
is that there is no dumped fuel.

<!-- S48 -->
A seven three seven that turns back early
circles until it has burned enough off.

Or it lands overweight,
and engineers inspect it afterwards.

<!-- S49 -->
Fuel dumping is not the normal solution.

It is the option available to the largest aircraft,
where the weight gap is simply too big.

<!-- S50 -->
So the next time that headline appears, you will know.

The aircraft was too heavy to land,
and big enough to have a way out.

<!-- S51 -->
And kerosene, unlike passengers,
does not need a parachute.

Does not need a four minute course.

And does not, on landing, request a refund.
