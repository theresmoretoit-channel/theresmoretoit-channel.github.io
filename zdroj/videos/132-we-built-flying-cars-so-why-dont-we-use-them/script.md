<!-- S01 -->
This car has a certificate
that says it's allowed to fly.

<!-- S02 -->
Slovak aviation authorities issued it
in 2022, after seventy hours in the air
and more than two hundred take-offs.

<!-- S03 -->
And it's not the first.
In 1956, the American government
certified a car that could fly.

<!-- S04 -->
Six were built.
Not enough people wanted one,
and it never went into production.

<!-- S05 -->
We were promised flying cars.
The strange thing is,
we built them. Again and again.

<!-- S06 -->
So why doesn't anyone use them?

<!-- S07 -->
Building a flying car
was never the hard part.

<!-- S08 -->
The hard part starts
the moment you have more than one.

<!-- S09 -->
But let's start with one.

<!-- S10 -->
A car and a plane
want almost opposite things.

<!-- S11 -->
A car has to be strong and heavy enough
to survive a crash.
A plane has to be as light as possible.

<!-- S12 -->
A car has to fit in a lane.
A plane needs wings
wider than the road.

<!-- S13 -->
So every flying car is a compromise.
The wings fold, or come off,
or get left behind.

<!-- S14 -->
And it still needs a runway.
The Dutch flying car that's road-legal
needs a hundred and eighty metres of it
just to take off.

<!-- S15 -->
So to fly to work,
you'd first drive to an airfield.

<!-- S16 -->
In 1949, the aircraft maker William Piper
put it in one sentence.

<!-- S17 -->
The roadable plane, he said,
is neither a good plane
nor a good automobile.

<!-- S18 -->
Two years earlier, an American company
had tried to get around that.

<!-- S19 -->
Its flying car came in two halves.
You'd buy the car,
and rent the wing at the airport.

<!-- S20 -->
It flew for the first time
in November 1947.

<!-- S21 -->
Three days later,
it ran low on fuel
and had to make a forced landing
on mud flats near San Diego.

<!-- S22 -->
The story goes that the pilot
had checked the wrong fuel gauge,
the one for the car.

<!-- S23 -->
Whether or not that's true,
it shows the problem.
Two machines in one body
means two of everything to get right.

<!-- S24 -->
So engineers had a better idea.
Skip the runway.
Take off straight up,
from a street, or a roof.

<!-- S25 -->
That's what most flying cars today
really are.
Not cars with wings,
but small aircraft that rise vertically.

<!-- S26 -->
And that's where physics
sends the bill.

<!-- S27 -->
At a hundred kilometres an hour,
an ordinary car needs
about thirteen kilowatts.

<!-- S28 -->
Its weight is carried by the road.
The engine only has to keep it moving.

<!-- S29 -->
A machine that hovers
has to carry all of that weight itself,
by throwing air downwards,
every second.

<!-- S30 -->
For something the size of a car,
that takes somewhere between
twenty and forty times as much power.

<!-- S31 -->
And the smaller the rotors,
the worse it gets.

<!-- S32 -->
A helicopter does the same job
with about ten times the power of a car.

<!-- S33 -->
But only because its rotor
is ten metres across.

<!-- S34 -->
So it's efficient
exactly because it could never
fit on a road.

<!-- S34b -->
To keep a car in the air cheaply,
we'd need something completely new.
Something like antigravity.
And that's far beyond anything
we know how to build.

<!-- S35 -->
And if the power comes from batteries,
there's a second problem.

<!-- S36 -->
A kilogram of petrol holds
about fifty times as much energy
as a kilogram of today's battery packs.

<!-- S37 -->
Electric motors waste much less of it,
but even then, the battery
still delivers only about a fifteenth as much.

<!-- S38 -->
A fuel tank gets lighter as it empties.
A battery doesn't.

<!-- S39 -->
Then there's the question
the old illustrations never asked.

<!-- S40 -->
Who's flying it?

<!-- S41 -->
A private pilot's licence takes
at least forty hours in the air,
and in practice usually more.

<!-- S42 -->
In the United States, there are
around fourteen hundred licensed drivers
for every private pilot.

<!-- S43 -->
So there are really only three ways
to give everyone a flying car.

<!-- S44 -->
Everyone learns to fly.

<!-- S45 -->
Someone on the ground
flies it for them.

<!-- S46 -->
Or it flies itself.

<!-- S47 -->
The second one runs out of people.
In a NASA simulation,
one person on the ground
could safely look after three aircraft.

<!-- S48 -->
Which leaves the third.

<!-- S49 -->
In China, a pilotless air taxi
has been certified since 2023.

<!-- S50 -->
Even so, it hasn't carried
a single paying passenger yet.

<!-- S51 -->
So the flying car may need more
than better engines.
It needs software we'd trust
with the sky above our heads.

<!-- S52 -->
And so far, we've only talked
about one machine.

<!-- S53 -->
Now put a few thousand of them
over one city.

<!-- S54 -->
When a car breaks down,
you pull over.

<!-- S55 -->
In the air, there's nowhere to pull over.

<!-- S55b -->
Antigravity would help here too.
You could pull over onto a hard shoulder
in the sky.

<!-- S55c -->
You just couldn't get out and walk.

<!-- S56 -->
Per hour of travel,
private flying is about fifty times
deadlier than driving.

<!-- S57 -->
And over a city,
it isn't only the people on board
who are at risk.

<!-- S58 -->
It's everyone underneath,
who never chose to fly at all.

<!-- S59 -->
Then there's the noise.

<!-- S60 -->
The newest air taxis
really are much quieter than helicopters.

<!-- S61 -->
But even a quiet aircraft,
times a thousand,
over the street where you live,
every morning and every evening.

<!-- S62 -->
And every one of them
has to land somewhere.

<!-- S63 -->
São Paulo has one of the biggest
helicopter fleets of any city,
with about four hundred helicopters
and around two hundred and sixty helipads.

<!-- S64 -->
That's what it takes
for just a few hundred machines.

<!-- S65 -->
So now, the last step.
Make it millions.

<!-- S66 -->
London has more than
two and a half million cars.

<!-- S67 -->
If just one in a hundred
became a flying car,
that's over twenty-six thousand aircraft
over one city.

<!-- S68 -->
That's about as many as are in the air
over the entire planet
at any one moment.

<!-- S69 -->
At its busiest, the whole of
the United States handles
about five and a half thousand flights
in the air at the same time.

<!-- S70 -->
It takes fourteen thousand
air traffic controllers to do it.

<!-- S71 -->
And every one of those flights
has an assigned route and altitude,
and a plan filed in advance.

<!-- S72 -->
A city full of flying cars
would need all of that,
for trips to the supermarket.

<!-- S73 -->
So that's the real answer.

<!-- S74 -->
A flying car is a perfectly good idea
for one person.

<!-- S75 -->
It just stops working
when everyone has one.

<!-- S76 -->
Which is why the biggest companies
building them today
have quietly changed the plan.

<!-- S77 -->
They're not selling flying cars.
They're building air taxis.

<!-- S78 -->
A small number of aircraft,
flown by professional pilots,
on a few carefully chosen routes.

<!-- S79 -->
In Dubai, Joby plans
to fly passengers between
four landing sites,
one of them at the airport.

<!-- S80 -->
But even this smaller dream
is hard.

<!-- S81 -->
Two of Europe's best-known
air taxi companies,
Lilium and Volocopter,
filed for insolvency
within two months of each other.

<!-- S82 -->
And Airbus paused its own project,
saying battery technology
wasn't ready yet.

<!-- S83 -->
So far, nowhere in the world
is an air taxi like this
carrying paying passengers.

<!-- S84 -->
If they do take off,
they'll look less like the future
we were promised,
and more like something we already have.

<!-- S85 -->
A helicopter service.
Just quieter, and maybe cheaper.

<!-- S86 -->
Maybe that's where the flying car
belonged all along.

<!-- S87 -->
We imagined it
as a replacement for the car.

<!-- S88 -->
But its real place
may be somewhere else entirely.

<!-- S89 -->
Not in every garage,
but on a handful of routes,
for the trips where flying
really is worth the cost.

<!-- S90 -->
We didn't fail to build flying cars.
We built them,
and found out what they're good for.

<!-- S91 -->
So, would you actually trust
a flying car above your city?
And if not, what would it take?
