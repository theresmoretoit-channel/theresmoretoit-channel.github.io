# The Gyroplane — The Helicopter That Isn't

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine a flying machine
that looks almost exactly
like a helicopter.

It has a big rotor on top.

<!-- S02 -->
It can take off and land
in a surprisingly small space.

And if its engine stops in mid-air,
it can still land safely.

<!-- S03 -->
Sounds like a helicopter, right?

Well… not quite.

This is a gyroplane.

Also called an autogyro.

<!-- S04 -->
And the weirdest thing about it
is this.

The engine doesn't power the rotor.

<!-- S05 -->
In a helicopter,
the engine spins the main rotor.

The rotor produces lift,
and by tilting the rotor,
the helicopter can also move
in different directions.

<!-- S06 -->
But there is a problem.

If you spin a rotor one way,
physics wants to spin
the rest of the helicopter
the other way.

<!-- S07 -->
That's why conventional helicopters
need a way to counteract that torque.

Usually a tail rotor.

<!-- S08 -->
A gyroplane doesn't have this problem.

Its engine powers
a completely separate propeller.

<!-- S09 -->
The propeller pushes
the aircraft forward.

And the airflow
through the big rotor on top
makes it spin.

This is called autorotation.

<!-- S10 -->
So the basic idea is this.

Engine, propeller, forward motion,
airflow, spinning rotor, lift.

<!-- S11 -->
But hold on.

Why should air flowing through a rotor
make that rotor spin at all?

<!-- S12 -->
The answer is the angle.

The rotor disc isn't flat.
It's tilted back.

So the air isn't pushed down through it,
the way it is under a helicopter.

It comes up through it instead.

<!-- S13 -->
Now think about one blade.

It's slicing forward through the air,
and at the same time,
air is arriving from below.

<!-- S14 -->
The flow it meets is the sum of both,
and it comes from underneath.

That tips the lift of the blade
slightly forward.

Forward, in the direction
the blade is already moving.

<!-- S15 -->
So the air isn't only
holding the rotor up.

It's driving it around.

<!-- S16 -->
Though not the whole blade does that.

Where the flow arrives steeply,
the blade is driven.

Where it arrives shallowly,
it drags.

<!-- S17 -->
Nobody is holding the rotor
at any particular speed.

It settles where those two balance,
and holds there by itself
for the whole flight.

<!-- S18 -->
The engine doesn't have to spin the rotor.

The air does it for free.

Well… not completely for free.

<!-- S19 -->
You are exchanging some forward speed
for the energy needed
to keep the rotor spinning.

But the important point is this.

There is no engine-driven rotor shaft.

<!-- S20 -->
So why doesn't the whole aircraft spin?

This is probably the first thing
you would ask.

<!-- S21 -->
If the rotor is spinning,
shouldn't the aircraft spin
in the opposite direction?

And yes, there can be
aerodynamic torques.

<!-- S22 -->
But there is a huge difference
between a rotor being driven
by an engine,
and a rotor being driven
by the airflow.

<!-- S23 -->
In a helicopter,
the engine continuously supplies torque
to overcome the aerodynamic drag
of the rotor.

<!-- S24 -->
The rotor pushes against the air.

The air pushes back.

And that reaction torque
has to be counteracted.

<!-- S25 -->
In a gyroplane,
the rotor is free-spinning.

It isn't being forced to rotate
by the engine.

The airflow is what keeps it rotating.

<!-- S26 -->
So there is no large engine torque
being transmitted
through the rotor shaft
into the aircraft.

<!-- S27 -->
And that's why you don't need
a tail rotor
just to keep the fuselage
from spinning.

<!-- S28 -->
But there's a catch.

If you're thinking,
"so this is just a cheap helicopter"…
not quite.

<!-- S29 -->
The gyroplane has one enormous
limitation.

It can't hover.

<!-- S30 -->
A helicopter can generate lift
while sitting almost stationary.

A gyroplane needs airflow
through its rotor.

<!-- S31 -->
That means it normally
has to move forward.

So unlike a helicopter,
you can't simply point it upward
and take off vertically.

<!-- S32 -->
You accelerate along the ground,
the rotor spins faster and faster,
and eventually there is enough lift
to take off.

<!-- S33 -->
Some gyroplanes can pre-spin
their rotors before takeoff,
allowing a very short takeoff run.

<!-- S34 -->
And that spinning up
can be taken further.

Far enough that a few gyroplanes
can leave the ground
without any run at all.

<!-- S35 -->
It's called a jump takeoff.

The rotor is spun up on the ground
past its normal flying speed,
with the blades set almost flat,
so they make hardly any lift.

<!-- S36 -->
Then the pilot changes the blade angle
all at once.

The stored spin turns into lift,
and the machine hops off the ground.

Cierva's machines could clear
about six metres.

<!-- S37 -->
But it's one jump,
out of energy the rotor already had.

The moment it's up,
it still has to fly forward
to keep that rotor turning.

It doesn't hover at the top.

<!-- S38 -->
But it's still not a helicopter.

It's much closer to a strange hybrid
between an aeroplane and a helicopter.

<!-- S39 -->
What happens when the engine stops?

Here's where the gyroplane
gets really interesting.

Imagine you're flying along…
and the engine suddenly dies.

<!-- S40 -->
In an aeroplane,
you now have a glider.

In a helicopter, the pilot
enters autorotation
and uses the airflow
to keep the rotor spinning.

<!-- S41 -->
And in a gyroplane?

You're already in autorotation.

<!-- S42 -->
The rotor is normally powered
by the airflow anyway.

So the engine can stop
turning the propeller…
but the rotor keeps spinning.

<!-- S43 -->
The pilot can control the descent
and make an emergency landing.

Of course, this isn't magic.

<!-- S44 -->
You still need altitude, speed,
a suitable landing area
and a competent pilot.

<!-- S45 -->
And it isn't crash-proof.

The rotor turns because the air
is pushing up through it.

Unload it, and it slows down.

<!-- S46 -->
Older designs carried the propeller
high above the centre of gravity,
and could pitch down into it.

Modern machines are built against it,
and pilots are taught
never to unload the rotor.

<!-- S47 -->
But an engine failure
doesn't automatically mean
that the aircraft has lost
its ability to generate lift.

<!-- S48 -->
And this thing is surprisingly old.

You might think gyroplanes are
some modern ultralight invention.

They're not.

<!-- S49 -->
The first successful practical gyroplane
was developed by Spanish engineer
Juan de la Cierva.

<!-- S50 -->
His C four made
its first successful flight
in nineteen twenty-three.

That's more than a century ago.

<!-- S51 -->
And Cierva's work on rotor systems
also contributed ideas
that later became important
in helicopter development.

<!-- S52 -->
So the gyroplane wasn't invented
as a cheap alternative
to modern helicopters.

<!-- S53 -->
It was actually one of the stepping stones
toward rotary-wing flight
as we know it today.

<!-- S54 -->
And this thing is surprisingly
affordable.

A modern two-seat gyroplane
can cost only a fraction of the price
of a comparable small helicopter.

<!-- S55 -->
Depending on the model,
a gyroplane can be available
for roughly twenty to forty per cent
of the purchase price
of a comparable helicopter.

<!-- S56 -->
And the difference doesn't end there.

A small gyroplane might burn
roughly ten to twenty litres
of fuel per hour,
depending on the engine,
the aircraft and the conditions.

<!-- S57 -->
At a cruising speed
around one hundred and forty
kilometres per hour,
that can work out to roughly
seven to fourteen litres
per hundred kilometres.

<!-- S58 -->
That's not exactly
economical transport…
but for a machine
with a rotor on top
that flies through the sky,
it's pretty remarkable.

<!-- S59 -->
So if you simply want to fly,
and don't need to hover…
a gyroplane starts looking
surprisingly attractive.

<!-- S60 -->
So why doesn't everyone fly one?

And this is the real question.

The gyroplane is relatively simple.

<!-- S61 -->
It doesn't need a powered main rotor.

It can survive an engine failure
remarkably well.

It can fly at respectable speeds.

<!-- S62 -->
And compared with a helicopter,
it can be much cheaper
to buy and to operate.

<!-- S63 -->
But you give up the helicopter's
most useful trick.

Hovering.

<!-- S64 -->
You need forward speed.

You need space
for takeoff and landing.

And you can't simply land vertically
in your back garden.

<!-- S65 -->
So the gyroplane
isn't a cheap helicopter.

It's something different.

It's an aircraft that takes
one of the most useful ideas
from the helicopter.

The rotor.

<!-- S66 -->
And combines it with
one of the simplest ideas in aviation.

A propeller pushing
the aircraft forward.

<!-- S67 -->
And somehow, this strange combination
has been flying
for more than a hundred years.
