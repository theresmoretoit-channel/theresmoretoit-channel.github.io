# Could We Make Acceleration Impossible to Feel?

<!-- S01 -->

Could we make passengers
in a plane or car
feel no acceleration at all?

<!-- S02 -->

The vehicle could speed up.

It could slow down.

It could turn.

And the people inside
would feel absolutely nothing.

<!-- S03 -->

No pushing into the seat.

No being thrown forward
when the vehicle brakes.

And no sudden jolts
during turbulence.

<!-- S04 -->

But this is not only
about comfort.

Acceleration is a ceiling.

<!-- S05 -->

A fighter pilot wears a suit
that squeezes the legs
and the belly.

And the pilot strains
against the force
the whole time.

<!-- S06 -->

Together, that buys
about nine times gravity.

For tens of seconds.

<!-- S07 -->

Without that,
a few times gravity is enough
to drain the blood
from your head.

Your vision greys out.

Then you black out.

<!-- S08 -->

So every vehicle
that carries people

is built around
a very low limit.

Not the limit of the engine.

The limit of the passenger.

<!-- S09 -->

This sounds like science fiction.

And it is.

But the physics behind it
is surprisingly interesting.

So what would we actually
have to invent
to make it possible?

<!-- S10 -->

Let's start with
the simplest problem:

Acceleration.

<!-- S11 -->

When a car accelerates,
your body wants to keep moving
at its original speed.

The seat pushes you forward.

That force accelerates your body
along with the car.

<!-- S12 -->

And you feel that force
as acceleration.

<!-- S13 -->

When the car brakes,
the opposite happens.

The seat belt stops you.

<!-- S14 -->

So if we want you
to feel no acceleration,

we somehow have to prevent
that force from reaching you.

<!-- S15 -->

One obvious solution
would be to move your seat.

When the plane accelerates forward,

the seat could move backward
at exactly the right speed.

<!-- S16 -->

From your point of view,

you would remain
almost perfectly still.

<!-- S17 -->

This is not science fiction.

A flight simulator sits
on six moving legs.

It moves well enough
to train real pilots.

<!-- S18 -->

But look at what
it actually does.

The legs give it
a metre or so of room.

So the platform cannot
keep accelerating.

<!-- S19 -->

When the pilot
opens the throttle,

the simulator tilts backwards.

<!-- S20 -->

And gravity pushes the pilot
into the seat.

The inner ear cannot tell
the difference.

<!-- S21 -->

So the simulator is not
cancelling acceleration.

It is borrowing gravity
to imitate it.

<!-- S22 -->

Remember that.

We will come back to it.

<!-- S23 -->

But back to the moving seat.

There is a problem.

A very big one.

<!-- S24 -->

Suppose the vehicle accelerates
at one G

for just ten seconds.

That's roughly
the acceleration of gravity.

<!-- S25 -->

After ten seconds,

the vehicle would be moving
about three hundred and fifty
kilometres per hour
faster than before.

<!-- S26 -->

To keep you stationary,

your seat would have to move
almost five hundred metres
relative to the vehicle.

<!-- S27 -->

So our first inertial damper
would need a seat rail
almost half a kilometre long.

<!-- S28 -->

Probably not very practical
for economy class.

So let's try something else.

<!-- S29 -->

What if we could push
the passenger directly?

<!-- S30 -->

Sensors could measure
the acceleration.

A computer could work out
the force required.

And an actuator could push
your body in exactly
the right direction.

<!-- S31 -->

That sounds much better.

But there is still
a fundamental problem.

Where does that force come from?

<!-- S32 -->

If you push the passenger forward,

something else has to receive
the corresponding reaction.

<!-- S33 -->

We haven't eliminated inertia —
the resistance of matter
to changing its motion.

We are simply fighting it.

<!-- S34 -->

And this is where
the science fiction version
gets really interesting.

What if we could somehow
change inertia itself?

<!-- S35 -->

Imagine a technology
that could temporarily
change how matter responds
to acceleration.

The vehicle could accelerate.

But your body
would not have to.

<!-- S36 -->

That would be a real
inertial damper.

Not a better suspension system.

Not a clever seat.

Something fundamentally new.

And we don't currently know
how to do that.

<!-- S37 -->

But now think about a force
you have never felt.

<!-- S38 -->

Gravity.

<!-- S39 -->

Right now, gravity is pulling you
towards the centre of the Earth.

<!-- S40 -->

And you cannot feel it.

<!-- S41 -->

What you feel
is the chair under you.

Take the chair away,
and the feeling goes with it.

<!-- S42 -->

In free fall,
you feel nothing at all.

<!-- S43 -->

Astronauts do not float
because gravity is gone.

They float because
they are falling continuously.

<!-- S44 -->

And here is why
that matters so much.

<!-- S45 -->

A seat pushes you
in one place.

That push then has to travel
through your body.

Your bones compress.

Your organs shift.

And that is what
you actually feel.

<!-- S46 -->

Gravity does not do that.

It gives every part of you
the same acceleration.

<!-- S47 -->

Nothing is compressed.

So there is nothing to feel.

<!-- S48 -->

Einstein built
general relativity
on exactly that observation.

A falling person
feels no gravity.

<!-- S49 -->

So the goal is not
to cancel a force.

The goal is to accelerate
every part of you
by the same amount.

<!-- S50 -->

Which is precisely
what gravity already does.

And it is why the simulator
could borrow it.

<!-- S51 -->

So what if we could
manipulate gravity itself?

<!-- S52 -->

In Einstein's general relativity,
gravity is not really a force.

Matter and energy
curve spacetime,

and things move
through that curve.

<!-- S53 -->

So if we could control
spacetime itself,

we would not have to push
the passenger at all.

We would change the geometry
the passenger moves through.

<!-- S54 -->

From inside the vehicle,
it would look almost magical.

The ship accelerates
at enormous speed.

And inside, nothing is felt.

<!-- S55 -->

This is roughly the kind
of idea that science fiction
calls an inertial dampener.

<!-- S56 -->

But there is a catch.

<!-- S57 -->

We can measure gravity
with extraordinary precision.

We can even detect
gravitational waves
from colliding black holes.

But we have no technology
that can turn it down.

<!-- S58 -->

We don't even know
whether gravity has
a quantum particle at all.

Physicists call it the graviton.

Nobody has ever detected one.

<!-- S59 -->

So could we really build
a science-fiction inertial damper?

<!-- S60 -->

With today's physics:

No.

<!-- S61 -->

But if we someday learn
to manipulate inertia,

gravity,

or spacetime itself,

the situation could change
completely.

<!-- S62 -->

Because then the problem
would no longer be:

"How do we make the vehicle
accelerate more gently?"

It would become:

"How do we make the passengers
not share that acceleration?"

<!-- S63 -->

And that distinction
could be enormous.

Because a spacecraft
that cannot hurt its passengers

no longer has to crawl
through space.

<!-- S64 -->

You could keep accelerating.

And accelerating.

And accelerating.

Until the spacecraft
reaches a significant fraction
of the speed of light.

<!-- S65 -->

The hardest part of
interstellar travel

might not be reaching
a high speed.

It might be surviving
the acceleration required
to get there.

<!-- S66 -->

So the inertial damper
is more than science fiction.

It is a question about one
of the most basic
properties of matter:

<!-- S67 -->

Why does matter resist
being accelerated
in the first place?

<!-- S68 -->

We know how to describe
that behaviour.

But we still don't have
a technology that lets us
switch it off.

<!-- S69 -->

Maybe we never will.

<!-- S70 -->

Or maybe one day,

the thing that sounds
most impossible

will turn out to be
the missing technology
that makes the stars
reachable.
