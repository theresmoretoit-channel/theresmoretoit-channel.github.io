# How Do We Clean Up Space Junk?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine thousands of tonnes of junk
flying around Earth.
Dead satellites.
Spent rocket stages.
Fragments left behind
by explosions and collisions.

<!-- S02 -->
And sometimes, pieces of metal
so small that they are difficult to track.
All of them can be travelling
at several kilometres per second.

<!-- S03 -->
Space is enormous.
So how can a little bit of garbage
possibly be a problem?

<!-- S04 -->
Because in space,
a piece of garbage
doesn't have to be large
to cause enormous damage.
It just has to hit something.
And when it does,
it can create hundreds or thousands
of new pieces.

<!-- S05 -->
So we have a rather strange problem.
How do you clean a place
where there is no floor,
no broom, and no garbage truck?

<!-- S06 -->
On Earth,
leaving something behind is easy.
Eventually, gravity brings it down.
In orbit, that is not necessarily
what happens.

<!-- S07 -->
A satellite in low Earth orbit
can travel at roughly
seven to eight kilometres per second.
That is more than
twenty-five thousand kilometres per hour.

<!-- S08 -->
And two objects
travelling in different orbits
can collide at even higher
relative speeds.
So a tiny screw, a fragment of insulation,
or a piece of metal
is not really "just a screw."
It is a projectile.

<!-- S09 -->
And that is why some pieces
of space debris can remain in orbit
for decades, centuries, or even longer.
It depends on how high they are.
The higher the orbit,
the less atmospheric drag they experience.
And the longer they can stay there.

<!-- S10 -->
This is where things get complicated.
Let's say we have a dead satellite.
We could simply send
another spacecraft to it.
The spacecraft catches it.
And brings it down.
Problem solved.
Except it is not that simple.

<!-- S11 -->
The cleanup spacecraft
first has to reach a similar orbit.
It has to approach the target
at the right speed
and from the right direction.
And it may have to approach a satellite
that is tumbling uncontrollably
through space.

<!-- S12 -->
Many old satellites
were never designed to be captured.
They don't have handles.
They don't have docking ports.
And they may not have
any functioning systems left
to help control them.
It is basically a flying wreck.

<!-- S13 -->
And now imagine trying to attach
another spacecraft to it
while both are moving
at several kilometres per second.
That is why active debris-removal missions
are so difficult.

<!-- S14 -->
Europe's ClearSpace-1 mission,
for example, is designed to capture
a non-functional object in orbit
and bring it down
together with the spacecraft.
But what about the enormous number
of smaller pieces?
For those, we need other ideas.

<!-- S15 -->
This sounds reasonable.
We have an object we don't want.
So we destroy it.
But that could make the problem
much worse.

<!-- S16 -->
If you smash one large piece of debris
into hundreds of smaller pieces,
you haven't removed it.
You have created a cloud of debris.
And every one of those pieces
can collide with something else.

<!-- S17 -->
That can create even more fragments.
And the cycle can continue.
This is the basic idea
behind the scenario known
as the Kessler syndrome.

<!-- S18 -->
A chain reaction of collisions
could create so much debris
that some orbits become increasingly
difficult or dangerous to use.
So if we want to clean up space,
we cannot simply smash everything
into smaller pieces.

<!-- S19 -->
Do we actually have to carry it down?
No.
And this is where the first trick appears.
We don't necessarily have to
catch the debris and tow it away.
We just have to change its orbit.

<!-- S20 -->
If we lower the lowest point
of an object's orbit enough,
it can begin passing
through the upper atmosphere.
The atmosphere creates drag.
The object loses energy.
Its orbit gets lower.

<!-- S21 -->
Eventually,
it enters the denser atmosphere.
And many small objects
burn up on the way down.
So the problem suddenly changes.
It is no longer:
"How do we get this satellite
back to Earth?"
It is:
"How do we give it a tiny push?"
And there are several surprisingly
strange ways to do that.

<!-- S22 -->
The most obvious solution
is still a robot.
Send a spacecraft to the dead satellite.
Grab it.
And change its orbit.

<!-- S23 -->
The advantage is that this can work
even with relatively large objects.
The disadvantage is that
every piece of debris is different.
Every mission costs money.
Every target requires navigation,
rendezvous, and some way
of safely attaching to it.

<!-- S24 -->
And if there are hundreds
or thousands of objects
we want to remove,
we would need a lot of missions.
So perhaps we need something
that doesn't have to fly
to every single piece.

<!-- S25 -->
Yes.
A net.
The idea is surprisingly simple.
A spacecraft approaches the target
and deploys a large net.
The net catches the object.
The spacecraft can then change its orbit
and bring the target down.

<!-- S26 -->
The advantage is that
you don't necessarily need
a precisely designed attachment point.
You just need to catch the object.

<!-- S27 -->
But there is a small problem.
You are deploying a large net
while both spacecraft and debris
are moving at several
kilometres per second.
And you really don't want your net
to become tangled with something else.
So even this apparently simple idea
requires extremely precise
navigation and control.

<!-- S28 -->
Now we get to a solution
that sounds like science fiction.
A laser.
But not because we are going
to blast the satellite apart.
That would be exactly the problem
we are trying to avoid.

<!-- S29 -->
Instead, a laser could give the object
a tiny push.
There are two main ideas.
The first uses radiation pressure.
Photons have no rest mass,
but they carry momentum.
When light hits an object,
it transfers some of that momentum.

<!-- S30 -->
Normally, the effect is tiny.
But in space,
we can potentially apply it
very precisely
and over a long period of time.

<!-- S31 -->
The second idea is much more interesting.
Laser ablation.
A powerful laser heats a tiny area
of the object's surface
until some of the material vaporises.

<!-- S32 -->
That material shoots away
from the surface.
And because of the conservation
of momentum,
the object receives a small impulse
in the opposite direction.
In other words,
it is basically a tiny rocket engine.
Except the fuel is the object itself.
And the laser doesn't have to
physically touch it.

<!-- S33 -->
Why could a laser actually work?
Because we don't need to change
the object's speed
by kilometres per second.
We only need to change it
by a tiny amount.

<!-- S34 -->
Imagine a ball moving around Earth.
Give it a very small change in velocity.
You might not notice anything
immediately.
But its orbit is now slightly different.
And after many orbits,
that tiny difference
can become significant.

<!-- S35 -->
The object's lowest point
can move into the upper atmosphere.
And then the atmosphere takes over.
That is what makes the idea
so interesting.
The laser doesn't have to
destroy the debris.
It just has to give it a little push.

<!-- S36 -->
So why don't we have
giant space lasers already?
Because, of course, there are problems.
The first is accuracy.
We have to know exactly
where the object is.
And that is particularly difficult
for small pieces of debris.

<!-- S37 -->
The second problem is energy.
Ablation requires
a sufficiently intense laser beam.
The third problem is the atmosphere.
If we are firing a laser from the ground,
the beam has to pass
through Earth's atmosphere,
which can scatter and distort it.

<!-- S38 -->
And the fourth problem
may be the most important.
What if we get the direction wrong?
With objects moving this fast,
even a small error can produce
a different orbit than intended.

<!-- S39 -->
So a laser is not
a giant space vacuum cleaner.
It is more like an extremely precise
orbital brake.

<!-- S40 -->
What about the really big stuff?
This is where lasers
become less attractive.
If you have a satellite
weighing several tonnes,
changing its orbit requires
a lot more energy.

<!-- S41 -->
So for large objects,
a robotic spacecraft may make
much more sense.
For smaller debris,
a laser could potentially be useful.
For objects that can be captured,
we could use nets
or other capture systems.

<!-- S42 -->
And for future satellites,
the best solution may be
to design them so they can safely
leave orbit at the end of their lives.
Because the cheapest piece
of space junk...
is the piece
that never becomes space junk.

<!-- S43 -->
Space debris is not only something
created decades ago.
Every year, we launch more spacecraft.
And the number of objects around Earth
continues to grow.

<!-- S44 -->
So we cannot solve the problem
simply by cleaning.
We also have to prevent new debris
from accumulating.
That means things like
reliable end-of-life disposal.
Tracking objects.
Avoiding collisions.
And designing spacecraft
with their eventual disposal in mind.

<!-- S45 -->
Otherwise, we would end up
in a rather absurd situation.
We would build increasingly
sophisticated vacuum cleaners...
while continuing to throw garbage
onto the floor.

<!-- S46 -->
So, can we actually clean up space?
Probably not with one magical invention.
There probably won't be
a single giant machine
that circles Earth
and collects everything.
Instead, we will need
a combination of technologies.

<!-- S47 -->
Robots for large objects.
Capture systems for suitable targets.
Lasers for some smaller pieces.
Better tracking of objects.
And, perhaps most importantly,
rules and engineering practices
that prevent new debris
from being created in the first place.

<!-- S48 -->
Because in space,
we don't always have to remove an object.
Sometimes, we just have to push it.
Maybe by a hundred metres per second.
Barely more than one percent of its speed.
Then gravity and the atmosphere
can do the rest.

<!-- S49 -->
But these are only some of the ideas.
Because lasers, robots, and nets
are not the only strange ways
we could deal with the junk
orbiting our planet.

<!-- S50 -->
There are concepts involving harpoons,
giant sails, magnetic fields,
electrodynamic tethers,
and even using sunlight itself.
So in the next episode,
we will look at some of the other ways
humanity is trying to answer
an absurd question:
How do you clean up something
moving at twenty-eight thousand
kilometres per hour?
