# How Could Aliens Actually Look?

<!-- S01 -->
When we imagine aliens,
we usually imagine something
strangely familiar.

<!-- S02 -->
Two arms.

<!-- S02b -->
Two legs.

<!-- S02c -->
A head.

<!-- S02d -->
Two eyes.

<!-- S02e -->
A mouth.

<!-- S03 -->
Maybe they're green. Maybe grey.
Maybe they have a few extra fingers.
But underneath, they're still...
people.

<!-- S04 -->
Yet evolution on another world
would have no reason
to build anything like us.
So what could an alien
actually look like?

<!-- S05 -->
Here's the thing.
We don't have to invent
anything crazy to find out.
Earth has already built
creatures stranger than
most science fiction.
We just don't think of them
as aliens.

<!-- S06 -->
Take a flower.
To you, it's yellow.

<!-- S07 -->
To a bee, it has a dark target
painted in the middle,
pointing straight at the nectar.

<!-- S08 -->
Bees see ultraviolet light.
The pattern is really there.
We simply can't see it.

<!-- S09 -->
And that's just one extra colour.

<!-- S10 -->
Pit vipers hunt in total darkness.
Two small pits on their face
sense the heat of a mouse,
so they can strike at a warm body
they cannot see.

<!-- S11 -->
Sharks feel electricity.
Every living fish
gives off a tiny electric field,
and a shark can find a fish
buried under the sand.

<!-- S12 -->
Some fish go one step further.
They don't just sense electricity.
They talk with it.

<!-- S13 -->
Pulses of electricity
for courtship, for warnings,
for "this is my territory".
A whole conversation
that we would never notice.

<!-- S14 -->
Migrating birds sense
Earth's magnetic field.
And many bats see with sound.
They shout, and listen for the echo.

<!-- S15 -->
Now imagine an alien
that has all of these.
Or none of them.

<!-- S16 -->
And colour itself
might not look like ours.

<!-- S17 -->
Plants on Earth are green
because chlorophyll
soaks up red and blue light,
and bounces the green back.

<!-- S18 -->
But most stars in our galaxy
aren't like the Sun.
They're red dwarfs,
smaller, cooler stars
that shine mostly in red and infrared.

<!-- S19 -->
Under a sun like that,
plants might evolve to swallow
every bit of light they can get.

<!-- S20 -->
Which means
their forests could be black.

<!-- S21 -->
Then there's the opposite question.
If light isn't useful on a world,
why have eyes at all?

<!-- S22 -->
Earth has an answer to that too.
In caves in Mexico, there's a fish
whose ancestors had perfectly good eyes.

<!-- S23 -->
After generations in the dark,
its descendants are blind.
Just skin where the eyes once were.

<!-- S24 -->
Now picture a world
under kilometres of ice,
like the oceans that may exist
on some moons of Jupiter.

<!-- S25 -->
Eyes there would be
about as useful as they are
in that cave.

<!-- S26 -->
And down there, life might not
need sunlight at all.

<!-- S27 -->
At the bottom of our own ocean,
around volcanic vents,
there are worms
two metres long.

<!-- S28 -->
They have no mouth.
No stomach. No gut.

<!-- S29 -->
Instead, they carry bacteria
inside their bodies
that turn chemicals from the vent
into food.

<!-- S30 -->
An animal that never eats.
It would be hard to design
something more alien than that.
And it's here.

<!-- S31 -->
Gravity would change the shape too.

<!-- S32 -->
Make an animal twice as tall,
and it becomes eight times heavier.
But its bones only get
four times stronger.

<!-- S33 -->
That's why an elephant
doesn't look like a big gazelle.

<!-- S34 -->
On a heavy world,
large animals would be short and squat.
On a light one,
they could be tall and spindly
in a way that would collapse on Earth.

<!-- S35 -->
So far, we've changed
the senses, the colours
and the proportions.
But we've kept the basic plan.
A body with a head.

<!-- S36 -->
Do aliens even need a head?

<!-- S37 -->
An octopus has
around five hundred million neurons.
And most of them aren't in its head.
They're in its arms.

<!-- S38 -->
Each arm can taste,
touch and make
some of its own decisions.

<!-- S39 -->
A starfish has
no brain at all.
Just a ring of nerves,
and no front or back.

<!-- S40 -->
And then there's this.
A slime mould.
A single giant cell
with no neurons whatsoever.

<!-- S41 -->
Put it in a maze
with food at two exits,
and it finds the shortest path.

<!-- S42 -->
Put oat flakes on a map,
where the cities around Tokyo are,
and it grows a network
that looks remarkably like
the Tokyo railway.

<!-- S43 -->
No brain. No neurons.
And yet it keeps finding
surprisingly efficient routes.

<!-- S44 -->
So a head is one solution.
It's not a rule.

<!-- S45 -->
Now let's go further.
Do aliens even need to be
one individual?

<!-- S46 -->
This is a Portuguese man o' war.
It looks like a jellyfish.

<!-- S47 -->
It may not be one animal.
It's a colony of many small bodies,
each specialised for a single job.
Floating, stinging, eating.

<!-- S48 -->
None of them can survive alone.
Together, they behave
like one creature.

<!-- S49 -->
So is that one organism?
Or hundreds?

<!-- S50 -->
Biologists still argue about it.
And an alien intelligence
could be the same kind of puzzle.

<!-- S51 -->
Then there's size.

<!-- S52 -->
In Utah, there's a forest
of around forty-seven thousand
aspen trees.

<!-- S53 -->
Except it isn't a forest.
Every one of those trunks
grows from the same root system,
with the same genes.

<!-- S54 -->
Biologically, it's a single organism.
Tens of thousands of trunks.
One genetic individual.

<!-- S55 -->
It's called Pando,
and all together
it weighs around six thousand tonnes.

<!-- S56 -->
You can walk right through it
and never realise
you're inside a single living thing.

<!-- S57 -->
And Pando doesn't even cover
the most ground.
In Oregon, a single honey fungus
spreads underground
across nearly ten square kilometres.

<!-- S58 -->
On the surface,
you only see a few mushrooms.

<!-- S59 -->
And size changes time.

<!-- S60 -->
In America, there's a mayfly
whose females spend
less than five minutes as adults.

<!-- S61 -->
Five minutes to fly, mate,
lay eggs and die.

<!-- S62 -->
Now imagine intelligence
evolving on that clock.

<!-- S63 -->
Or the opposite.
A giant, slow creature
might take a year to finish a thought.
To it, a human life
could be a flicker.

<!-- S64 -->
And then there's the question
we rarely ask.
Does life need to stand on a planet
at all?

<!-- S65 -->
In 1976, Carl Sagan
and Edwin Salpeter imagined life
in the atmosphere of Jupiter.

<!-- S66 -->
Giant gas bags,
kilometres across,
drifting in the clouds.
Never touching the ground,
because there isn't one.

<!-- S67 -->
Nobody knows if anything like that exists.
It was a thought experiment.

<!-- S68 -->
But nobody has shown
that it's impossible.
And it shows how many of our assumptions
come from standing on a rock.

<!-- S69 -->
Which brings us
to the biggest problem of all.

<!-- S70 -->
Imagine landing on another world.
You see rocks. Dust.

<!-- S70b -->
Hills.

<!-- S70c -->
Nothing moves.

<!-- S71 -->
"There's no life here."

<!-- S72 -->
But we just saw a forest
that is one living thing.
A colony that acts like an animal.
A cell that finds its way
through a maze.

<!-- S73 -->
What if that landscape
is alive?

<!-- S74 -->
What if it thinks
once every thousand years,
through chemistry we've never measured?

<!-- S75 -->
We'd be standing
in the middle of an alien...
and see nothing but geology.

<!-- S76 -->
So what would an alien
actually look like?

<!-- S77 -->
The honest answer is,
we don't know.

<!-- S78 -->
But Earth gives us a hint.
With one chemistry and one planet,
life has already made
creatures we'd barely recognise.

<!-- S79 -->
Another world wouldn't
start from our history,
our gravity or our sunlight.

<!-- S80 -->
When we imagine an alien,
we usually start with a human...
and change the shape.

<!-- S81 -->
Maybe the real question
isn't what aliens look like.

<!-- S82 -->
Maybe it's whether we'd recognise one
if we were looking right at it.
