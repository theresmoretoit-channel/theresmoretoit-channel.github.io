# Why Do People Go Mushroom Hunting?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you come from a country
where nobody picks wild mushrooms.

You're walking through a forest,
and you see someone carrying a basket.

They spend a few minutes
looking around under the trees.

Then they suddenly get excited.

<!-- S02 -->
They pull a strange brown thing
out of the ground,
brush the dirt off it,
and drop it into the basket.

And you think: what on earth
are they doing?

Then you find out
they're planning to eat it for dinner.

<!-- S03 -->
Which seems stranger still
when you learn that the same forest
holds hundreds of different species.

Some are delicious.

Some will make you ill.

And some can kill you.

<!-- S04 -->
So why do people do this?

Why is it completely normal
in one country,
and almost unthinkable in another?

And is mushroom hunting
a ridiculous thing to do...

or a surprisingly sensible way
of getting food?

<!-- S05 -->
Let's start with something
that sounds like a technicality,
and isn't.

A mushroom isn't a plant.

It isn't an animal either.

Fungi are their own kingdom.

But if you had to pick a side...

<!-- S06 -->
fungi are closer to us
than they are to the tree
they're growing under.

Animals and fungi went their separate ways
about a billion years ago.

Plants had already gone their own way,
earlier still.

<!-- S07 -->
So the thing in the basket
is a distant relative.

A very distant one.

And what you can see in the forest
isn't even the whole organism.

<!-- S08 -->
The part with a cap and a stem
is just the fruiting body.

Most of the organism is underground,
or inside wood —
a network of threads
running through the soil.

<!-- S09 -->
It's a bit like an apple.

The apple isn't the tree.

And the mushroom isn't the fungus.

<!-- S10 -->
How much is hidden?

In Oregon, one single fungus
spreads across nearly
ten square kilometres of forest.

One organism.
Thought to be thousands of years old.

By area, the largest living thing
anyone has ever found.

<!-- S11 -->
And for most of the year
you could walk straight over it
and see nothing at all.

Then, for a few weeks,
it puts up something you can pick.

<!-- S12 -->
Which brings us
to the obvious question.

Why eat them?

Because as food,
mushrooms are a strange choice.

They're mostly water.

They aren't a good source of calories.

<!-- S13 -->
So the honest answer isn't nutrition.

It's that they're there.

Humans don't have to grow all their food.

Sometimes they can just... find it.

Berries. Fruit. Nuts. Roots.

And mushrooms.

<!-- S14 -->
You only had to know where to look.

And here's the part
that turns the forest
into something more
than a free supermarket.

<!-- S15 -->
Most of these mushrooms
cannot be farmed at all.

Not because nobody has tried.

Because of what they are.

<!-- S16 -->
Underground, the threads of the fungus
wrap around the roots of a tree
and join with them.

The tree hands over sugar
that it made from sunlight.

The fungus hands back
water and minerals,
drawn from far more soil
than roots could ever reach.

<!-- S17 -->
It's a trade.

It's called mycorrhiza,
and it is not a curiosity.

The large majority of land plants
do some version of it.

The forest runs on it.

<!-- S18 -->
Which is lovely —
and a serious problem
if you'd like to sell mushrooms.

A fungus that has lived off a tree
for millions of years
has lost the ability
to live on its own.

<!-- S19 -->
Give it a bag of sterile sawdust
and nothing happens.

It isn't looking for food.

It's looking for a partner.

<!-- S20 -->
Now look at a supermarket shelf.

Button mushrooms.
Oyster mushrooms.
Shiitake.

Every one of them is a species
that feeds on dead material
and needs no tree at all.

They can be grown in a shed.

<!-- S21 -->
And the ones that aren't on that shelf —
the porcini, the chanterelles —
are missing for exactly one reason.

Nobody can grow them in a shed.

<!-- S22 -->
So the forest isn't a cheaper version
of the shop.

For some of these mushrooms,
it is the only shop there is.

<!-- S23 -->
Which would be a happy ending,
except for one detail.

Some of them are poisonous.

And the dangerous ones
are not dangerous
in the way you'd expect.

<!-- S24 -->
Take the death cap.

Its toxin doesn't attack your stomach.

It jams the molecular machine
that reads your genes.

Cells stop making proteins.

The liver, which processes
almost everything you eat,
takes the worst of it.

<!-- S25 -->
But that isn't what makes it deadly.

This is.

You feel completely fine.

For six hours.
Often twelve.

<!-- S26 -->
Then you're violently ill.

And then you get better.

The symptoms fade.
You appear to recover.

<!-- S27 -->
Three to six days after the meal,
the liver starts to fail.

<!-- S28 -->
That one species, on its own,
accounts for something like
ninety per cent
of fatal mushroom poisonings
worldwide.

Not because it's the strongest poison
in the forest.

Because by the time
your body tells you anything,
it has had half a day's head start.

<!-- S29 -->
So you might hope
for some simple rule.

There are plenty of them.

Poisonous ones turn a silver spoon black.

Anything the animals eat is safe.

Bright colours mean danger.

<!-- S30 -->
None of them work.

Silver doesn't react to the toxin.

Plenty of animals happily eat
things that would kill us.

And some of the deadliest species
are a modest, unremarkable brown.

<!-- S31 -->
Nature doesn't label anything.

So mushroom hunting isn't:
I see a mushroom,
I eat the mushroom.

<!-- S32 -->
It's: I see a mushroom.
I work out which species it is.
I am certain.

Then I eat it.

<!-- S33 -->
And that's the interesting part.

Because a mushroom
is not a category
you can work with.

An experienced picker
doesn't recognise mushrooms.

They recognise species.

<!-- S34 -->
Dozens of them.

Which trees they grow under.
Which month they appear.
What the underside looks like.
What the flesh does when you cut it.

<!-- S35 -->
It's a biological database,
held in somebody's head.

And traditionally,
they didn't learn it from a book.

They learned it from a person.

Usually outdoors.
Usually as a child.

<!-- S36 -->
Someone points at the ground and says:

that one's good.

That one you don't touch.

And the child remembers.

<!-- S37 -->
Thirty years later,
they say the same two sentences
to somebody else's child.

Do that for a few centuries
and you get a country
where taking a basket into a forest
and coming home with dinner
is completely unremarkable.

<!-- S38 -->
In Czechia, a recent survey found
that seventy-three per cent of adults
go out and pick mushrooms.

Not once in their lives.

As a thing they do.

<!-- S39 -->
Now take the same forest.

The same trees.
The same species,
in the same places.

And nobody picks anything.

Not because it can't be eaten.

Because nobody there
sees it as food.

<!-- S40 -->
Somebody noticed this
on his honeymoon.

In nineteen twenty-seven,
an American and his Russian wife
were walking in the woods
in New York State.

<!-- S41 -->
She saw mushrooms,
ran off delighted,
and started filling her arms.

He found the whole thing revolting.

Same forest.
Same mushrooms.
Same afternoon.

Two completely different worlds.

<!-- S42 -->
They spent the next thirty years
trying to explain it,
and gave the two attitudes names.

Mycophilia. Mycophobia.

Love of mushrooms,
and fear of them.

Neither one is about the mushrooms.

<!-- S43 -->
And that's a fascinating thing
about food.

What counts as normal
is mostly an agreement.

Somewhere, snails are dinner.
Somewhere, frogs.
Seaweed. Insects.

<!-- S44 -->
Some people drink milk
from another species
their entire lives
and never think about it once.

<!-- S45 -->
Mushrooms are the same.

One person looks at the forest floor
and starts planning supper.

Another looks at the same thing
and thinks: that's a fungus.

<!-- S46 -->
Which is a shame,
because they can be extraordinary.

Fried in butter.
In soup. In a sauce.
In risotto. Pickled.

Or with onion and eggs.

<!-- S47 -->
And then there's drying,
which sounds like the dull option
and is the best one.

Because drying doesn't only
stop them going off.

It changes what they are.

<!-- S48 -->
Mushrooms are rich in the compounds
behind umami —
the savoury taste you get
from meat, from broth,
from aged cheese.

<!-- S49 -->
As a mushroom dries,
its own cells break down
and release a second compound
that does something strange.

On its own,
that compound tastes of very little.

But put the two together,
and the savoury hit isn't added.

It's multiplied.

<!-- S50 -->
In tests with the pure compounds,
mixing the two in equal amounts
has been reported to raise
savoury intensity around thirtyfold.

<!-- S51 -->
So a dried mushroom
is not a weaker version
of a fresh one.

It's a stronger ingredient
than the mushroom ever was.

Which is why, in the middle of winter,
you can open a jar...

and the whole kitchen
smells of the forest.

<!-- S52 -->
And then there's the other advantage.

It's free.

Well.

Not counting the car.
The fuel. The boots.
The basket. The knife.

And several hours of your life
spent wandering about in the woods.

<!-- S53 -->
But the mushrooms themselves?

Those are free.

You leave home with an empty basket,
walk into a forest,
and come back carrying
several kilograms of food
that nobody grew.

<!-- S54 -->
One thing, though, and it matters.

Mushroom hunting isn't a competition
to see who brings home
the biggest basket.

And picking something
you can't confidently name
is a genuinely bad idea.

Some dangerous species
look a lot like edible ones.

<!-- S55 -->
Getting a blueberry wrong
is disappointing.

Getting a mushroom wrong
is a different category of mistake.

Which is why the knowledge
still travels the way it does.

From somebody who already has it,
standing next to you,
holding the thing.

<!-- S56 -->
So is mushroom hunting ridiculous?

Not really.

It's the last ordinary survivor
of something humans did
for almost all of our history.

Finding food,
instead of making it.

<!-- S57 -->
And it survived
because industry couldn't take it over.

Not for lack of money in it.

<!-- S58 -->
The mushroom won't grow
without its tree.

And the knowledge
won't fit on a label.

So it stayed where it started.

In forests.
In families.
Passed from one person to another
by pointing at the ground.

<!-- S59 -->
Today we can buy strawberries in winter,
and avocados from another continent.

And mushrooms, in a plastic tray.

But then you take a basket
and walk into a forest.

And you start looking at the world
differently.

<!-- S60 -->
You notice the trees.
The moss. The soil.
Where the water sits.

You start reading the ground
for places something might be hiding.

<!-- S61 -->
Then you see it.

A small brown cap under the leaves.

You pick it.

And you realise that a few hours from now,
it might be on your plate.

<!-- S62 -->
So the next time you see someone
disappearing into a forest with a basket,
don't assume they've lost their mind.

They're collecting dinner
from a shop with no door,
no shelves,
and nothing written on anything.

<!-- S63 -->
And if they know what they're doing,
it makes a very good one.
