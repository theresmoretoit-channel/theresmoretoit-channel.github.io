# Why Are Touchscreens in Cars So Dangerous?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you're driving
at fifty kilometres an hour.
About thirty miles an hour.

<!-- S02 -->
You want to turn the temperature down.
In an old car, you reach for a knob.

<!-- S03 -->
You don't need to look for it.
You just reach out, turn it,
and you're done.

<!-- S04 -->
Now do the same thing
in a modern car.

<!-- S05 -->
You look at the screen.
Find the climate menu.
Tap it.
Find the temperature.
Tap again.
Then look back at the road.

<!-- S06 -->
It sounds like a tiny difference.
But the car hasn't stopped.

<!-- S07 -->
At fifty kilometres an hour,
every second is fourteen metres.

<!-- S08 -->
Fourteen metres,
with nobody watching the road.

<!-- S09 -->
For decades, we made cars safer
with better brakes and airbags.

<!-- S10 -->
And then we put computers
right in front of the driver.

<!-- S11 -->
So how dangerous is that, really?

<!-- S12 -->
And if it's a problem,
why is almost every new car doing it?

<!-- S12b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S13 -->
Let's be fair to the screen first.

<!-- S14 -->
A map on a screen is better
than a paper atlas
unfolded over the steering wheel.
A reversing camera makes parking safer.

<!-- S15 -->
The trouble is something more specific.
Researchers call it
visual-manual interaction.

<!-- S16 -->
You have to look.
You have to understand what you see.
And then you have to touch
exactly the right spot.

<!-- S17 -->
A button has a shape.
A knob has resistance.
A switch has a position.

<!-- S18 -->
A touchscreen has...
just glass.

<!-- S19 -->
Your fingers don't know
where the virtual button is.
Only your eyes do.

<!-- S20 -->
And your eyes are exactly
what driving needs most.

<!-- S21 -->
Think about what a computer
on your desk asks you to do.

<!-- S22 -->
Look at the screen.
Move the mouse.
Click.
Read the result.

<!-- S23 -->
That's perfectly reasonable at a desk.
The desk isn't moving.

<!-- S24 -->
In a car, the screen wants your eyes.
So does the road.
And they can't both have them
at the same time.

<!-- S25 -->
So how long can a driver
look away?

<!-- S26 -->
There's no magic number.
But in 2013,
America's road safety regulator
published voluntary guidelines
for carmakers.

<!-- S27 -->
A task on a built-in screen
should be possible with glances
of no more than two seconds each,
and no more than twelve seconds in total.

<!-- S28 -->
Two seconds doesn't sound like much.
At a hundred kilometres an hour,
it's fifty-six metres.

<!-- S29 -->
It's not a line
between safe and dangerous.
It's a test
that a good interface should pass.

<!-- S30 -->
So how do real cars do?

<!-- S31 -->
In 2017, researchers
at the University of Utah
tested the infotainment systems
of thirty new cars,
for the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety.

<!-- S32 -->
They found huge differences
between cars.
Some systems were far more demanding
than others.

<!-- S33 -->
And the most demanding task
was entering a destination
into the navigation.

<!-- S34 -->
On average,
it took about forty seconds.

<!-- S35 -->
Forty seconds.
At fifty kilometres an hour,
that's more than half a kilometre.

<!-- S36 -->
Of course, nobody stares at a screen
for forty seconds straight.
The glances come in pieces.
A second here. Two seconds there.

<!-- S37 -->
But every one of those pieces
is a stretch of road
you didn't see.

<!-- S38 -->
In 2022, a Swedish car magazine
ran a simpler test.

<!-- S39 -->
They took eleven modern cars
and one Volvo from 2005.
The old Volvo had only buttons and knobs.

<!-- S40 -->
At a hundred and ten kilometres an hour,
drivers did four simple jobs.
Seat heating and temperature.
The radio.
The trip computer.
Dimming the lights.

<!-- S41 -->
The old Volvo:
about ten seconds.
Roughly three hundred metres.

<!-- S42 -->
The slowest modern car:
almost forty-five seconds.
Nearly one point four kilometres.

<!-- S43 -->
Same jobs. Same speed.
More than four times the distance.

<!-- S43b -->
But a brand-new Dacia
took about thirteen and a half seconds.
And so did a brand-new Volvo.

<!-- S44 -->
What mattered
wasn't how modern the car was.
It was how much attention
the interface demanded.

<!-- S45 -->
We already tell drivers
not to look at their phones.

<!-- S46 -->
A dashboard screen
has the same basic problem.
Your eyes are somewhere
other than the road.

<!-- S47 -->
That doesn't mean the two
are equally dangerous.
A car's screen usually sits closer
to your line of sight,
and some functions lock
while you're driving.

<!-- S48 -->
But the road doesn't care
why you looked away.

<!-- S49 -->
So why not just talk to the car?

<!-- S50 -->
Voice control helps a lot.
You don't have to look at anything.

<!-- S51 -->
But voice control
doesn't solve everything.
The same researchers in Utah
have spent years studying this.

<!-- S52 -->
Even with their eyes on the road,
drivers busy with a demanding
voice task react more slowly,
and notice less around them.

<!-- S53 -->
Hands-free doesn't mean
distraction-free.
It just means less of it.

<!-- S54 -->
So here's the strange part.
None of this is a secret.

<!-- S55 -->
Carmakers employ whole teams
of engineers who study
exactly this.

<!-- S56 -->
So why is almost every new car
moving its controls onto a screen?

<!-- S57 -->
Because from the carmaker's side,
a touchscreen makes a lot of sense.

<!-- S58 -->
Every physical button is a part.
It has to be designed, tested,
wired and fitted.
And then kept as a spare part
for years.

<!-- S59 -->
A car with a few dozen switches
needs a few dozen parts,
and the wiring to connect them.

<!-- S60 -->
A screen replaces all of them
with one part,
and software.

<!-- S61 -->
And software is flexible.
One screen can serve every model,
every trim level,
every market.

<!-- S62 -->
A different language?
Change the software.
A new feature?
Change the software.

<!-- S63 -->
Some cars even get
new functions years later,
over the air,
like a phone update.

<!-- S64 -->
And then there's the showroom.

<!-- S65 -->
A buyer spends a few minutes
in a new car before buying it.
A big, bright screen
looks like the future.

<!-- S66 -->
The problems show up later.
On a dark motorway,
when you just want the fan
a little lower.

<!-- S67 -->
That's the real trade-off.

<!-- S68 -->
The same technology
that makes a car cheaper to build
and easier to update
can make it harder to drive.

<!-- S69 -->
Nobody decided
to make cars more dangerous.
Each decision makes sense on its own.
The cost just lands on the driver.

<!-- S70 -->
And now, some of that
is starting to change.

<!-- S71 -->
Volkswagen moved many of its controls
to touch panels and sliders.
Drivers complained.

<!-- S72 -->
In 2025, its head of design
called it a mistake,
and promised physical buttons
for the most important functions.

<!-- S73 -->
And in Europe,
crash tests have started to count it too.

<!-- S74 -->
From 2026, Euro NCAP,
the European safety rating,
looks for five functions
on real, physical controls.

<!-- S75 -->
Indicators.
Hazard lights.
The horn.
The wipers.
And the emergency call.

<!-- S76 -->
A car that hides them in a screen
loses points
on its way to five stars.

<!-- S77 -->
Some of the oldest controls in the car
are now counted as safety features.

<!-- S78 -->
So what should a car interface
actually look like?

<!-- S79 -->
Probably not fewer computers.
Just computers that ask less
of the driver.

<!-- S80 -->
Things you need while driving
get physical controls,
the kind you can find without looking.

<!-- S81 -->
Information you need to see
goes where your eyes already are,
behind the steering wheel,
or projected onto the windscreen.

<!-- S82 -->
Voice handles what doesn't need your eyes.
And complicated settings
can wait until you've stopped.

<!-- S83 -->
Many new cars also watch the driver.
A camera can tell
when you've looked away too long.

<!-- S84 -->
That helps.
But it can't be the excuse.

<!-- S85 -->
"Don't worry, the car will watch you
while you look at the screen"
is the wrong way round.

<!-- S86 -->
The better answer is simpler.
Don't make the driver look at the screen
in the first place.

<!-- S87 -->
For decades, carmakers competed
on mechanical engineering.
Better engines.
Better brakes.

<!-- S88 -->
Now much of that competition
happens in software.
Bigger screens.
More menus.
More apps.

<!-- S89 -->
And that's impressive.
But a car isn't a laptop.

<!-- S90 -->
A laptop can ask
for all of your attention.
A car can't.

<!-- S91 -->
Because the most important task
in your car
isn't the infotainment system.

<!-- S92 -->
It's driving.
And its interface
is the whole world
outside your windscreen.

<!-- S93 -->
So maybe the best dashboard
isn't the one with the biggest screen.

<!-- S94 -->
It's the one you can use
without looking at it.

<!-- S95 -->
Because at a hundred kilometres an hour,
every second you look away
is twenty-eight metres.

<!-- S96 -->
And that's the price
of every glance.
