# Why We May Not Be Able to Live Forever

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine we had a technology
that could repair the human body.

We could remove damaged cells.

Replace worn-out tissue.

<!-- S02 -->
Repair damaged DNA.

Rejuvenate mitochondria.

We could even make some cells
biologically young again.

<!-- S03 -->
An eighty-year-old could have
the body of a forty-year-old.

So...

we've solved ageing.

Could we live forever?

<!-- S04 -->
Maybe not.

Because there is one part of the body
that makes this much more complicated.

The brain.

<!-- S05 -->
And one recent mathematical model
took a perfectly maintained
human body and did the maths.

It might eventually reach
somewhere around a hundred
and fifty years.

<!-- S06 -->
But is that really a hard limit?

And why would the brain be the problem?

<!-- S07 -->
Our bodies are constantly
repairing themselves.

Cells die.

New cells replace them.

<!-- S08 -->
Our skin renews itself.

Our blood cells are
constantly replaced.

The lining of our intestines
can renew itself
incredibly quickly.

<!-- S09 -->
So why can't the whole body
just keep doing this forever?

Because the repair process
isn't perfect.

<!-- S10 -->
DNA gets damaged.

Mitochondria wear out.

Waste products accumulate
inside cells.

<!-- S11 -->
Some cells stop working properly,
but don't die.

These are called senescent cells.

<!-- S12 -->
And over time, cells also change
the way they switch genes on and off.

These are epigenetic changes.

<!-- S13 -->
It's a little like an operating system
that has been running for decades.

It still works.

But over time, bugs accumulate.

<!-- S14 -->
Old configurations remain.

Files become corrupted.

And the system becomes
less reliable.

<!-- S15 -->
Our bodies aren't computers.

But the analogy is not
completely wrong.

<!-- S16 -->
Now imagine that science
gets really good at this.

We can remove senescent cells.

Repair damaged DNA.

<!-- S17 -->
Rejuvenate mitochondria.

Restore stem cells.

And reset some of the epigenetic
changes associated with ageing.

<!-- S18 -->
We wouldn't just be treating
individual diseases anymore.

We could potentially keep the body
in a younger state.

<!-- S19 -->
Instead of letting the body
deteriorate for eighty years
and then trying to repair it,
we'd maintain it continuously.

<!-- S20 -->
Like servicing a machine.

Replace worn-out parts.

Repair damage.

Keep everything working.

So why couldn't we do
the same thing with a human?

<!-- S21 -->
Maybe a body is a machine.

But not every part of a machine
can simply be swapped.

Some are easy to replace.

Others are not.

<!-- S22 -->
And the most difficult one
is the brain.

The human brain contains roughly
eighty-six billion neurons.

<!-- S23 -->
And those neurons are connected
by an enormous network of synapses.

That network contains a large part
of what makes you you.

<!-- S24 -->
Your memories.

Your skills.

Your personality.

Everything you've learned
during your life.

<!-- S25 -->
So we can't simply say:

"This neuron is old.
Let's replace it."

With a skin cell,
that's relatively simple.

With a neuron,
it could be a very big deal.

<!-- S26 -->
Because the new neuron might not have
exactly the same connections.

And if you change enough
neurons and synapses,
you may change the structure
of the brain itself.

<!-- S27 -->
Most neurons in our brains
stay with us for decades.

And while they're there,
damage slowly accumulates.

Including mutations in their DNA.

<!-- S28 -->
Our cells have remarkably good
systems for repairing DNA.

But they're not perfect.

<!-- S29 -->
A cell that survives for a hundred
or a hundred and fifty years
has a lot more time
to accumulate mistakes
than one that survives
for only a few months.

<!-- S30 -->
And eventually, enough damage
could affect how the cell works.

And this is where that number
comes in.

<!-- S31 -->
A recent mathematical model
asked a fascinating question.

What happens if we remove most
of the ordinary causes of ageing?

<!-- S32 -->
The answer was surprising.

The model estimated a median
human lifespan of roughly
a hundred and fifty-six years.

<!-- S33 -->
Median, not maximum.

In the model, half of the population
would reach beyond a hundred
and fifty-six years.

<!-- S34 -->
But this does not mean
there is a biological timer
that kills us at a hundred
and fifty-six.

And it doesn't prove
that humans can't live longer.

<!-- S35 -->
It's a mathematical model
based on a particular
set of assumptions.

<!-- S36 -->
But what happens
when we fix almost everything else?

<!-- S37 -->
Some cells are extremely
difficult to replace
without changing the information
they contain.

<!-- S38 -->
The model points at two of them.

The heart.

And the brain.

<!-- S39 -->
A heart is a pump.

If we ever learn to grow one,
we could simply fit a new one
and lose nothing.

<!-- S39b -->
We have already done a version of this.

People have lived with an artificial heart
that has no pulse at all,
its speed set from outside the body.

<!-- S39c -->
Not for long. Weeks, sometimes months.

And not without complications.

But the principle held.

Their blood kept moving,
and they were still themselves.

<!-- S40 -->
The brain is the one place
where replacing the part
means replacing the information.

<!-- S41 -->
Let's imagine that science
gets even better.

We can repair the DNA
inside every neuron.

Remove damaged proteins.

Restore mitochondria.

<!-- S42 -->
Fix every problem
we know how to fix.

Maybe we could keep the brain
biologically young too.

<!-- S43 -->
But that leads to
a much stranger question.

What does it actually mean
to repair a brain?

<!-- S44 -->
Think about a computer.

If you replace a damaged transistor,
the data stays intact.

<!-- S45 -->
Replace the hard drive
with an exact copy,
and the data is still there.

<!-- S46 -->
But what happens if you slowly
replace every component?

At some point, none of the original
hardware remains.

Is it still the same computer?

And what about a human being?

<!-- S47 -->
Let's take this to an extreme.

Imagine replacing a different
one percent of your neurons
every year.

<!-- S48 -->
The new neurons are perfect copies.

Same function.

Same connections.

Same information.

<!-- S49 -->
After one hundred years,
none of your original neurons
would remain.

But the process happened
so gradually that you never noticed.

<!-- S50 -->
Would you still be the same person?

Most people would probably say yes.

<!-- S51 -->
There was never a moment
when you suddenly disappeared.

<!-- S52 -->
Now change the thought experiment.

Do exactly the same thing...

but in one second.

Is the result different?

<!-- S53 -->
And what if we could create
a perfect copy of your entire brain?

It would have your memories.

Your personality.

Your experiences.

<!-- S54 -->
And it would look at you and say:

"I'm still me."

Would that copy actually be you?

Or would it simply be a new person
who happens to have
all your memories?

<!-- S55 -->
At this point, we're no longer
talking only about biology.

We're talking about
personal identity.

<!-- S56 -->
And that's why I think
extending healthspan
is much more interesting
than simply extending lifespan.

<!-- S57 -->
We don't need to live
for a thousand years.

We don't even need to live
for two hundred.

<!-- S58 -->
Imagine something much simpler.

You reach forty.

You're still physically capable.

Then fifty.

Still healthy.

<!-- S59 -->
Sixty.

Still active.

Seventy.

Still independent.

Eighty.

Still capable of living
your own life.

<!-- S60 -->
What if we could slow biological
ageing enough to make that normal?

That could matter far more
than simply pushing
human lifespan higher.

<!-- S61 -->
Because age gives us
something valuable.

Something we don't have at twenty.

Experience.

<!-- S62 -->
A twenty-year-old may have
plenty of energy.

But they are still working out
what to do with it.

<!-- S63 -->
At forty, you've accumulated
twenty years of experience.

At sixty, perhaps forty.

And that experience
can be incredibly valuable.

<!-- S64 -->
You may understand
your profession better.

You may understand people better.

You may understand yourself better.

You may simply make better decisions.

<!-- S65 -->
But that's exactly when the biological
hardware starts slowing down.

If we could keep
the hardware working...

we could keep the experience.

<!-- S66 -->
Modern life doesn't always fit
very well with human biology.

We often spend decades
learning, building careers,
and figuring out what we want.

<!-- S67 -->
And by the time we're ready,
biology may already be
running out of time.

<!-- S68 -->
Slowing biological ageing
could give us something
more important than extra years.

We'd gain freedom
over the order of our lives.

<!-- S69 -->
So how long could we actually keep living?

Maybe the future isn't a world
where humans become immortal.

<!-- S70 -->
Maybe it's something
more interesting.

A world where the boundaries
between youth, middle age
and old age start to blur.

<!-- S71 -->
A hundred-year-old person might not
resemble today's hundred-year-old
at all.

<!-- S72 -->
And if we eventually learn
how to repair the brain itself,
even a hundred and fifty-six years
might not be a meaningful limit.

<!-- S73 -->
Because that number depends on what
we believe we can repair.

<!-- S74 -->
And technology has a strange habit.

When we don't know
how to fix something,
we tend to think
it's a fundamental limit.

Until someone figures out
how to fix it.

<!-- S75 -->
So maybe the real question isn't:

"How long can a human life last?"

<!-- S76 -->
Maybe it's:

"How long can we keep
a human being human?"

<!-- S77 -->
And that may be a much more
interesting question
than immortality.
