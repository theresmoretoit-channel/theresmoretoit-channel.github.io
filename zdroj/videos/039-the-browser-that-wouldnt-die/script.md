# Internet Explorer 6 — The Browser That Wouldn't Die

<!-- S01 -->
Internet Explorer 6
was dead.

At least,
Microsoft said it was.

<!-- S02 -->
The company had spent years
telling people to upgrade.

Firefox was better.

Chrome was better.

Internet Explorer 7 was better.

Internet Explorer 8 was better.

<!-- S03 -->
There was a countdown
on Microsoft's own website.

Percent by percent.

Country by country.

<!-- S04 -->
And yet, in 2010,
millions of computers
were still running
Internet Explorer 6.

<!-- S05 -->
Not because their owners liked it.

Not because Microsoft
wanted them to.

Because something they needed
depended on it.

<!-- S06 -->
And that is how a browser
stops being software.

It becomes infrastructure.

<!-- S07 -->
The strange thing is,
Microsoft knew.

They were trying to kill
Internet Explorer 6.

And at the same time,
they were selling companies
a way to keep it alive.

<!-- S08 -->
This is part three.

Part one was how one browser
became that powerful.

Part two was what it did
to the people building for it.

Both are linked below.

<!-- S09 -->
This one is the harder question.

What happens
when obsolete software
becomes infrastructure?

<!-- S10 -->
Start in January,
2010.

Internet Explorer 6
is nine years old.

Firefox is six.

Chrome is two.

And more than one browser in ten
is still Internet Explorer 6.

<!-- S11 -->
Then Google discovers
that somebody is inside
its own network.

<!-- S12 -->
Not a defaced website.

Source code.

Employee accounts.

The Gmail accounts
of Chinese human rights activists.

<!-- S13 -->
And at least twenty
other companies
had been opened the same way.

<!-- S14 -->
The way in was a flaw
in Internet Explorer
that nobody had fixed,
because nobody knew it was there.

The attack used it
against Internet Explorer 6.

<!-- S15 -->
The researchers who took it apart
gave the operation a name.

Aurora.

And Microsoft broke
its own monthly schedule
to ship the fix,
three weeks early.

<!-- S16 -->
And then something happened
that had not happened before.

Governments told their citizens
to stop using a browser.

<!-- S17 -->
Germany's federal
security office
said use something else.

France said the same.

Firefox.

Chrome.

Anything but this.

<!-- S18 -->
In Germany, people downloaded
three hundred thousand copies
of Firefox
over a single weekend.

<!-- S19 -->
A few weeks later,
Google stopped waiting.

From the first of March,
Google Docs and Google Sites
would no longer work properly
in Internet Explorer 6.

<!-- S20 -->
So by the spring
of 2010,
nobody was arguing anymore.

The browser was a security problem.

Two governments said so.

Google said so.

<!-- S21 -->
And it still didn't go away.

That is the part
worth explaining.

<!-- S22 -->
The usual explanation
is Windows XP.

Old operating system,
old browser,
nobody upgraded,
end of story.

That explanation is wrong.

<!-- S23 -->
Internet Explorer 7
came out in 2006.

It ran on Windows XP.

<!-- S24 -->
Internet Explorer 8
came out in 2009.

It ran on Windows XP too.

That is where it stopped.
Internet Explorer 9,
in 2011,
would not run on Windows XP
at all.

<!-- S25 -->
Microsoft even pushed
Internet Explorer 7
out through Windows Update,
as a critical update.

If you had automatic updates on,
it came to you.

<!-- S26 -->
So from 2006
onwards,
a machine running Windows XP
could have a newer browser.

That is four years
before anybody
had heard of Aurora.

<!-- S27 -->
Which means that every machine
still on Internet Explorer 6
in 2010
was not stuck there
by its operating system.

<!-- S28 -->
The operating system
wasn't the reason.

The application was.

<!-- S29 -->
Somewhere inside that company
there was a piece of software
that only worked
in Internet Explorer 6.

<!-- S30 -->
Not a website.

An application.

It ran on ActiveX,
so it could reach the file system,
a printer, a scanner,
a database, a card reader.

<!-- S31 -->
It had been written
when Internet Explorer 6
was the only target
anybody cared about.

<!-- S32 -->
And it worked.

That is the awkward part.

It worked every morning,
and people ran their day on it.

<!-- S33 -->
Change the browser,
and you are not
upgrading software.

You are betting
that something nobody
fully understands
still behaves the same way.

<!-- S34 -->
Microsoft understood that
better than anybody.

<!-- S35 -->
Because when it shipped
Internet Explorer 7
through Windows Update,
its biggest customers
panicked.

<!-- S36 -->
Ten thousand machines.

One automatic update.

Every internal application
that had ever assumed
Internet Explorer 6.

<!-- S37 -->
So Microsoft did something
remarkable.

It published a tool
whose only purpose
was to stop its own new browser
from being installed.

<!-- S38 -->
It was called
the Blocker Toolkit.

You ran it,
and it wrote a setting
that told Windows Update
never to offer
Internet Explorer 7 again.

<!-- S39 -->
There was no expiry date.

<!-- S40 -->
Then Internet Explorer 8
came out,
and Microsoft published
a blocker for that.

Then one for nine.

Then one for eleven.

<!-- S41 -->
That is worth saying twice.

The company that wanted you
to upgrade
also shipped the switch
for not upgrading.

Four times.

<!-- S42 -->
And it wasn't an accident.

It was demand.

Microsoft was not fighting
its customers' inertia.

It was supplying it.

<!-- S43 -->
But blocking an update
only buys time.

Sooner or later
the machine itself dies,
and somebody has to buy
a new one.

<!-- S44 -->
And after 2009,
a new machine
meant Windows 7.

<!-- S45 -->
Windows 7 would not run
Internet Explorer 6.

Not with a blocker.

Not with a setting.

The browser was simply gone.

<!-- S46 -->
So the company had a choice.

Stay on an operating system
that Microsoft would stop
patching in a few years.

Or replace the application.

<!-- S47 -->
Microsoft offered
a third option.

And this is the part
that is hard to believe
until you see
the product page.

<!-- S48 -->
It was called
Microsoft Enterprise Desktop
Virtualization.

Large companies bought it
as part of a subscription.

<!-- S49 -->
What it did
was run a complete copy
of Windows XP
inside Windows 7.

Invisibly.

In the background.

<!-- S50 -->
And inside that hidden
Windows XP,
it ran Internet Explorer 6.

<!-- S51 -->
The second version
went further.

An administrator gave it
a list of addresses.

Anything on that list
opened in the old browser,
inside the hidden machine.

<!-- S52 -->
The employee clicked a link
and a window appeared.

They never knew
they had just started
an entire operating system
to look at one page.

<!-- S53 -->
So look at what Microsoft
was doing in the same year.

Telling the world
that Internet Explorer 6
had to go.

And selling its largest customers
a product whose whole purpose
was keeping it running.

<!-- S54 -->
Microsoft was killing
Internet Explorer 6.

And Microsoft was also selling you
a way to keep it alive.

<!-- S55 -->
And that isn't hypocrisy.

That is what a dependency
looks like from the inside.

Even the company
that made the thing
can't simply take it away.

<!-- S56 -->
It didn't stop there, either.

A few years later,
Internet Explorer 11
shipped with compatibility modes
for older sites,

one of them behaving
like Internet Explorer 8.

<!-- S56b -->
It was there
for the old internal applications
and the ActiveX controls
inside them.

Microsoft's own documentation
said many of those
would simply keep working.

<!-- S57 -->
The old browser
was no longer
a different machine.

It was a setting
inside the new one.

<!-- S58 -->
Up to here
this sounds like a story
about companies.

Bad software.

Cautious managers.

Budgets.

<!-- S59 -->
In South Korea
it was a story
about a country.

<!-- S60 -->
In the late nineties,
Korean regulations required
online payments to use
a particular kind of encryption.

No browser supported it.

<!-- S61 -->
The only way to add it
was ActiveX.

And the only browser
with ActiveX
was Internet Explorer.

<!-- S62 -->
So banking ran on ActiveX.

Government sites
ran on ActiveX.

Shopping ran on ActiveX.

Anything involving money
or identity
arrived with a plug-in
you had to install first.

<!-- S63 -->
A whole country's online life
now depended
on one company's browser.

Not by accident.

By regulation.

<!-- S64 -->
The requirement was lifted
in 2014.

The websites kept
the plug-ins anyway.

<!-- S65 -->
That is the difference
between a bad decision
and infrastructure.

A bad decision
can be reversed.

Infrastructure
has to be replaced.

<!-- S66 -->
And you could see
the same thing on a map.

In January
2012,
Internet Explorer 6
finally fell below one percent
in the United States.

<!-- S67 -->
In China, that same winter,
a quarter of all browsers
were still Internet Explorer 6.

In the spring before,
it had been a third.

<!-- S68 -->
Same web.

Same browser.

Same winter.

Because the question was never
which browser is better.

It was what somebody
had already built on top of it.

<!-- S69 -->
And some of what
had been built on it
was not small.

In September
2013,
eighty-five percent
of the computers
in Britain's national
health service
were still running Windows XP.

<!-- S70 -->
Out of roughly
eight hundred thousand.

<!-- S71 -->
Support for Windows XP
was going to end
in April
2014.

<!-- S72 -->
So the British government
signed a deal with Microsoft.

Five and a half million pounds
for twelve more months
of security updates
across the whole public sector.

<!-- S73 -->
Not to upgrade anything.

To buy one more year
of not upgrading.

<!-- S74 -->
That is what a dependency costs
once somebody
puts a number on it.

<!-- S75 -->
And the reason that year
was worth five and a half
million pounds
is the same everywhere.

<!-- S76 -->
Before you can replace
the old thing,
you have to find out
what it does.

All of it.

Including the parts
nobody wrote down
and nobody remembers.

<!-- S77 -->
You don't just inherit
the software.

You inherit every assumption
anybody ever made about it.

And after ten years
nobody knows
what all of them are.

<!-- S78 -->
So how did it actually end?

Not with a deadline.

<!-- S79 -->
In December
2011,
Microsoft announced
that it would stop asking.

<!-- S80 -->
From then on,
Windows would upgrade
Internet Explorer by itself,
to the newest version
the machine could run.

On Windows XP,
that was eight.

<!-- S81 -->
You could still say no.

Most people didn't,
because most people
never knew there was
a question.

<!-- S82 -->
That winter,
Internet Explorer 6
went under one percent
in the United States.

<!-- S83 -->
Support for Windows XP
ended in April
2014,
exactly as promised.

Two years after

<!-- S84 -->
And even that line
didn't hold.

Three weeks later
a serious flaw turned up
in Internet Explorer,
and Microsoft patched it
on Windows XP anyway.

<!-- S85 -->
Internet Explorer 6
was still getting
security updates
somewhere in the world
until January
2016.

Fourteen years
after it shipped.

<!-- S86 -->
Internet Explorer itself
was finally switched off
in June
2022.

<!-- S87 -->
And Microsoft Edge

Microsoft has promised

2029.

<!-- S88 -->
Twenty-eight years
after the browser shipped.

Nineteen years
after it was declared dead.

<!-- S89 -->
So that is the answer
to the question
we started with.

<!-- S90 -->
A browser becomes a platform.

A platform becomes
a dependency.

A dependency becomes
infrastructure.

Infrastructure becomes
technical debt.

And technical debt
becomes somebody's job.

<!-- S91 -->
Usually not the job
of the person who built it.

<!-- S92 -->
And once enough things
depend on something,
being better is no longer
enough to replace it.

Everybody else
has to move as well.

<!-- S93 -->
Which is the strangest thing
about technology.

Sometimes the thing that wins
isn't the thing that survives.

Sometimes it becomes
the thing everyone
has to survive.

<!-- S94 -->
Internet Explorer 6
didn't die
when Microsoft
stopped making it.

<!-- S95 -->
It died when enough
of the world
stopped depending on it.

<!-- S96 -->
And that took
a very long time.
