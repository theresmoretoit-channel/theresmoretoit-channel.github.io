# What Would It Take for Russia to Change?
**Script v0.6**

<!-- S01 -->
In May 2008, Russia got a new president.
He was forty-two,
and a lawyer by training.

<!-- S02 -->
A few months before taking office,
he told an economic forum in Siberia
that freedom is better than unfreedom.

<!-- S03 -->
He talked about fighting corruption,
independent courts,
and modernising the economy.

<!-- S04 -->
Three months after he took office,
Russian troops were fighting in Georgia.

<!-- S05 -->
And in 2011, he announced
that he would step aside,
so that the man he had replaced
could return to the job.

<!-- S06 -->
His name was Dmitry Medvedev.
The man before him and after him
was Vladimir Putin.

<!-- S07 -->
Russia has already tried
changing the face at the top.
On its own, that did not
change the system.

<!-- S08 -->
In the previous video, we looked at why
Russia keeps choosing confrontation,
even when it makes the country weaker.

<!-- S09 -->
The short version:
the pattern is not Russia's destiny.
It keeps returning because it serves
a political system
that needs an enemy abroad.

<!-- S10 -->
So what would it actually take
to change that system?
And how long would it take?

<!-- S10b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S11 -->
Start with the most tempting answer.
Wait for a new leader.

<!-- S12 -->
The trouble is
that a new leader inherits the old tools.

<!-- S13 -->
In October 1993, President Yeltsin,
the man who had faced down
the Soviet hardliners,
sent tanks to fire on his own parliament.

<!-- S14 -->
Two months later, Russia adopted
a new constitution.
It gave the president enormous power.

<!-- S15 -->
It was written for a reformer
who wanted to push reforms through.
It works just as well
for anyone who comes after him.

<!-- S16 -->
In 2020, it was amended again.
The change allows Putin
to stay in office until 2036.

<!-- S17 -->
That is the deeper Russian pattern.
Change has usually come from the top.
And what the top gives,
the top can take back.

<!-- S18 -->
Alexander the Second freed the serfs
in 1861.
After he was assassinated,
his son rolled back
many of his other reforms.

<!-- S19 -->
Khrushchev's thaw
was followed by Brezhnev's freeze.
Gorbachev's openness
ended with the collapse of the state.

<!-- S20 -->
So Russia is not a country
that has never tried to change.
It is a country where change
has rarely been built to last.

<!-- S21 -->
What would make it last?

<!-- S22 -->
The first condition sounds dull.
Power would have to be able to lose.

<!-- S23 -->
In Russian criminal courts,
fewer than one defendant in a hundred
is acquitted.

<!-- S24 -->
That number alone proves little.
But Russian courts also rarely rule
against the state in political cases.
The judge looks less like a referee,
and more like part of the state's team.

<!-- S25 -->
Since the Soviet Union collapsed,
Russia has never had a national government
lose a genuinely competitive election
and peacefully hand over power
to its political opponents.

<!-- S26 -->
That is the real test.
Not whether elections exist,
but whether the people in charge
can lose one and go home.

<!-- S26b -->
Some former dictatorships
have passed that test.
How they got there
is the subject of an earlier video.

<!-- S27 -->
Why does this matter for war and peace?

<!-- S28 -->
Because a leader who can lose power
has to think about the cost of mistakes.
A leader who cannot
only has to think about loyalty.

<!-- S29 -->
The second condition is about money.

<!-- S30 -->
Norway also lives off oil and gas.
But its oil money goes into a fund
now worth more than two trillion dollars.

<!-- S31 -->
Over time, the budget is meant to spend
roughly what the fund is expected to earn
in real terms,
while leaving room for bad years.

<!-- S32 -->
Oil alone does not make
a country authoritarian.
What matters is who controls the money,
and whether anyone can check them.

<!-- S33 -->
In Russia, the same small circle
controls the pipes, the budget,
and the courts that could question them.

<!-- S34 -->
Breaking that link
would mean independent courts,
real property rights,
and businesses that can say no
to the state.

<!-- S35 -->
The third condition
is the one closest to the war.
Russia would have to treat
its neighbours' borders as final.

<!-- S36 -->
Not as a new principle.
As one it has already signed.

<!-- S37 -->
In 1994, as Ukraine gave up
its nuclear weapons,
Russia gave it security assurances.
Among them, a promise to respect
its independence and existing borders.

<!-- S38 -->
In 1997, the two countries signed a treaty
that confirmed those borders again.

<!-- S39 -->
There is a precedent
for a former great power
letting go of lost territory.

<!-- S40 -->
After the Second World War,
Germany lost about a quarter
of its pre-war land.

<!-- S41 -->
In 1970, West Germany signed a treaty
accepting its border with Poland.
In 1990, a reunited Germany
confirmed that it was final.

<!-- S42 -->
Today, Germany is
Poland's largest trading partner.
It gave up a claim
and gained a neighbour.

<!-- S43 -->
The fourth condition is people.

<!-- S44 -->
Since 2022, hundreds of thousands
of Russians have left,
many of them programmers,
scientists, and engineers.

<!-- S45 -->
A modern economy cannot be ordered
into existence from the Kremlin.
It needs people who want to stay
and can build without permission.

<!-- S46 -->
So how long would all this take?

<!-- S47 -->
Some things could change in months.

<!-- S48 -->
A new government could stop the war,
release more than a thousand people
recognised as political prisoners,
and let independent media
work again.

<!-- S49 -->
Other things would take years.

<!-- S50 -->
Independent courts need judges
who were not trained to obey.
Real elections need parties
that have had time to grow.

<!-- S51 -->
And some things take a generation.

<!-- S52 -->
In a 2018 survey,
two thirds of Russians said
they regretted the collapse
of the Soviet Union.

<!-- S53 -->
Expectations about power
do not change with a new law.
They change when people see
that the new rules hold.

<!-- S54 -->
Trust between neighbours is slower still.

<!-- S55 -->
In 1970, on the day of that treaty,
the West German chancellor knelt
at the memorial to the Warsaw Ghetto.

<!-- S56 -->
Twenty years later, after reunification,
the border was confirmed for good.
Fourteen years after that,
Poland joined the European Union.

<!-- S57 -->
Thirty-four years from the first gesture.
And that was with
a democratic Germany on one side.

<!-- S58 -->
And there is something
we cannot know at all.
When change will begin,
or what direction it will take.

<!-- S59 -->
The next leader might not be
a reformer.

<!-- S60 -->
In June 2023, a private army
marched towards Moscow
and got within a few hundred kilometres
before turning back.

<!-- S61 -->
Its leader was not trying
to make Russia more peaceful.
He wanted the generals replaced
and the war fought harder.

<!-- S62 -->
A weaker regime does not automatically
produce a better one.

<!-- S63 -->
But history is full of surprises
in the other direction too.

<!-- S64 -->
In 1969, a Soviet dissident
wrote an essay with a question
for a title.

<!-- S65 -->
Will the Soviet Union
survive until 1984?

<!-- S66 -->
Few experts took
the prediction seriously.
The Soviet Union looked permanent.

<!-- S67 -->
It lasted until 1991.

<!-- S68 -->
So is Russia a prisoner
of its own history?

<!-- S69 -->
Its history explains
why the old pattern keeps coming back.
Power at the top.
Reforms that can be taken away.

<!-- S70 -->
But the same history also contains
the freed serfs, the thaw,
and the open years.
Moments when Russia
tried something different.

<!-- S71 -->
What it has rarely contained
are institutions strong enough
to keep those choices alive.

<!-- S72 -->
That is what would have to be different
this time.
Not a better man at the top.
A system that does not depend on one.

<!-- S73 -->
Nobody can say when that might happen,
or whether it will.

<!-- S74 -->
The past can explain
why a pattern keeps returning.

<!-- S75 -->
It cannot decide
whether it returns again.
