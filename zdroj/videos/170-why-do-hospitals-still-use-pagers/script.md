<!-- S01 -->
In 2026,
a hospital doctor starts a shift
with a smartphone in one pocket.

<!-- S02 -->
And clipped to the belt,
something from the 1980s.
A small plastic box
that can only beep
and show a line of text.

<!-- S03 -->
A pager.

<!-- S04 -->
That little box
has been in hospitals for a long time.
In 1949, an inventor called Al Gross
patented what he called
a telephone pager.

<!-- S05 -->
The next year,
New York's Jewish Hospital
started using his system
to reach its doctors.
That was more than twenty years
before the first mobile phone call.

<!-- S06 -->
And it never really left.
In a survey published in 2017
of American hospital clinicians,
almost eight in ten of those who answered
had been given a pager by their hospital.

<!-- S07 -->
For about half of all who answered,
it was the main way
messages about patients reached them.

<!-- S08 -->
In 2019, the British government
ordered the NHS to stop using pagers
for everyday messages
by the end of 2021.
There were about
a hundred and thirty thousand of them.

<!-- S09 -->
In the summer of 2026,
they were still in use.

<!-- S10 -->
So why won't it go away?

<!-- S11 -->
The answer starts with one strange fact
about how a pager works.

<!-- S12 -->
It never says anything.
A typical hospital pager
has no transmitter.
It only listens.

<!-- S13 -->
What many of them listen for
is a code from the 1970s.
In the mid-1970s,
the British Post Office
brought together pager makers
from Britain, Europe and Japan
to agree on one way of paging.

<!-- S14 -->
The code they worked on
is still on the air today,
named after the group: POCSAG.
Every pager has its own number.
The network sends that number,
followed by the message.

<!-- S15 -->
Your phone works differently.
The network keeps track
of roughly which area it's in.

<!-- S16 -->
When a call comes in,
the network calls out across that area
to find it.
Engineers even call that paging.

<!-- S17 -->
Then your phone answers.
And from that moment on,
it's a two-way conversation.

<!-- S18 -->
A one-way pager network
never gets an answer.
It doesn't know where you are,
and it doesn't try to find out.

<!-- S19 -->
So it broadcasts every message
across its whole coverage area.
Every pager listens.
Only the one with your number reacts.

<!-- S20 -->
That one decision explains
nearly everything
that makes the pager hard to replace.
Start with the battery.

<!-- S21 -->
Transmitting is expensive.
This kind of pager never does it.
Its receiver is simple,
and it sleeps most of the time.
It wakes only for its own short slot
in each cycle of the broadcast.

<!-- S22 -->
So one small battery
can keep it going for weeks.
A phone needs a charger every day.

<!-- S23 -->
Then there's the building.

<!-- S24 -->
Hospitals are hard places for radio.
Thick concrete. Basements.
Rooms shielded on purpose,
around scanners and X-ray machines.

<!-- S25 -->
A phone needs a link
in both directions.
It has to hear the mast,
and the mast has to hear the phone.

<!-- S26 -->
The hard half is the phone's.
A small, battery-powered transmitter
has to reach a mast
that may be kilometres away.

<!-- S27 -->
A pager only needs the other half.
That transmitter can be powerful,
and mounted high up.
And it sends slowly,
a few thousand bits a second at most.

<!-- S28 -->
A slower signal can hold up better
in noisy conditions.
Together with power and frequency,
that often lets a paging network reach
places where phones struggle.

<!-- S29 -->
The third advantage
only shows up on the worst day.

<!-- S30 -->
When disaster strikes,
everyone reaches for a phone at once.
And every call puts another demand
on a network that's already overloaded.

<!-- S31 -->
After Hurricane Katrina in 2005,
Verizon said that where phones
still worked at all,
the circuits were often jammed.

<!-- S32 -->
Pager networks have limits too,
and their masts can fail.
But a thousand people receiving
the same message
doesn't mean a thousand connections.
It's still just one broadcast.

<!-- S33 -->
That's the fourth advantage,
the one hospitals care about most.

<!-- S34 -->
When a patient's heart stops,
a whole team has to come running.
Doctors, an anaesthetist,
several nurses.

<!-- S35 -->
In many British hospitals,
someone dials 2222.
And every pager on that team
goes off in the same second.

<!-- S36 -->
No code to enter.
No app to open.
No group to put together first.

<!-- S37 -->
For decades, that was the whole case
for the pager.
It can reach people
across the building,
all at once,
without needing them to answer.

<!-- S38 -->
But that same decision
has a price.

<!-- S39 -->
Because the pager can't talk,
it can't confirm the message arrived.
The nurse who sends a page
doesn't know if the doctor saw it.

<!-- S40 -->
And the doctor can't reply.
They have to find a phone
and call back...
by which time the nurse
may have moved on.

<!-- S41 -->
That's the first price.
The second is worse.

<!-- S42 -->
A message sent everywhere
can be heard everywhere.

<!-- S43 -->
Some pager systems
still send their messages as plain text.
Encrypted paging exists.
But these systems don't use it.

<!-- S44 -->
With a cheap radio receiver,
anyone in range
can pick those messages up.

<!-- S45 -->
In 2018, researchers in Vancouver
found hospital pages on the air
with patients' names,
diagnoses and room numbers.

<!-- S46 -->
In August 2026,
the BBC found that the NHS service
that matches donated organs
had been sending transplant patients'
names, birth dates and organ details
the same way.

<!-- S47 -->
It apologised.
And stopped sending
patients' personal details over the air.

<!-- S48 -->
You'd think that settles it.
Hospitals now have secure messaging apps.
Two-way, encrypted,
with a tick when your message is seen.

<!-- S49 -->
And for most messages,
the apps are winning.
Could you check the blood results
in bed twelve?
Can this patient move to the ward?
Those are conversations.
A phone is better at conversations.

<!-- S50 -->
In 2022, Cambridge University Hospitals
made exactly that switch.

<!-- S51 -->
It upgraded its Wi-Fi
to cover the whole site.
For the first week,
messages went to the app and the pagers.
After that, everyday pages
dropped almost to nothing.

<!-- S52 -->
But three kinds of call
were never meant to move.
Cardiac arrest.
Major trauma.
Fire.

<!-- S53 -->
Even the old pager system
wasn't switched off.
It would stay in place
until a resilience review
showed it was safe to remove.

<!-- S54 -->
These are the calls
where nobody has time to reply.
Where the message has to reach
a whole team at once,
wherever they are.

<!-- S55 -->
And that's the real answer.
A hospital isn't sending
one kind of message.
It's sending two.

<!-- S56 -->
Most of them are questions.
A question has to arrive,
be seen,
and get an answer.
A phone does all three.

<!-- S57 -->
A few of them are alarms.
An alarm has to reach a whole group,
right now.
Nobody needs to reply.
They need to run.

<!-- S58 -->
Those are different jobs.
So it makes sense
to use different tools.

<!-- S59 -->
It isn't that the pager is unbreakable.
A modern hospital can have backup power,
spare Wi-Fi,
and emergency systems of its own.

<!-- S60 -->
What the pager offers
is another channel.
Simpler, different,
and one that can fail in different ways.

<!-- S61 -->
For an alarm,
that matters more than cleverness.
You want the channel
that doesn't fail at the same moment
as everything else.

<!-- S62 -->
For more than seventy years,
that channel has been a little box
that can't say a word.

<!-- S63 -->
Which leaves an awkward question.

<!-- S64 -->
Outside hospitals,
almost everyone has left the pager behind.
Japan switched off
its last commercial pager service
in 2019.

<!-- S65 -->
Fewer customers mean fewer networks,
fewer companies making the devices,
fewer people who know how to fix them.

<!-- S66 -->
If that goes on,
hospitals may become
some of the last customers
of a whole technology.
And a network with few customers
is hard to keep running.

<!-- S67 -->
So whatever replaces the pager
for those last few calls
can't just be a better phone.

<!-- S68 -->
It has to keep the pager's oldest trick.
Reach everyone at once.
Keep working when other things stop.
And say as little as possible.

<!-- S69 -->
So here's a question for you.
In an emergency,
would you rather get a message
you can answer...
or one designed to reach you
when everything else fails?
Let me know in the comments.
