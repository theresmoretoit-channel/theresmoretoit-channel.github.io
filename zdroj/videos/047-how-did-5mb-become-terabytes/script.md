<!-- S01 -->
In 1956, IBM shipped the first hard drive.
It held about five megabytes.

<!-- S02 -->
The machine was taller than the person
operating it, and with the compressor
that kept it running,
it weighed more than a ton.

<!-- S03 -->
Five megabytes is one photograph
from a modern phone.
Today you can hold a drive in one hand
that stores nearly ten million times more.

<!-- S04 -->
So what happened in between?

<!-- S05 -->
The obvious answer is that we got better
at building small machines.
That answer isn't wrong.
It's just the boring half.

<!-- S06 -->
Because the machine got about
a thousand times lighter.
The storage got about
a billion times denser.

<!-- S07 -->
A thousand against a billion.
Everything interesting about storage
is hiding in that gap.

<!-- S08 -->
To see where the billion came from,
you have to start with what
a hard drive was actually for.

<!-- S09 -->
Before it, computers kept their data
on punched cards and on magnetic tape.
Tape works. But tape is a queue.

<!-- S10 -->
If the thing you want sits at the end,
you travel through everything before it.
A machine could spend its afternoon
winding backwards and forwards.

<!-- S11 -->
The new machine could go straight to it.
That was the whole point of it,
and it is what the name says.
Random access.

<!-- S12 -->
Fifty metal disks, sixty centimetres
across, spinning twenty times a second.
An arm moved between them and put
a magnetic head over the right track.

<!-- S13 -->
It could find any piece of data
in under a second.
In 1956, that bordered on magic.

<!-- S14 -->
And it came with a problem
that has never gone away.
The head must not touch the disk.

<!-- S15 -->
Touch it, and you scrape away
the data and the head together.
But it has to stay near enough
to feel one magnetic patch.

<!-- S16 -->
IBM's answer was to float it
on compressed air.
The head hovered twenty micrometres
above the surface.

<!-- S17 -->
Which is why the machine needed
its own air compressor.
Two hundred kilograms of that ton
was a pump, blowing air under a head.

<!-- S18 -->
And that five megabytes needs a footnote,
because it wasn't five.
It was five million characters
of six bits each.

<!-- S19 -->
In today's money,
three and three quarter megabytes.

<!-- S20 -->
But here is the number that matters,
and nobody ever quotes it.

<!-- S21 -->
On that disk, one square centimetre
held three hundred bits.
Three hundred.

<!-- S22 -->
A modern drive puts nearly three hundred
billion into the same square centimetre.
That is where the billion came from.
Not from the box. From the bit.

<!-- S23 -->
The box did shrink, of course,
and the first thing to go
was that compressor.

<!-- S24 -->
In 1961, IBM shaped the head
like a tiny wing,
so that the spinning disk
made its own cushion of air.

<!-- S25 -->
No pump. No plumbing.
And the head could now fly
three times nearer the surface,
which bought thirteen times the density.

<!-- S26 -->
In 1973 came a drive nicknamed Winchester.
It sealed the disks and the heads
into one clean assembly,
so that nothing could get in.

<!-- S27 -->
What made storage grow
was never the box.
It was making the bit smaller.
So what is a bit, physically?

<!-- S28 -->
The disk is coated with a magnetic film
made of tiny grains.
A bit is a patch of those grains,
magnetised the same way.

<!-- S29 -->
Point them one way for a zero,
and the other way for a one.
Want more capacity?
Then use fewer grains for each bit.

<!-- S30 -->
Every generation did exactly that.
Smaller grains. Narrower tracks.
Arms that could aim more precisely.
But there is a price.

<!-- S31 -->
A smaller patch makes a weaker signal.
Halve the patch, and the head
has half as much to listen to.
By the late eighties, it was getting thin.

<!-- S32 -->
Then physics handed the industry
something it had not asked for.

<!-- S33 -->
In 1988, Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg
independently found the same
strange effect.

<!-- S34 -->
Stack thin magnetic layers
with a sliver of metal between them,
and the resistance of the stack
depends on how the layers line up.

<!-- S35 -->
A small magnetic field.
A large change in resistance.
They called it giant magnetoresistance.

<!-- S36 -->
Which is a read head.
A magnetic patch goes past the sensor,
and the sensor's resistance jumps.
Small signals stop being a problem.

<!-- S37 -->
IBM shipped the first one in 1997.
The Nobel Prize for the discovery
came in 2007.

<!-- S38 -->
By then the effect had been sitting
inside ordinary computers
for a decade.

<!-- S39 -->
And the numbers it unlocked are absurd.
Three hundred bits per square centimetre
in 1956. Nearly three hundred billion now.

<!-- S40 -->
Roughly a billion times more data
on the same piece of surface.
That is how five megabytes
became terabytes.

<!-- S40b -->
By the way, if you'd like to see
where the next question takes us,
consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S41 -->
Except that isn't the end of the story.
It's the point where
the story hits a wall.

<!-- S42 -->
Because you cannot keep taking
grains away from a bit.
A grain holds its direction
because flipping it costs energy.

<!-- S43 -->
Shrink the grain, and that cost falls.
And a disk is not a quiet place.
Room temperature is atoms
shoving each other about.

<!-- S44 -->
Make the grain small enough,
and an ordinary thermal nudge
is enough to turn it over.

<!-- S45 -->
The bit flips. On its own.
Nobody wrote anything.

<!-- S46 -->
Physicists call it
the superparamagnetic limit.
Engineers had a blunter way to put it.
They were running out of atoms.

<!-- S47 -->
Around the turn of the century,
the old way of recording ran into it,
somewhere near fifteen billion bits
per square centimetre.

<!-- S48 -->
For the first time in forty years,
the curve was about to stop.
So the industry cheated. Twice.

<!-- S49 -->
The first cheat was to notice
that the disk has a dimension
nobody had been using.

<!-- S50 -->
Until then, each bit lay flat,
stretched along its track,
like a word printed on a page.
Flat things get thin when you shrink them.

<!-- S51 -->
So the magnetisation was turned
through ninety degrees,
to point down into the disk instead.

<!-- S52 -->
When there was no more room
on the surface,
we started standing the bits upright.

<!-- S53 -->
A standing bit can be narrow
and still be deep,
so it keeps enough material
to hold its direction.

<!-- S54 -->
Drives started shipping that way in 2005.
Every hard drive you have bought since
works like this.

<!-- S55 -->
The second cheat is stranger.
If the material is too stable to write,
heat it first.

<!-- S56 -->
So they put a laser in the head.

<!-- S57 -->
It heats one spot on the disk
to about four hundred and fifty degrees
for a single nanosecond.

<!-- S58 -->
Hot, the spot is soft enough to write.
A nanosecond later it has cooled,
and the bit is frozen where it was put.

<!-- S59 -->
There is a laser inside the hard drive,
melting the problem out of the way
a few hundred degrees at a time.
This is not a laboratory. You can buy it.

<!-- S60 -->
Meanwhile, somebody else was asking
a different question.
What if we take the disk out entirely?

<!-- S61 -->
No platter. No arm. No head.
Nothing that has to fly.
Nothing that has to spin.

<!-- S62 -->
Flash memory stores a bit
as trapped electric charge,
sealed behind an insulator
inside a cell.

<!-- S63 -->
Put charge in, and the cell needs
a higher voltage to switch on.
Measure that voltage,
and you know what was stored.

<!-- S64 -->
Nothing moves, so nothing has to be found.
Every cell is equally near.
The seek time the RAMAC was built around
simply stops existing.

<!-- S65 -->
And flash ran the same race.
Smaller cells. More of them.
And it hit the same kind of wall.

<!-- S66 -->
Because the charge in a cell
is not really a quantity.
It is a number of electrons.

<!-- S67 -->
Shrink the cell, and that number falls,
until a handful of electrons
leaking away is the difference
between a one and a zero.

<!-- S68 -->
And it got worse,
because the industry had started
storing more than one bit in each cell.

<!-- S69 -->
Four bits means sixteen different
charge levels inside one cell,
and the gaps between them
are a few hundred millivolts.

<!-- S70 -->
Smaller cells make those gaps smaller.
So the same wall arrived,
in a technology with no moving parts,
for entirely different reasons.

<!-- S71 -->
And in 2013 the answer arrived.
It was the same answer.

<!-- S72 -->
Samsung stopped making the cells smaller
and started stacking them
on top of each other.
Twenty-four layers, in the first one.

<!-- S73 -->
Today the count is in the hundreds,
and the roadmaps talk about a thousand.
The chip did not get wider.
It got taller.

<!-- S74 -->
Put the two technologies side by side.
The bit cannot get any smaller,
so stand it upright.
The cell cannot get any smaller,
so stack the cells.

<!-- S75 -->
Different materials. Different physics.
Different companies.
The same move.

<!-- S76 -->
And the old technology did not die.
It moved. More than nine tenths
of the storage in cloud data centres
is still spinning disks,
because per byte it is still far cheaper.

<!-- S77 -->
So the seventy-year story of storage
is not really a story
about miniaturisation.

<!-- S78 -->
We shrank things.
We reached the floor
of what matter will put up with.
And then we built upwards.

<!-- S79 -->
Five million characters once needed
fifty disks, a motor, a compressor,
and a room to keep them in.

<!-- S80 -->
The same data now sits in a patch of film
you could not see
without an electron microscope.

<!-- S81 -->
What got smaller was never the machine.
It was the amount of matter
we need in order to remember one thing.

<!-- S82 -->
And every time physics told us
that was as small as it goes,
we found another direction to build in.
