<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you are standing
in a huge car park.
There are hundreds of cars around you.
And someone says:
"Take the red car."

<!-- S02 -->
Pretty straightforward.
But what if they said:
"Take the car red."
That sounds strange in English.
But you would still understand it.

<!-- S03 -->
So why do some languages
put adjectives before the noun,
while others put them after?
And is one of those systems
actually better?

<!-- S04 -->
In English, we say:
a red car.
The adjective comes first.
The same basic pattern
appears in German.
{de} Ein rotes Auto.

<!-- S05 -->
And in Swedish.
{sv} En röd bil.
And in Norwegian.
{no} En rød bil.

<!-- S06 -->
But then we have languages
such as French.
{fr} Une voiture rouge.
Literally:
a car red.

<!-- S07 -->
Spanish does something similar.
{es} Un coche rojo.
A car red, again.

<!-- S08 -->
And this is not some odd exception.
It is the majority.
Linguists have surveyed
more than thirteen hundred languages.

<!-- S09 -->
In eight hundred and seventy-nine of them,
the noun comes first.
In three hundred and seventy-three,
the adjective does.

<!-- S10 -->
So the order English uses
is the rarer of the two.
Most languages
say car red.

<!-- S11 -->
And then there is Polish.
Polish can do something
rather interesting.
For example.
{pl} Czarne buty turystyczne.

<!-- S12 -->
Literally:
black boots tourist.
So you have got one adjective
before the noun,
and another one after it.

<!-- S12b -->
And it is not random.
Black is just a description,
so it goes in front.
Hiking is what the boots are for,
so it goes behind.

<!-- S13 -->
Polish basically says:
"Why choose?"
And that turns out
to be quite useful.

<!-- S14 -->
So which way is better?
At first glance,
there is a good argument
for putting the noun first.

<!-- S15 -->
Consider:
"the car red."
The first thing you hear is car.
So immediately,
you know what we are talking about.

<!-- S16 -->
Then the adjective
simply adds information.
The car... red.
The car... old.
The car... enormous.

<!-- S17 -->
This is useful
when the object itself is new.
Imagine someone says:
"There is a car outside."

<!-- S18 -->
Now you know
what we are talking about.
And then:
"It is red."
Perfectly straightforward.

<!-- S19 -->
The noun gives the listener
something to attach
the rest of the information to.

<!-- S22 -->
Everything that follows
is just adding detail.
So perhaps putting the noun first
is more efficient.
Problem solved.
Except... not really.

<!-- S23 -->
Let us go back to that car park.
There are two hundred cars.
And I say:
"Take the car."

<!-- S24 -->
Thanks. Very useful.
I already knew I needed a car.
The problem is:
which one?

<!-- S25 -->
Now imagine I say:
"Take the red car."
Suddenly, red is
the important piece of information.

<!-- S26 -->
"Car" tells you almost nothing.
"Red" lets you actually find
the thing you are looking for.

<!-- S27 -->
And that changes the whole argument.
Because if we already know
what we are talking about,
then giving us the noun first
can be a waste of time.

<!-- S28 -->
The adjective may be
the more useful piece.
So red car
can be more efficient
than car red,
because the important information
arrives first.

<!-- S29 -->
And this is where
it gets interesting.
Perhaps the question is not:
"Which word order is better?"

<!-- S30 -->
Perhaps the real question is:
"Which information
does the listener already have?"

<!-- S31 -->
If we are introducing something new,
the noun is important.
But if we are standing
next to a row of cars,
the noun is almost redundant.

<!-- S32 -->
We already know
we are talking about cars.
What we need is the information
that tells one car from another.
The colour. The size.
The age. Whatever.

<!-- S33 -->
And this is something we forget
when we talk about grammar.
Language does not
happen in isolation.

<!-- S34 -->
We do not normally walk around
describing unknown objects
to people with no context.
We share the same room.
We can see the same things.

<!-- S35 -->
We have already been talking
about the subject.
We have previous sentences.
We have gestures.
We have context.

And all of that changes
which words actually
carry information.

<!-- S36 -->
In information theory,
something the receiver already knows
carries very little new information.
And language works
in a surprisingly similar way.

<!-- S38 -->
There is another argument
for putting the noun first.
Imagine this:
"The large new red German
electric sports..."

<!-- S39 -->
And you are still waiting.
What?
A car? A motorcycle?
A boat? An aircraft?

<!-- S40 -->
English can build
impressively long noun phrases
where the listener has to wait
until the very end
to discover what all those adjectives
are actually describing.

<!-- S41 -->
Putting the noun first solves that.
"The car — large, new, red,
German, electric, sporty..."
Now you know
what we are talking about
from the beginning.

<!-- S42 -->
So noun first
has a real advantage.
But in ordinary conversation,
context usually means
we do not have to introduce
everything from scratch.

<!-- S42b -->
And that is why adjective first
can be extremely useful.

<!-- S43 -->
Which brings us back to Polish.
{pl} Czarne buty turystyczne.
Black boots, tourist.

English would normally
put everything before the noun.
Black hiking boots.

<!-- S43b -->
Norwegian does both,
but not with adjectives.
Those stay in front, like English.
It moves the possessive instead.
{no} Bilen min.
The car mine.
Swedish and Danish
keep that in front.

<!-- S45 -->
And that is a nice reminder
that languages do not have to choose
one universal rule.
They can use different positions
for different kinds of information.

<!-- S46 -->
Which brings us to a language
that takes this idea
in a rather more
creative direction.

<!-- S47 -->
French.
Because just when you think
you have worked out the rules,
French decides that the position
of an adjective
can change its meaning.

<!-- S48 -->
Take:
{fr} Un grand homme.
This means, roughly,
a great or important man.

But:
{fr} Un homme grand.
means a tall man.

<!-- S49 -->
Same adjective.
Same noun.
Different position.
Different meaning.

<!-- S50 -->
And there is more.
{fr} Un pauvre homme.
can mean an unfortunate
or pathetic man.

<!-- S51 -->
Whereas:
{fr} Un homme pauvre.
means a poor man.
As in, someone who
does not have much money.

<!-- S52 -->
From an English
or Czech point of view,
this feels slightly unfair.
If I say black shoes,
or, for some reason, poetically,
shoes black,
they are still black shoes.

<!-- S53 -->
But French says:
"Ah. You have moved the adjective."
And suddenly:
"No, no. We meant something
completely different."

<!-- S54 -->
The position of the adjective
has become part of the meaning.
It is almost as if the language
had hidden an extra piece
of information in the word order.

<!-- S55 -->
And honestly...
that is a little bit naughty.

<!-- S56 -->
So what is the verdict?
After all this,
you would probably expect us
to declare a winner.
Noun first?
Or adjective first?

<!-- S57 -->
But there is not really
a universal winner.
Noun first has a very nice property:
"I will tell you what
we are talking about first."

<!-- S58 -->
Adjective first has another:
"I will tell you
what makes it different first."
And both are useful.

<!-- S60 -->
And perhaps this is
the most interesting part of it.
Languages are not
perfectly engineered systems.

<!-- S61 -->
Nobody sat down
thousands of years ago,
designed English, and said:
"Right. Adjectives go before nouns.
This should maximise
communicative efficiency."

<!-- S62 -->
Languages evolved.
People used them.
People changed them.
Some patterns became common.
Others disappeared.

<!-- S63 -->
And different languages
ended up making
different compromises.
English, German, Swedish and Czech
generally put adjectives
before nouns.

<!-- S64 -->
French, Spanish
and many other languages
often put them afterwards.
Polish can happily mix the two.
And French can even use the position
to change the meaning.

<!-- S65 -->
So which one is actually better?
Perhaps the answer is:
it depends.

<!-- S66 -->
When the noun is new information,
noun first can be useful.
When the noun is already obvious
from context,
adjective first can be better.

<!-- S67 -->
And that means
the supposedly simple question —
should we say red car or car red —
turns into a much bigger one.

<!-- S68 -->
"What information
does the listener need
right now?"
And that is a question
every human language has to solve.
They just solve it
in different ways.

<!-- S69 -->
So the next time
someone tells you
"take the red car,"
you can appreciate the grammar.

<!-- S70 -->
But more importantly,
you will know exactly why
they did not bother telling you
to take the car first.
There are two hundred of them.
And you already know they are cars.
