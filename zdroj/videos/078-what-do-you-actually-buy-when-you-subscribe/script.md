# What Do You Actually Buy When You Subscribe?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
There was a time when buying something
meant you actually owned it.

<!-- S02 -->
If you bought an album,
you owned the record.

If you bought a film, you owned the tape.

If you bought a game, you owned the disc.

And if you bought a piece of software,
nobody expected you to pay for it again
next month.

<!-- S03 -->
Today, we pay every month.

For music.

For films.

For software.

For storage.

For games.

<!-- S03b -->
And more and more things
that used to be simply bought...

are quietly turning into services.

<!-- S04 -->
For a company, the arithmetic is simple.

A customer who buys a program once,
for a hundred dollars,
is worth a hundred dollars.

<!-- S05 -->
A customer who pays ten dollars a month
is worth a hundred and twenty a year.

If they stay for ten years...

twelve hundred.

<!-- S06 -->
So what do you actually buy
when you subscribe?

And could you still live
without subscriptions?

<!-- S07 -->
The easy answer is that companies
pushed all this on us.

That's not quite what happened.

<!-- S08 -->
To see why, it helps to go back
to something very simple.

A cassette tape.

<!-- S09 -->
If you had the cassette,
you had the music.

You could copy it.

You could put it on a shelf
and forget about it for twenty years.

<!-- S10 -->
Nobody could switch it off remotely.

Nobody could remove the album
from your region.

Nobody could change the terms of service.

<!-- S11 -->
And nobody could send you an email
saying...

Your subscription has expired.

<!-- S12 -->
The same was true for books, CDs, DVDs,
and software.

You bought something.

You took it home.

And it was yours.

<!-- S12b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S13 -->
Then computers became common.

In theory, that should have made
ownership even easier.

<!-- S14 -->
Instead of hundreds of CDs,
thousands of songs as files.

Instead of shelves of DVDs,
films on a hard drive.

And files are easy to copy,
back up, and move.

<!-- S15 -->
There was just one problem.

Most people didn't want to deal with them.

<!-- S16 -->
Files.

Folders.

Drives.

Backups.

And the eternal question...

Where did I save that thing?

<!-- S17 -->
So Microsoft and Apple started
hiding the complexity.

You didn't need to know
where your photos were stored.

You just opened Photos.

<!-- S18 -->
It was a genuine improvement.

Millions of people could use a computer
without knowing how it worked.

And that was exactly the point.

<!-- S19 -->
Then came the cloud,
and it solved an even bigger problem.

Your files no longer had to live
on your computer.

<!-- S19b -->
Your photos could be everywhere.

Your documents could be everywhere.

<!-- S20 -->
Buy a new phone, log in...

and everything is already there.

No copying.

No restoring backups.

No digging through old hard drives.

<!-- S21 -->
But something subtle happened.

We moved from "I have the file"...

to "I have access to the file".

<!-- S23 -->
When you bought a CD,
you bought a thing.

When you subscribe to Spotify,
you buy access.

<!-- S24 -->
When you bought a DVD,
you bought a copy.

When you subscribe to Netflix,
you buy access to a catalogue.

<!-- S25 -->
And catalogues change.

A film can disappear.

The price can go up.

The terms can change.

<!-- S26 -->
And if you stop paying...

the access disappears.

The money doesn't come back.

<!-- S27 -->
That's perfectly fair.

You paid for the time you had access.

But it isn't ownership.

<!-- S27b -->
At first, it all seemed reasonable.

Don't want to buy a record collection?

Fine. Pay for Spotify.

<!-- S27c -->
Don't want a shelf full of DVDs?

Fine. Pay for Netflix.

Don't want to install and maintain
your own software?

Fine. Pay for a cloud service.

<!-- S28 -->
And none of this means
subscriptions are a trick.

They solve real problems.

<!-- S29 -->
If you want to hear almost any song
you can think of,
you don't want to spend ten years
building a collection.

You want to press Play.

<!-- S30 -->
If you want to watch a film tonight,
you don't want to order a disc
and wait for it.

You want to press Play.

<!-- S31 -->
Convenience is valuable.

And people are perfectly willing
to pay for it.

<!-- S32 -->
Software shows the trade most clearly.

Imagine buying a program in 1998.

You installed it from a CD,
and used it for ten years.

<!-- S33 -->
The company might disappear.

The website might disappear.

But the program was still
on your hard drive...

and it still ran.

<!-- S34 -->
Today, many programs work differently.

You don't buy the program.

You subscribe to the service.

And if you stop paying,
it may stop working.

<!-- S35 -->
Remember the hundred dollars?

This is how it turns into twelve hundred.

<!-- S36 -->
So could you go back?

Could you actually live
without subscriptions?

<!-- S37 -->
Surprisingly...

yes.

At least for most things.

<!-- S38 -->
Linux, with thousands of free programs.

LibreOffice instead of Microsoft 365.

Music bought as files.

Films on physical discs.

Games that don't need
a permanent online service.

<!-- S39 -->
Your own photos on your own disks.

Your own home server.

Even some AI models,
running on your own computer.

<!-- S40 -->
And once the hardware is paid for...

there doesn't have to be
another monthly bill.

<!-- S41 -->
But there is a price.

Not necessarily money.

Effort.

<!-- S42 -->
If you own your data,
you have to look after it.

You make backups.

You replace failing disks.

You move old files to new formats.

You remember where things are.

<!-- S42b -->
If you own your films, you store them.

If you own your music, you organise it.

If you own your software,
you install and update it yourself.

<!-- S43 -->
The cloud takes all of that away.

That's what you're really paying for.

Not storage.

Someone else to worry about it.

<!-- S44 -->
Imagine two options.

Option one: you own everything.

A home server, three backups,
local copies.

You organise your files.

You maintain your hardware.

And now and then, a whole Saturday
spent fixing something.

<!-- S45 -->
Option two: twenty dollars a month
in subscriptions.

Everything syncs on its own.

Your photos appear on your phone.

Your documents are everywhere.

Your music is always there.

Your films are one click away.

<!-- S46 -->
For most people,
option two is probably more convenient.

And that's a perfectly rational choice.

<!-- S47 -->
And that's the real reason
subscriptions won.

Companies didn't force us
to stop owning things.

We gave up ownership...

because convenience was worth it.

<!-- S48 -->
At least, until it stops being worth it.

<!-- S49 -->
Because there is one question
worth asking now and then.

What happens if the service
disappears tomorrow?

<!-- S50 -->
For a DVD, the answer is simple.

You still have the DVD.

For a book, you still have the book.

For a file on your own disk,
you still have the file.

<!-- S51 -->
For a subscription?

It depends.

<!-- S52 -->
Maybe you just lose a film catalogue.

Maybe you lose years of photos.

Maybe you lose the software
you need for your work.

<!-- S52b -->
And this isn't just theoretical.

In 2024, Ubisoft shut down the servers
for a racing game called The Crew.

People had bought it.

But it needed those servers to run...

so it simply stopped running.

<!-- S52c -->
Days later, Ubisoft began removing
the game from players' accounts.

No offline version. No general refund.

<!-- S52d -->
And the service doesn't even have to disappear.

All of this works beautifully...
as long as your internet does.

<!-- S52e -->
A storm. A cut cable.
A few days without a signal.

And suddenly, much of what you pay for...
is out of reach.

<!-- S53 -->
The question isn't whether
the service is good or bad.

It's who is in control.

<!-- S54 -->
And that's worth teaching children.

Not that the cloud is bad.

Not that everyone should build a server
in their basement.

But what a file is.

Where their photos actually live.

Who controls their account.

And the difference between owning
something and being allowed to use it.

<!-- S55 -->
Then the choice is theirs.

Netflix, because they like it.

Cloud storage, because running a server
isn't worth their time.

But they'll know what they're buying.

<!-- S56 -->
And that may be the strangest part
of the whole story.

Technology gave us something
our parents could barely imagine.

<!-- S56b -->
Almost unlimited storage.

Perfect copies.

Instant distribution.

Access from anywhere in the world.

<!-- S57 -->
And yet...

in 1995, a person might have owned
hundreds of CDs.

In 2026, they might have access
to over a hundred million songs...

and own none of them.

<!-- S58 -->
That's an incredible trade.

And maybe it's a good one.

Maybe convenience is worth more
than ownership.

Maybe we don't need to own everything.

But we should at least recognise
the trade we're making.

<!-- S59 -->
Because there's a big difference between
"I have it"...

and "I can use it,
as long as someone else lets me."

<!-- S60 -->
And maybe the future doesn't have to be
one or the other.

We can have the convenience of services...

and still keep our own copy
of the things that actually matter.

<!-- S60b -->
A subscription can be incredibly useful.

But there's something strangely satisfying
about a file on your own disk...

that nobody can take away
just because you stopped paying.

<!-- S60c -->
Or books on your own shelves.

Or a stack of vinyl records.

<!-- S61 -->
Because sometimes, the most modern thing
you can do...

is own your own stuff.
