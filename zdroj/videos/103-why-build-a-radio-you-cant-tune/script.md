<!-- S01 -->
It's almost two in the morning,
on the twenty-first of August, 1968.
In Prague, a radio announcer
begins to read a statement.

<!-- S02 -->
Foreign armies have crossed the border
during the night.
And the statement says it happened
without the knowledge of the country's
own leaders.

<!-- S03 -->
As he reads,
the country's medium-wave transmitters
go silent.
Someone has switched them off.

<!-- S04 -->
Across the country, radios hiss.
But in kitchens, offices and workshops,
a small box on the wall
keeps talking.

<!-- S05 -->
It carries the statement
to the very last word.

<!-- S06 -->
That box had no tuning dial.
No antenna.
No buttons for other stations.

<!-- S07 -->
One cable, one volume knob,
and one programme...
whatever came through the wall.

<!-- S08 -->
So how did the one radio
that couldn't choose anything
end up carrying the news
everyone else had lost?

<!-- S09 -->
Because it wasn't really a radio.
It never picked up
a single radio wave.

<!-- S10 -->
In Czech its name simply meant
broadcasting by wire.
Most people shortened that
to a nickname: the wire box.

<!-- S11 -->
Not the loudspeakers on poles
in the village square.
This one lived indoors.

<!-- S12 -->
The programme travelled
from a central station,
through cables, into the home.
Like a telephone call
that never ended.

<!-- S13 -->
At the amplifier, the signal
was stepped up to a hundred and twenty volts,
so it could run for kilometres
along the line.

<!-- S14 -->
Near the listener, a transformer
brought it back down to thirty volts.

<!-- S15 -->
And inside the box,
there was almost nothing.
A transformer, a loudspeaker,
and a knob that clicked
between volume steps.

<!-- S16 -->
Nothing to tune...
because there was nothing
to tune into.

<!-- S17 -->
And switching off a transmitter
does nothing to a cable.
That night in 1968,
the wire simply kept going.

<!-- S17b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.
It really helps the channel.

<!-- S18 -->
But that only makes it stranger.
Why would a country build
a whole second radio network,
cable by cable,
into people's homes?

<!-- S19 -->
The first Czechoslovak lines
went live in 1953.
And the idea came
from the Soviet Union.

<!-- S20 -->
The Soviets began building
wired radio networks in the 1920s.
A simple loudspeaker was far cheaper
than a conventional radio receiver.
For millions of families,
it was their only way
to listen to the radio.

<!-- S21 -->
They called it the radio point.
In its simplest form,
one socket in the wall,
one programme from the central service,
or from a local station.

<!-- S22 -->
For a government, it solved
two problems at once.
It was cheap to put a voice
into every home...
and it was the government's voice.

<!-- S23 -->
And the reasoning was military.
In a war, a radio transmitter
is a big, obvious target.

<!-- S24 -->
A major transmitter
could serve an entire region.
If it went down,
a huge area could lose its signal.

<!-- S25 -->
A cable network doesn't depend
on one big mast.
It has its own amplifiers,
its own lines, its own route.
Knock out the transmitter,
and the wire can still talk.

<!-- S26 -->
It wasn't invincible.
The amplifiers needed electricity.
Cables could be cut.
It was a spare route,
not a fortress.

<!-- S27 -->
And it had everyday advantages, too.
No need to fight a weak indoor signal.
No radio interference to contend with.
And a receiver so simple
that it cost very little.

<!-- S28 -->
But the design had
one more consequence.
And a one-party state
could hardly have asked
for a better one.

<!-- S29 -->
The listener couldn't choose.
The programme was decided for you.
One cable, one voice.

<!-- S30 -->
Not all of it was propaganda.
There was music,
stories for children,
radio plays, the weather.
Plenty of people left it on all day,
just for company.

<!-- S31 -->
But all of it arrived
through a channel
the state owned from end to end.
There was no dial to turn
to Vienna,
or to Radio Free Europe.

<!-- S32 -->
What the state couldn't control
was the off switch.
Or whether anyone
was actually listening.

<!-- S33 -->
And many homes had
an ordinary radio as well.
The box for the kitchen,
the real radio for the evening...
and, discreetly,
for stations from the West.

<!-- S34 -->
So it's tempting to call it
a control device
dressed up as a radio.

<!-- S35 -->
Except that democracies
wired up radio too.

<!-- S36 -->
Switzerland started its own version
in 1931.
In deep mountain valleys,
reception was often poor,
so programmes went down
the telephone line instead.

<!-- S37 -->
Italy launched a similar service
in 1958, also over telephone lines,
with several channels...
including one of nothing
but classical music.

<!-- S38 -->
And in British towns,
from the late 1920s,
companies rented out loudspeakers
connected by wire.

<!-- S39 -->
The same idea,
doing very different jobs.

<!-- S40 -->
In the Alps, it beat the mountains.
In Italy, it offered choice.
In Czechoslovakia,
it was a spare route for war...
with one channel,
chosen by the state.

<!-- S41 -->
The technology didn't decide
what it was for.
That depended on who controlled
the network... and the programme.

<!-- S42 -->
And in August 1968,
the people controlling the transmitters
turned out not to control
every route.

<!-- S43 -->
Back to that night.
Armies of five Warsaw Pact countries,
led by the Soviet Union,
had crossed the border
to end the Prague Spring...
months in which the Communist Party itself
had tried to loosen its grip.

<!-- S44 -->
The announcer, Vladimír Fišer,
was reading a statement
from the party's own leadership.
The troops, it said,
had come without the knowledge
of the president, parliament, the government,
or the party's first secretary.

<!-- S45 -->
And it called the invasion
a denial of the basic norms
of international law.

<!-- S46 -->
But the man in charge
of the country's communications,
Karel Hoffmann,
was a hardliner
who backed the invasion.
He had the medium-wave transmitters
shut down
just as those words went out.

<!-- S47 -->
Here's the irony.
A network built in case
foreign armies came.
And on the night they came,
the transmitters went dark...
but the wired network stayed on.

<!-- S48 -->
So the statement reached listeners
in full.
Only by wire.

<!-- S49 -->
The wire didn't stop a single tank.
It was cables and amplifiers,
nothing more.

<!-- S50 -->
But for those first hours,
the box that couldn't choose
anything at all
was the one that told people
what was happening.

<!-- S51 -->
And it wasn't the end
of the story.
Later that morning,
the broadcast fell silent again.
Around eleven,
it came back...
first from a bunker-like emergency studio
elsewhere in Prague.

<!-- S51b -->
Then from a studio
the troops had missed...
hidden inside the radio building,
with soldiers already in its corridors.

<!-- S52 -->
In the days that followed,
studios across the country
took turns keeping the broadcast going,
like a relay.

<!-- S53 -->
The wire was one backup.
The people running the radio
turned out to be another.

<!-- S54 -->
And Karel Hoffmann?
He didn't fall.
He rose.
For the next two decades,
he sat near the top
of the Communist Party.

<!-- S55 -->
The regime ended in 1989.
And in 2003,
thirty-five years after that night,
a Czech appeals court
convicted him of sabotage,
for the order to switch off
the transmitters.

<!-- S56 -->
He went to prison in 2004,
at the age of eighty...
and was released
less than a month later,
on health grounds.

<!-- S57 -->
The wire outlived his career,
but not by much.

<!-- S58 -->
Cheap radios got better.
FM brought clearer sound
and several stations,
with no cable to every building.

<!-- S59 -->
Next to that,
a radio with one programme
had nothing left to offer.

<!-- S60 -->
After 1989, the networks
kept running for a while.
Then, one by one,
they were switched off.
In 1999, the last one went silent.

<!-- S61 -->
It's easy to remember the box
as a propaganda speaker.
And it was built inside a system
that decided what people heard.

<!-- S62 -->
But the same idea carried radio
through the Swiss mountains,
and classical music across Italy.

<!-- S63 -->
And for one night in 1968,
the wire carried the very news
someone had tried to silence.

<!-- S64 -->
A wire doesn't care
what it carries.
What matters is who holds
the other end...

<!-- S65 -->
and whether there's
another radio in the house.
