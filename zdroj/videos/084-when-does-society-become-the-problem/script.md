# When Does Society Become the Problem?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine waking up tomorrow
and discovering that something
about the way the world sees you
has become a problem.

<!-- S02 -->
Not because you have hurt anyone.
Not because you have done anything wrong.

<!-- S03 -->
But because the way you look,
the way you sound,
or the person you know yourself to be
does not fit the expectations
of the people around you.

<!-- S04 -->
You still have to get dressed.
Go to work. Buy groceries.
Talk to your family. Answer questions.

And all the while, you have to think
about things most people
never need to consider.

<!-- S05 -->
Will I have to show an ID
that no longer matches me?

Will a form make me tick a box
that doesn't fit?

Will someone call me by a name that hurts?

<!-- S06 -->
Will I get to simply be myself today?

For some transgender people,
these are not hypothetical questions.
They are part of everyday life.

<!-- S07 -->
In Denmark, researchers used
national records to identify
transgender people and compare
their suicide rates with those
of the rest of the population
over four decades.

<!-- S08 -->
Their rate of death by suicide
was about three and a half times higher.

<!-- S09 -->
But a number like that
is only one part of the story.

<!-- S10 -->
Because transgender people
are not all alike.

Some are happy and comfortable
with themselves.

<!-- S11 -->
Others struggle for years to reconcile
their identity, their bodies,
and the way the world treats them.

<!-- S12 -->
There is a difference between the pain
a person experiences within themselves
and the pain that other people add to it.

<!-- S13 -->
Sometimes, the two become
almost impossible to separate.

<!-- S14 -->
When your own body feels wrong.

<!-- S15 -->
We need to talk about something
that is harder to see from the outside.

Gender dysphoria.

<!-- S16 -->
For some transgender people,
the relationship between
their experienced gender and their bodies
can cause profound distress.

<!-- S17 -->
It may involve physical characteristics
that feel deeply wrong.

It may involve
the changes brought by puberty.

It may involve being perceived as a gender
that does not match their identity.

<!-- S18 -->
For others, the distress may be less
about their bodies and more about
how they are treated
or recognised by the world.

<!-- S19 -->
Stand in front of a mirror.
Anyone looking over your shoulder
would see nothing unusual.

Just a face. A body.
A person getting ready for the day.

<!-- S20 -->
But for you, that reflection may bring
a sense of alienation, grief,
or distress that is difficult to explain.

<!-- S21 -->
You might try to ignore it.

You might even believe that
if you simply tried harder to fit in,
the feeling would disappear.

<!-- S22 -->
Sometimes people spend years
trying to make that happen.

And when it does not,
they may begin to wonder
whether something is wrong with them.

<!-- S23 -->
But transgender identity itself
is not classified as a mental disorder
in the World Health Organization's ICD-11.

It uses its own term,
gender incongruence,
and lists it separately
from mental and behavioural disorders.

<!-- S23b -->
The American psychiatric manual
does use the term gender dysphoria.

But there, it describes the distress,
not the identity itself.

<!-- S24 -->
That distinction matters.

It means we should not
automatically treat a person's identity
as the illness that needs to be cured.

<!-- S25 -->
At the same time,
real distress deserves real care.

Some people benefit
from psychological support.
Some pursue social or medical transition.

<!-- S26 -->
And we should never assume
that we understand someone's suffering
simply because we can see their body.

<!-- S27 -->
The world adds its own weight.

<!-- S28 -->
Now imagine that the distress
you already experience
is only the beginning.

You speak,
and someone laughs at your voice.

<!-- S29 -->
You go online,
and a stranger who has never met you
explains what you really are.

<!-- S30 -->
At school, the teacher calls the register
and pauses at your name.

<!-- S31 -->
You phone your bank,
and the person on the line
doesn't believe the account is yours.

<!-- S32 -->
And perhaps nothing
particularly dramatic happens
on any single day.

That is part of the problem.

<!-- S33 -->
Stress does not always arrive
as one terrible event.

Sometimes it builds
through hundreds of small experiences.

<!-- S34 -->
A remark you cannot forget.

A conversation you rehearse in advance.

A place you avoid.

<!-- S35 -->
A part of yourself you keep hidden
because you do not know
how people will react.

<!-- S36 -->
These moments do not reset
every morning.

They add up, over months and years.

<!-- S37 -->
In psychology, one framework
for understanding this
is called minority stress.

<!-- S38 -->
It describes the extra burden
of stigma, discrimination,
expected rejection, and navigating a world
where your identity
may not be accepted.

<!-- S39 -->
A 2022 systematic review
and meta-analysis
examined eighty-five studies.

It found that several
minority-stress factors were associated
with depression, suicidal thoughts,
and suicide attempts.

<!-- S40 -->
A separate review of European research,
published in 2024, found discrimination
among the most consistently documented
risk factors.

<!-- S41 -->
The environment in which people live
is not irrelevant to their mental health.

<!-- S42 -->
And that raises
an uncomfortable question.

What happens when someone
who is already struggling
must also spend their life
defending their right
to exist as themselves?

<!-- S43 -->
The numbers behind the pain.

<!-- S44 -->
Let's look at some numbers carefully,
starting with Denmark.

<!-- S45 -->
Out of more than six and a half million
people in national records,
from 1980 to 2021,
researchers identified almost
three thousand eight hundred
as transgender.

<!-- S46 -->
Compared with everyone else,
their rate of recorded suicide attempts
was nearly eight times higher.

Their rate of death by suicide,
the number we started with,
was about three and a half times higher.

<!-- S47 -->
But the number of deaths was small.

Twelve.

So the true difference could lie
anywhere from about two
to about six times.

<!-- S48 -->
In more recent decades,
the gap narrowed.
But it did not disappear.

<!-- S49 -->
The study could only find people
who had received hospital care
related to their gender identity,
or who had legally changed their gender.

<!-- S50 -->
Trans people who never did either
are not in the data.

Still, it compares
an entire population,
not just people
who chose to answer a survey.

<!-- S51 -->
What a number like this shows
is a difference.

It does not show the cause.

<!-- S52 -->
A suicide attempt can have
many contributing factors.

<!-- S53 -->
Mental health conditions,
family conflict, isolation, trauma,
financial insecurity,
difficulties accessing care,
and experiences of discrimination
can overlap.

<!-- S54 -->
We cannot look at a statistic
and determine the cause
of an individual person's suffering.

<!-- S55 -->
But the question is not
whether every problem has one cause.

It is whether we are willing
to take every relevant cause seriously.

<!-- S56 -->
For young people, one of the largest
recent sources is The Trevor Project.

In 2024, it published a survey of
more than eighteen thousand LGBTQ+
young people in the United States.

<!-- S57 -->
Among the transgender
and non-binary respondents,
forty-six percent had seriously
considered suicide in the previous year.

Fourteen percent had attempted it.

<!-- S58 -->
It was an online survey,
not a random sample,
so the exact figures cannot be applied
to every young trans person.

<!-- S59 -->
But behind every percentage is someone
who had to live through
the experience being counted.

Someone who may have needed help.

<!-- S60 -->
If you're struggling yourself,
you can find links to free,
confidential support services
in the description,
including options in many countries.

<!-- S61 -->
What happens when people are accepted?

<!-- S62 -->
There is another side to this story.

People are not only vulnerable
to their environments.
They can also find strength in them.

<!-- S63 -->
A supportive family cannot magically
eliminate every form of gender dysphoria.

And acceptance is not a substitute
for professional mental health care
when that care is needed.

<!-- S64 -->
The same survey asked
how accepting young people felt
their local communities were.

<!-- S65 -->
Twenty percent of those
who described their communities
as very unaccepting
reported a suicide attempt
in the previous year.

<!-- S66 -->
Among those who described them
as very accepting, it was eight percent.

<!-- S67 -->
Transgender and non-binary
young people also reported
lower rates of suicide attempts
when everyone they lived with
respected their pronouns,

or when they had access
to a gender-neutral bathroom at school.

<!-- S68 -->
These are correlations, not proof.

But they challenge the idea
that the social environment
makes no difference.

<!-- S69 -->
We cannot remove every source
of suffering from another person's life.

We can, however, choose not to make
that suffering worse.

<!-- S70 -->
We can listen without ridicule.

We can respect someone's privacy.

<!-- S71 -->
We can allow a person to seek
appropriate medical
and psychological care
without treating their existence
as a public spectacle.

<!-- S72 -->
We can disagree
about difficult questions
without turning another human being
into a target.

These things may sound small.

<!-- S73 -->
To someone who spends every day
expecting rejection,
they may not be small at all.

<!-- S74 -->
Who is responsible?

<!-- S75 -->
At this point, someone might object.

Why should society be held responsible
for another person's mental health?

<!-- S76 -->
Nobody gets through life without pain.

We cannot make everyone happy,
and we cannot force everyone
to agree with everyone else.

<!-- S77 -->
All of that is true.

But it misses the difference
between the pain a person carries within
and the pain that others add to it.

<!-- S78 -->
We can still ask whether our words
and actions make discrimination,
misunderstanding, or conflict
more common.

<!-- S79 -->
And we can ask why
a person's dignity should depend
on whether the majority finds
their identity easy to understand.

<!-- S80 -->
You do not need to agree
with every decision
a transgender person makes
to treat them with basic respect.

And you do not need to abandon
your questions to reject cruelty.

<!-- S81 -->
These are not the same thing.

<!-- S82 -->
Sometimes, when someone does not fit
neatly into the categories
we understand,
we make that person's life harder
simply because their existence
makes us uncomfortable.

<!-- S83 -->
We may call it tradition,
or common sense.

But we should still ask
what our actions accomplish.

<!-- S84 -->
Do they help someone who is struggling?

Or do they simply make
another person's life more painful?

<!-- S85 -->
The mirror.

<!-- S86 -->
Let's return to the person
from the beginning.

They wake up tomorrow morning.

They get dressed.

They leave their home.

<!-- S87 -->
They go to work, or to school,
or to meet a friend.

They still have their own doubts,
their own struggles,
and their own complicated relationship
with the world.

<!-- S88 -->
We may never understand exactly
what it feels like to live their life.

And perhaps we do not need to.

<!-- S89 -->
This series was meant
to look beyond the surface.

To see the person
behind the appearance.

The person behind the arguments.

The person behind the statistics.

<!-- S90 -->
And to recognise that
whatever difficulties someone faces,
they do not become less human
because their life is different from ours.

<!-- S91 -->
We often ask transgender people
to explain themselves.

To justify their identity.

To defend their bodies.

To prove that their suffering is real.

<!-- S92 -->
But perhaps there is another question
we should ask ourselves.

What does our response
to their suffering reveal about us?

<!-- S93 -->
When someone is already struggling,
do we offer understanding,
even if we do not fully understand?

<!-- S94 -->
Or do we demand that they
make us comfortable before we are willing
to treat them with dignity?

<!-- S95 -->
A society is revealed
in how it treats people
who have less power.

People who are misunderstood.

People whose lives challenge
our assumptions about what is normal.

<!-- S96 -->
We cannot promise that every person
will be free from pain.

But we can choose whether our society
becomes another source of that pain.

<!-- S97 -->
And perhaps that is
the final lesson of this series.

The question is not only how a person
learns to live in a world
that does not understand them.

<!-- S98 -->
The question is what kind of world
we are choosing to build.

<!-- S99 -->
Because sometimes, when we look
at the suffering of another person,
we are not only looking at their life.

<!-- S100 -->
We are looking into a mirror.

And what we see there
is partly our own reflection.
