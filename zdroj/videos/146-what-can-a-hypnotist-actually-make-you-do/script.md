<!-- S01 -->
A researcher tells you
that the number six no longer exists.

<!-- S02 -->
Not that you shouldn't use it.
It has simply gone.

<!-- S03 -->
Now count from one to ten.
Some people skipped the number six entirely.
Then came a page of sums, full of sixes.

<!-- S04 -->
This was real research, by Frederick Evans and colleagues.
Some of the volunteers were hypnotised.
The others had been told to fake it.

<!-- S05 -->
The fakers did the sensible thing.
They turned the sixes into sevens or zeroes.

<!-- S06 -->
The hypnotised group
tended to do something much stranger.
They worked as if the sixes weren't there.

<!-- S07 -->
The fakers were solving a puzzle
about a missing number.
For the others, the number seemed to be missing.

<!-- S08 -->
So for some people, something real is going on.
But how far does it go?

<!-- S09 -->
Can someone plant a command in your head?
Make you do something you'd normally refuse?
Change what you remember?

<!-- S10 -->
All of that has been tested in a lab.
And the trick that makes the answers
worth anything
is the one you just saw:
test the fakers too.

<!-- S10b -->
You've probably seen hypnosis already, in films.
A doctor sends a sleepwalker out to kill.
A soldier turns assassin
at the sight of a playing card.

<!-- S10c -->
The hypnotist has a swinging watch and a special power.
The victim sinks into something like sleep,
obeys, remembers nothing,
and stays under until someone snaps their fingers.

<!-- S11 -->
First, forget the swinging watch.
Hypnosis isn't sleep.
The person is awake and paying attention.

<!-- S11b -->
It doesn't even take relaxing.
Researchers have hypnotised people
with their eyes open,
while they pedalled an exercise bike.

<!-- S12 -->
A hypnotist narrows your attention,
then offers suggestions.
Your arm is getting heavy.
This water tastes sweet.

<!-- S12b -->
When it works, it doesn't feel like being overpowered.
The arm just seems to get heavy by itself.
For many researchers, that feeling is what makes it
a suggestion rather than an instruction.

<!-- S12c -->
And the special power?
One of the standard tests in hypnosis research
comes with a recorded voice.
It belongs to a radio announcer,
with no training in psychology or hypnosis.

<!-- S12d -->
In the lab, at least,
nearly all the action is in the person being hypnotised.

<!-- S12e -->
What about getting stuck in a trance?
In a study published in 1971,
volunteers were hypnotised by a tape recording,
and a second group was told to fake it.
Then the light went out and the tape stopped.
The researcher cursed the fuse and rushed out.

<!-- S12f -->
It was staged, and they were being watched
through a one-way screen.
Five of the six fakers opened their eyes within seconds.
When they heard footsteps coming back,
they shut them again and carried on faking.

<!-- S12g -->
For several minutes, most of the hypnotised group
didn't change at all.
Then, slowly and with difficulty,
they brought themselves out of it.
Those who came out opened their eyes
after about sixteen minutes, on average.
Nobody snapped any fingers.

<!-- S13 -->
On standard tests, about one person in eight
responds strongly.
About as many barely respond at all.
Everyone else is somewhere in between.

<!-- S13c -->
And it doesn't mean you're gullible.
It's linked more closely to absorption,
getting lost in a book or a piece of music,
than to how easily you're persuaded.

<!-- S14 -->
Keep that top one in eight in mind,
because most of the strangest results in this video
come from them.

<!-- S15 -->
In 1939, a psychologist called Rowland
put a rattlesnake in a box
and asked hypnotised volunteers to pick it up.

<!-- S16 -->
A pane of invisible glass was in the way.
They couldn't see it.
Three of the four reached for the snake,
one of them told it was a coil of rope.

<!-- S17 -->
Then he invited forty-two ordinary people
to do the same.
All but one wouldn't even go near the box.

<!-- S18 -->
It looked like proof.
Hypnosis could switch off fear.

<!-- S19 -->
In 1960, in Sydney,
Martin Orne and Frederick Evans
ran it again, with one change.

<!-- S20 -->
A venomous snake behind the glass.
Then a beaker of real nitric acid,
dissolving a coin.
Take the coin out with your bare hand, they said.

<!-- S21 -->
After that, throw the acid
in the assistant's face.
By then, it had been secretly swapped
for a harmless liquid.

<!-- S22 -->
Five of the six hypnotised volunteers
did all of it,
just like in the older studies.

<!-- S23 -->
Here's the change.
They also tested six people
who couldn't be hypnotised,
and told them to fake it.

<!-- S24 -->
All six went for it too.

<!-- S25 -->
Then six more, wide awake,
with no hypnosis and nothing to fake.
Five of them still did both acid tasks.

<!-- S26 -->
Afterwards, they nearly all gave the same reason.
This was an experiment,
run by responsible scientists.
So it had to be safe.
Some assumed the snake had been defanged,
or that the acid wasn't really acid.

<!-- S27 -->
Orne and Evans also asked a few colleagues,
informally, to try the same tasks.
They refused every time.

<!-- S28 -->
So reaching into acid didn't take hypnosis.
It only took the setting.

<!-- S29 -->
That doesn't prove hypnosis can't be misused.
Orne and Evans were clear about that.
It means this test can't answer the question,
because the laboratory setting
can make people assume they're safe.

<!-- S30 -->
It also explains a lot about stage shows.
The volunteers chose to be there.
The audience is watching.
Everyone knows what's meant to happen.

<!-- S31 -->
Some of them may genuinely feel it.
But someone clucking like a chicken on stage
tells you very little
about what a hypnotist could do to a stranger.

<!-- S32 -->
So is it all just the situation?
Not quite.

<!-- S33 -->
In a study published in 1968,
Orne's team gave seventeen highly responsive volunteers
a command.
For the next two days,
whenever you hear the word experiment,
your hand will rise and touch your forehead.

<!-- S34 -->
Then they told them to forget it.
Fourteen fakers got the same instruction.
In the lab, they all responded.

<!-- S35 -->
The real test was somewhere else.
It was the secretary in the waiting room.

<!-- S36 -->
As they left, she mentioned
the next part of the experiment.
The next day, she asked:
are you here for Dr Sheehan's experiment?

<!-- S37 -->
Not one of the fakers responded on both days.

<!-- S38 -->
Five of the hypnotised group did.
A hand rising to the forehead,
for a secretary.

<!-- S39 -->
So a command really can outlast the session,
with the hypnotist out of the room.

<!-- S40 -->
Now the small print.
That's five out of seventeen,
all picked because they responded exceptionally well.

<!-- S41 -->
And near the end, when it seemed obvious
the researcher was about to cancel it,
only two of the hypnotised group still responded.

<!-- S42 -->
So it isn't software.
It isn't guaranteed,
and the situation can switch it off.

<!-- S43 -->
But suggestion can reach further in
than you'd expect.
Try this.

<!-- S44 -->
Say the colour of each word.
Not the word. The colour.

<!-- S45 -->
You felt that.
Reading happens automatically.
Even when you're trying to ignore the word,
it gets in the way.

<!-- S46 -->
In 2002, Amir Raz and his colleagues
gave people a suggestion
to stop reading those words,
and pay attention only to the ink.

<!-- S47 -->
In sixteen highly suggestible people,
the delay disappeared.
In sixteen less suggestible people,
it didn't.

<!-- S48 -->
A later scan of a smaller group
found less activity
in the area linked to conflict,
and in the visual areas too.

<!-- S48b -->
A later study reduced that delay
with the suggestion alone, and no hypnosis,
in highly suggestible people.

<!-- S49 -->
Nobody took those people over.
They were doing something to their own reading.
Exactly how is still debated.

<!-- S50 -->
The most unsettling results, though,
are about memory.

<!-- S51 -->
In films, a hypnotist erases a memory.
What's been shown in the lab is far weaker.
People can be told to forget
what happened during the session itself.
It's temporary.
Give the agreed cue, and it comes back.

<!-- S52 -->
The opposite idea had a far better run:
that hypnosis can recover
what you've forgotten.

<!-- S53 -->
In 1976, in Chowchilla, California,
three men hijacked a school bus.
They locked the driver and twenty-six children
in a truck trailer buried in a quarry.
All of them escaped.

<!-- S54 -->
The driver, Ed Ray, had seen the kidnappers' vans.
Under hypnosis,
he came up with their number plates,
all but one digit.

<!-- S55 -->
It helped lead the police to the kidnappers.

<!-- S56 -->
Police in America were already training officers
in hypnosis.
It looked as if the mind keeps a recording,
and you just need a way to play it back.

<!-- S57 -->
In 1983, two researchers hypnotised
twenty-seven highly hypnotisable people.
They suggested that, one night the week before,
loud noises had woken them up.

<!-- S58 -->
That was invented.

<!-- S59 -->
Afterwards, thirteen of them stated
that it had really happened.

<!-- S60 -->
Nearly half, from one suggestion.

<!-- S61 -->
In 1985, the American Medical Association
reviewed the evidence.
Memories recovered under hypnosis
weren't more accurate, it concluded.
They appeared less reliable
than ordinary recall.

<!-- S62 -->
Ed Ray's plates could be checked.
That's what made them useful.
It doesn't show that hypnosis improves memory,
and most memories can't be checked at all.

<!-- S63 -->
Memory isn't a recording.
Each time you remember something,
you rebuild it,
and a suggestion can get into the rebuild.

<!-- S64 -->
So is hypnosis any use at all?
Here, the answer is a clear yes.

<!-- S65 -->
A 2019 review pooled eighty-five trials
of hypnosis against pain,
all with pain induced in healthy volunteers.

<!-- S66 -->
With direct suggestions against pain,
highly suggestible people
reported forty-two percent less.
Medium, twenty-nine percent.
Low, seventeen.

<!-- S67 -->
In New York, two hundred patients
having breast surgery
were randomly split.
Some got fifteen minutes of hypnosis beforehand.
The rest got someone who just listened.

<!-- S68 -->
The hypnosis group needed less of two drugs,
and reported about half as much pain.
On average, each patient cost seven hundred
and seventy-two dollars less,
mainly because the surgery was shorter.

<!-- S69 -->
The hypnosis came on top of normal anaesthesia,
not instead of it.
Those patients knew which group they were in,
and it's no replacement for medical care.

<!-- S70 -->
But notice what's happening here.
Nobody is being controlled.
Somebody agreed to it,
and their attention moved.

<!-- S71 -->
So can someone program your mind?
Not the way films show it.
There's no remote control.

<!-- S72 -->
But think about those volunteers.
A hand rising at reception.
A night of noises that never happened.
A page of sums with no sixes.

<!-- S74 -->
From the inside,
a suggestion doesn't feel like a suggestion.
It feels like you.

What's a memory you'd swear by,
and how would you actually check it?
