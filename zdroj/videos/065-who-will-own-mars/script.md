<!-- S01 -->
Imagine it's 2045.
The tenth Starship
has just landed on Mars.
There are no astronauts inside
planning to go home in a few months.

<!-- S02 -->
These people have just moved there.
And they brought construction equipment,
reactors, solar panels, factories...
everything they need to build
the first real city on another planet.

<!-- S03 -->
Now here's the question.
Who owns Mars?

<!-- S04 -->
Does it belong to Elon Musk?
Or to the United States?
Does SpaceX own it?
Or does nobody own it at all?

<!-- S05 -->
And what happens when, fifty years later,
there are millions of people
living on Mars?
Will they still answer to Earth?

<!-- S06 -->
Or will they eventually say:
"Thanks for the help.
But from now on, we govern ourselves."

<!-- S07 -->
In 1967,
the Outer Space Treaty came into force.
Among other things, it says
that no country can claim the Moon, Mars,
or any other celestial body
as its territory.

<!-- S08 -->
So if Elon Musk lands on Mars
and plants a SpaceX flag,
he can't say:
"I hereby declare Mars
to be part of the United States."

<!-- S09 -->
Mars isn't America.
And SpaceX isn't a country.

<!-- S09b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S10 -->
SpaceX, however, seems to see it
a little differently.
In 2020, it published the terms of service
for Starlink, its satellite internet.
And buried in them
is a sentence about Mars.

<!-- S11 -->
Both parties, it says,
recognise Mars as a free planet...
and that no Earth-based government
has authority or sovereignty
over Martian activities.

<!-- S12 -->
A satellite internet contract
that already tells Earth to stay out.
But the treaty has an answer
to that, too.

<!-- S13 -->
It says that private space activities
have to be authorised
and continuously supervised
by the appropriate state.

<!-- S14 -->
So if SpaceX is doing something on Mars,
the United States still has
international responsibilities
connected to those activities.

<!-- S15 -->
But what exactly does "supervise" mean
when the company is, most of the time,
hundreds of millions of kilometres away?

<!-- S16 -->
There is no lawyer from Washington
standing on Mars.
There is no American police force.
And you definitely can't
just send a sheriff over.

<!-- S17 -->
So let's imagine the first few decades.
SpaceX builds the first base.
Then another.

<!-- S18 -->
China builds one.
Europe builds another.
India builds another.
Maybe international partnerships
and private companies join in too.

<!-- S19 -->
And because Mars is really, really big...
nobody is going to occupy
the whole planet.
Instead, there will be
individual islands of civilisation.

<!-- S20 -->
One city here.
A mining operation
two thousand kilometres away.
A Chinese settlement somewhere else.

<!-- S21 -->
And between them...
nothing.
Thousands of kilometres
of Martian wilderness.

<!-- S22 -->
Here's another problem.
Suppose someone lands on Mars
and builds a city.
You own the city.
You own the buildings.
You own the reactor.
You own everything you've built.

<!-- S23 -->
But you don't own the land
it's sitting on.

<!-- S24 -->
Now imagine you have a settlement on Mars.
There are a hundred thousand people
living there.
You have a nuclear reactor.
You have a water supply.
And one day, another company arrives
and says:
"Hi. We're going to build our base
right here."

<!-- S25 -->
Right next to you.
And you say:
"Uh... we'd rather you didn't."

<!-- S26 -->
They say:
"Why not? Nobody owns this land."
And technically, they have a point.

<!-- S27 -->
But their landing could throw
enormous amounts of dust into the air.
They could contaminate
your water source.
They could damage your equipment.

<!-- S28 -->
So we start getting into the idea
of safety zones.
Not:
"This territory belongs to us."

<!-- S29 -->
But:
"Before you operate here,
talk to us first,
because you might interfere
with our activities."

<!-- S30 -->
In 2020, the United States
and seven other countries
signed the Artemis Accords...
principles for exploring the Moon,
Mars, comets and asteroids.
Safety zones are written right into them.

<!-- S31 -->
Legally, that's something very different
from owning the land.
Practically...
it starts looking suspiciously
like property.

<!-- S32 -->
When European powers colonised
other parts of the world,
they encountered enormous areas
that, from their perspective,
were simply... available.

<!-- S33 -->
Never mind that people
already lived there.

<!-- S34 -->
Then someone arrived.
They built a settlement.
More people arrived.
Governments appeared.

<!-- S35 -->
And eventually it became
rather difficult to say:
"It's just a few people
living somewhere far away."
Because it had become a society.

<!-- S36 -->
Take the American colonies.
Britain could say:
"You are our subjects."
And legally, that made sense.

<!-- S37 -->
But eventually the colonies had
their own cities, their own economy,
their own political institutions,
their own military,
and their own interests.

<!-- S38 -->
And eventually came the question:
why should we be governed
by a government
thousands of kilometres away?

<!-- S39 -->
Now put that on Mars.
Imagine the year 2150.
There are ten million people
living on Mars.

<!-- S40 -->
Most of them were born there.
They have never been to Earth.
Earth is something they know from history.
Like we know the Roman Empire today.

<!-- S41 -->
And then some government on Earth
tells them:
"Under our laws,
you are still our citizens."
The Martian government replies:
"No, we're not."

<!-- S42 -->
Earth insists:
"You have to obey our laws."
Mars asks:
"And why would we do that?"

<!-- S43 -->
"Because we founded you."
"That was a hundred years ago."

<!-- S44 -->
Now we reach a problem
that no law can completely solve.
A state isn't just a legal document.
It's also the ability
to actually exercise power.

<!-- S45 -->
If Washington gives an order
to a Martian settlement...
how exactly does it enforce it?
Do you send the police?
You have to transport them
to another planet.

<!-- S46 -->
Do you send the military?
Sure.
But an interplanetary war
is slightly more complicated
than sending tanks across a border.

<!-- S47 -->
And if Mars already has its own industry,
energy infrastructure and defences...
then the situation starts looking
a lot like any other independent power.

<!-- S48 -->
Could Mars itself become a country?
And here's the surprising part.
Current space law
doesn't really provide a clear answer.

<!-- S49 -->
The Outer Space Treaty says:
"You cannot claim Mars
as your territory."
But it doesn't provide
a particularly elegant answer
to this question:

<!-- S50 -->
what happens if,
after several generations,
a society develops on Mars
and decides to create its own state?

<!-- S51 -->
Two science-fiction shows
illustrate this beautifully.

<!-- S51b -->
First, For All Mankind.
Mars is still primarily a place
where Earth powers compete.
The United States. The Soviet Union.
Later, other players.

<!-- S52 -->
Everyone wants a base.
Everyone wants resources.
Everyone wants to be first.
Mars is essentially an extension
of Earth's geopolitics.

<!-- S53 -->
Then there's The Expanse.
And there, the situation
is completely different.
Mars isn't just a colony any more.
It's a civilisation of its own.

<!-- S54 -->
It has its own government.
Its own military.
Its own economy.
Its own culture.
And its own interests.

<!-- S55 -->
And that may be
what eventually happens in reality.
Not in twenty years.
Probably not in a hundred.

<!-- S56 -->
But if people stay on Mars
for several generations...
it's hard to imagine that it would remain
nothing more than
a distant colony of Earth forever.

<!-- S57 -->
Let's go back to Elon.
Because that's where we started.
Imagine SpaceX really does establish
the first Martian colony.
The first Martian city
might even be named Musk City.

<!-- S58 -->
But a hundred and fifty years later?
Millions of people are living there.
Elon Musk has been dead for generations.

<!-- S59 -->
And some teacher is telling students:
"The first Martian settlement
was established by a company
called SpaceX."
And some student asks:
"Wait, what was SpaceX?"

<!-- S60 -->
So here's the paradox.
The Outer Space Treaty
may prevent Elon Musk
from saying in 2045:
"Mars is mine."

<!-- S61 -->
But it may not prevent Mars
from saying, in 2200:
"Mars is ours."
And I think that's
the much more interesting question.

<!-- S62 -->
The first stage of colonisation
will probably look something like
For All Mankind.
Countries and corporations will compete
for bases, resources and prestige.

<!-- S63 -->
But if colonisation actually succeeds...
we might eventually end up
in something much closer
to The Expanse.

<!-- S64 -->
And then the important question
won't be "Who owns Mars?"
It will be:
who gets to decide how the people
living on Mars are governed?

<!-- S65 -->
Mars may never belong
to a single country.
It may never belong to SpaceX.
It may not even belong
to humanity as a whole.

<!-- S66 -->
Maybe one day it will have
new countries, new nations,
and new political identities.

<!-- S67 -->
And hundreds of years from now,
someone on Mars might look at Earth
and ask:
"So our ancestors
really came from there?"

<!-- S68 -->
And Earth will be to them
what Europe is to many people
in the Americas today:
the place their ancestors
once came from.

<!-- S69 -->
Perhaps that is where
the real history of Mars will begin.
Not when the first Starship lands.

<!-- S70 -->
But when the first Martians
look at Earth and say the sentence
humanity has heard
many times before:
"We are not your colony."
