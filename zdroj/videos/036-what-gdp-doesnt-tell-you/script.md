<!-- S01 -->
Imagine a hurricane
destroys thousands of houses.

<!-- S02 -->
People lose their homes.
Roads are damaged.
Businesses are destroyed.

<!-- S03 -->
And yet...
GDP can go up.

<!-- S04 -->
Wait.
How can destroying things
make an economy look better?

<!-- S05 -->
To understand that,
we need to talk about one of the most
important numbers in economics.

<!-- S06 -->
GDP.

<!-- S07 -->
GDP stands for
gross domestic product.

<!-- S08 -->
In simple terms, it measures
the market value of all final
goods and services produced
within a country's borders,
during a certain period of time.

<!-- S09 -->
Usually, we talk about a year
or a quarter.

<!-- S10 -->
So if factories produce cars,
companies provide software services,
restaurants serve food,
builders construct houses...

<!-- S11 -->
All of that contributes to GDP.

<!-- S12 -->
GDP is basically an attempt
to answer one question.
How much economic production
is happening?

<!-- S13 -->
And that's actually
a very useful question.

<!-- S14 -->
So why does everybody
care about GDP?

<!-- S15 -->
Because it gives us
a reasonably standardised way
to measure the size
and growth of an economy.

<!-- S16 -->
If GDP is growing, the economy
is generally producing more.

<!-- S17 -->
If GDP is shrinking,
economic activity
is generally declining.

<!-- S18 -->
And because we can calculate it
repeatedly, we can compare...
this year with last year,
one country with another,
or one period of economic growth
with another.

<!-- S19 -->
So now we can go back
to the hurricane.

<!-- S20 -->
The hurricane destroys houses.
That's terrible.

<!-- S21 -->
But then people have to rebuild them.

<!-- S22 -->
Construction companies get paid.
Workers get paid.
Materials are purchased.
Businesses provide services.

<!-- S23 -->
The government may spend
enormous amounts of money
repairing infrastructure.

<!-- S24 -->
All of that is economic activity.
And economic activity
contributes to GDP.

<!-- S25 -->
So GDP can increase after a disaster.

<!-- S26 -->
But nobody would seriously argue
that destroying a city
made its inhabitants richer.

<!-- S27 -->
This is an important distinction.
GDP measures activity.

<!-- S28 -->
It doesn't automatically measure
whether that activity
was good or bad.

<!-- S29 -->
And that's not the only
weird thing about GDP.

<!-- S30 -->
Imagine that a country produces
exactly the same amount of stuff
this year as last year.

<!-- S31 -->
But everything becomes
ten percent more expensive.

<!-- S32 -->
The country's GDP,
measured in current prices,
can still increase
by roughly ten percent.

<!-- S33 -->
Did the economy actually produce more?
No.
Prices simply went up.

<!-- S34 -->
That's why economists distinguish
between nominal GDP and real GDP.

<!-- S35 -->
Nominal GDP uses current prices.

<!-- S36 -->
Real GDP tries to remove
the effect of changing prices.

<!-- S37 -->
So when you hear that an economy
grew by, say, three percent,
you usually want to know
whether that's real economic growth,
rather than simply higher prices.

<!-- S38 -->
Another number you'll often see is...
GDP per capita.

<!-- S39 -->
Total GDP tells you
how big an economy is.

<!-- S40 -->
GDP per capita is more useful
when comparing people
in different countries.

<!-- S41 -->
And purchasing power parity, or PPP,
tries to account for the fact
that a dollar doesn't buy
the same amount everywhere.

<!-- S42 -->
But even GDP per capita
adjusted for PPP
has a major limitation.

<!-- S43 -->
It is an average.
And averages can hide
enormous differences.

<!-- S44 -->
GDP is also not
the same thing as wealth.
This is an easy mistake to make.

<!-- S45 -->
GDP measures economic production
over a period of time.

<!-- S46 -->
Wealth is about
what has accumulated over time.

<!-- S47 -->
Think of GDP as a flow.
And wealth as a stock.

<!-- S48 -->
A country can produce a huge amount
every year without everybody
becoming equally wealthy.

<!-- S49 -->
And a country can have
enormous accumulated wealth,
even if its current economic growth
is relatively slow.

<!-- S50 -->
So...
GDP is not a country's bank account.

<!-- S51 -->
And this is where
things start getting weird.

<!-- S52 -->
Imagine you cook dinner for your family.
You spend an hour preparing the food.
Your family eats it.

<!-- S53 -->
You've created something valuable.

<!-- S54 -->
But as far as GDP is concerned...
that hour didn't happen.

<!-- S55 -->
Now imagine you pay a restaurant
fifty dollars to prepare
exactly the same meal.

<!-- S56 -->
Suddenly there is
a market transaction.
And GDP increases.

<!-- S57 -->
The meal didn't magically
become more valuable.

<!-- S58 -->
The economic activity simply became
easier for the statistical system
to measure.

<!-- S59 -->
The same thing happens
with many forms of unpaid work.

<!-- S60 -->
Childcare.
Cleaning.
Cooking.
Taking care of relatives.

<!-- S61 -->
These things can be extremely valuable.
But a lot of them
don't appear in GDP.

<!-- S62 -->
And this isn't
a modern criticism of GDP.

<!-- S63 -->
Simon Kuznets, one of the people
who developed national income
accounting, warned about this problem
almost a century ago.

<!-- S64 -->
In a nineteen thirty-four report
to the US Senate, he explicitly noted
that the welfare of a nation
could not simply be inferred
from a measure of national income.

<!-- S65 -->
And unpaid household work
was among the things
left outside the measurement.

<!-- S66 -->
And GDP doesn't tell us
whether people are happy.

<!-- S67 -->
It doesn't directly tell us
whether people are healthy.
Whether they feel safe.
Whether they have free time.

<!-- S68 -->
Whether the environment is clean.
Or whether people actually
like the way they live.

<!-- S69 -->
Two countries can have
very similar GDP per capita...
and radically different
quality of life.

<!-- S70 -->
GDP simply wasn't designed
to answer those questions.

<!-- S71 -->
And the hurricane wasn't
the strangest example.

<!-- S72 -->
A country at war may produce
enormous quantities of weapons,
while destroying enormous
amounts of wealth.

<!-- S73 -->
All of the production is counted.
None of the destruction is subtracted.

<!-- S74 -->
There's a whole video about that one.

<!-- S75 -->
Pollution, crime, accidents and disease
can all generate
economic activity too.

<!-- S76 -->
None of this means
these things are good.
It simply means that GDP
isn't a moral score.

<!-- S77 -->
It doesn't give you points
for doing good things.
And it doesn't subtract points
for doing bad things.

<!-- S78 -->
So...
is GDP a terrible economic indicator?

<!-- S79 -->
No.
Quite the opposite.
GDP is incredibly useful.

<!-- S80 -->
If we want to know whether an economy
is producing more or less,
GDP gives us valuable information.

<!-- S81 -->
If we want to understand recessions,
recoveries and economic growth,
it's extremely useful.

<!-- S82 -->
It is not perfect.

<!-- S83 -->
But having a reasonably
consistent thermometer
is much better than having
no thermometer at all.

<!-- S84 -->
The problem isn't GDP.

<!-- S85 -->
The problem is asking GDP
to answer questions
it was never designed to answer.

<!-- S86 -->
Think of GDP as a thermometer.

<!-- S87 -->
A thermometer is great
at telling you the temperature.

<!-- S88 -->
But you would not use it
to measure your blood pressure.

<!-- S89 -->
And you certainly would not use it
to determine whether
you're happy with your life.

<!-- S90 -->
GDP works the same way.

<!-- S91 -->
It tells us something important
about the economy.

<!-- S92 -->
Just not everything that's important
about a society.

<!-- S93 -->
So the next time you hear...
"GDP increased by three percent."

<!-- S94 -->
Don't immediately translate that into...
"People are three percent better off."

<!-- S95 -->
That's not what it means.

<!-- S96 -->
It means that, according to
this particular measure,
the economy produced more
economic value than before.

<!-- S97 -->
And that's useful information.
But it's only one piece of the puzzle.

<!-- S98 -->
Because a country
is more than its economy.

<!-- S99 -->
And economic growth is not
automatically the same thing
as human progress.

<!-- S100 -->
GDP is a thermometer.

<!-- S101 -->
It was never meant to be
a complete medical examination.
