<!-- S01 -->
Every second,
tens of billions of particles
pass through your thumbnail.

<!-- S02 -->
Through your skin, your bones,
and straight through the Earth
beneath your feet.

<!-- S03 -->
Through your whole body,
it's about a hundred trillion
every second.

<!-- S04 -->
They don't stop. They don't slow down.
And almost none of them
ever touch you.

<!-- S05 -->
At night, they don't stop either.
They come up through the floor,
after crossing the entire planet.

<!-- S05b -->
Even your body gives off thousands
every second,
from the potassium inside it.

<!-- S06 -->
They're called neutrinos.
And for a long time, nobody was sure
they existed at all.

<!-- S07 -->
So how can something pass
through a whole planet
without hitting anything?
And if it never hits anything,
how do we know it's there?

<!-- S08 -->
The story starts
with something that went missing.

<!-- S09 -->
In 1914, James Chadwick
was studying beta decay,
a kind of radioactivity
where an atomic nucleus
throws out an electron.

<!-- S10 -->
Every nucleus of the same kind
releases the same amount of energy.
So every electron should come out
with the same energy, too.

<!-- S11 -->
They didn't.
Some came out fast, some slow,
spread across a whole range.

<!-- S12 -->
On average, the electrons
carried away only part of the energy.
The rest seemed to vanish.

<!-- S13 -->
This was serious.
Conservation of energy
is one of the rules
that physics relies on most.
Some physicists were ready to give it up.

<!-- S14 -->
In 1930, Wolfgang Pauli
suggested something else.
In a letter to a meeting of physicists,
he called it a desperate remedy.

<!-- S15 -->
What if the nucleus released
a second particle
alongside the electron?
Neutral, very light,
and invisible to every instrument
of the day.

<!-- S16 -->
It would carry off the missing energy.
The two particles would share it,
sometimes one way, sometimes the other.
That's why the electrons never matched.

<!-- S17 -->
Pauli called it the neutron.
But in 1932, the same James Chadwick
found a much heavier neutral particle,
and that one kept the name.

<!-- S18 -->
So Enrico Fermi gave Pauli's particle
a new one.
Neutrino.
Italian for "little neutral one".

<!-- S19 -->
But Pauli had a problem.
A particle that slips through everything
also slips through every detector.

<!-- S20 -->
The story goes that he bet
the astronomer Walter Baade
a case of champagne
that nobody would ever catch one.

<!-- S21 -->
For a quarter of a century,
it looked like a safe bet.

<!-- S22 -->
Then two American physicists,
Frederick Reines and Clyde Cowan,
turned the problem around.
If neutrinos almost never interact,
use an enormous number of them.

<!-- S23 -->
A nuclear reactor
produces huge numbers of antineutrinos,
the antimatter twins of neutrinos.
So they put tanks of water
next to a reactor in South Carolina.

<!-- S24 -->
Once in a long while,
an antineutrino hits a proton
in the water.
The proton becomes a neutron,
and a positron flies off.

<!-- S25 -->
The positron vanishes almost at once,
in a flash of two gamma rays.

<!-- S26 -->
A few millionths of a second later,
the neutron is swallowed
by cadmium dissolved in the water.
Another flash.

<!-- S27 -->
Flash, pause, flash.
Almost nothing else
makes that pattern.

<!-- S28 -->
On the fourteenth of June, 1956,
they sent Pauli a telegram.
They had found his particle.

<!-- S29 -->
Pauli wrote back:
"Everything comes to him
who knows how to wait."

<!-- S30 -->
The reply never reached them.
Reines only learned of it
many years later.

<!-- S31 -->
And the champagne?
According to the story, Pauli paid up,
and the theorists drank it.
The two experimenters got none.

<!-- S32 -->
So what is a neutrino,
and why does it pass through everything?

<!-- S33 -->
It's a relative of the electron.
But it has no electric charge,
so electric forces can't touch it.

<!-- S34 -->
And it ignores the strong force,
the one that holds
atomic nuclei together.

<!-- S35 -->
That leaves just two ways to interact.
Gravity, which is hopelessly weak
for a single particle.
And the weak force.

<!-- S36 -->
So it's not that atoms are mostly empty,
and neutrinos slip through the gaps.
If that were the reason,
everything would slip through.

<!-- S37 -->
It's that a neutrino can fly
right through an atomic nucleus,
and the chance of anything happening
is almost zero.

<!-- S38 -->
How close to zero?
Send a neutrino from the Sun
straight through the middle of the Earth.

<!-- S39 -->
Almost thirteen thousand kilometres
of rock and iron.
Fewer than one in a billion
will hit anything on the way.

<!-- S40 -->
So build a wall of lead.
Not a metre thick.
Not a kilometre.
A light-year thick.

<!-- S41 -->
That's nearly ten trillion kilometres
of solid lead.

<!-- S42 -->
Fire neutrinos from the Sun
at that wall,
and most of them
would come out the other side.

<!-- S43 -->
And the Sun is firing them at us
all the time.

<!-- S44 -->
In its core, hydrogen fuses into helium.
Every time four hydrogen nuclei
become one helium nucleus,
two neutrinos are born.

<!-- S45 -->
The Sun does this so often
that it releases about
a hundred and eighty
trillion trillion trillion neutrinos
every second.

<!-- S45b -->
That sounds absurd.
But the Sun gives off about
ten million times more particles of light.
We just happen to see those.

<!-- S46 -->
By the time they reach us,
that's sixty-five billion every second
through every square centimetre.
Your thumbnail, from the start.

<!-- S47 -->
And they get out fast.
The energy that leaves the Sun as light
spends tens of thousands of years
working its way up from the core.

<!-- S48 -->
A neutrino leaves in about two seconds.
Eight minutes later,
it's passing through you.

<!-- S49 -->
So the sunlight you see today
began its journey out of the core
long before the pyramids.
A neutrino tells you
what the core was doing
eight minutes ago.

<!-- S50 -->
But how do you catch something
that a light-year of lead won't stop?

<!-- S51 -->
You make the target enormous.
And you wait.

<!-- S52 -->
In Japan, a kilometre under a mountain,
sits Super-Kamiokande.
A tank holding fifty thousand tonnes
of ultra-pure water.

<!-- S53 -->
Its walls are lined with
more than eleven thousand light sensors,
each half a metre across.

<!-- S54 -->
Very rarely, a neutrino
slams into an electron in the water,
hard enough to send it racing
faster than light travels through water.
It leaves a faint ring of blue light.

<!-- S55 -->
Every day, about a hundred thousand
billion billion solar neutrinos
pass through that tank.

<!-- S56 -->
It catches around fifteen.

<!-- S57 -->
Long before Super-Kamiokande,
someone tried to count
the Sun's neutrinos
with a much stranger tank.

<!-- S58 -->
In the 1960s, Ray Davis
built it almost a mile underground,
in a gold mine in South Dakota.

<!-- S59 -->
He filled it with
three hundred and eighty thousand litres
of dry-cleaning fluid.

<!-- S60 -->
The fluid is full of chlorine.
Every so often, a neutrino from the Sun
turns one chlorine atom into argon.

<!-- S61 -->
Every couple of months,
Davis flushed the argon out
and counted it.
Not grams of it.
Individual atoms.

<!-- S62 -->
He found about one new atom
every two days.

<!-- S63 -->
And that was the problem.
The best models of the Sun
predicted three times as many.

<!-- S64 -->
Two thirds of the Sun's neutrinos
seemed to be missing.

<!-- S65 -->
Either the experiment was wrong,
or our picture of the Sun was wrong.
For more than thirty years,
nobody could say which.

<!-- S66 -->
It was the second time
something had gone missing
around neutrinos.
And this time, the answer was stranger.

<!-- S67 -->
Neutrinos come in three kinds,
or flavours.
Electron neutrinos.
Muon neutrinos.
Tau neutrinos.

<!-- S67b -->
What sets them apart is their partner.
When a neutrino does hit something,
it can turn into an electron, a muon or a tau,
the electron's two heavier cousins.

<!-- S68 -->
The Sun makes only electron neutrinos.
And Davis's tank could only catch
electron neutrinos.

<!-- S69 -->
But on the way to Earth,
a neutrino doesn't keep its flavour.
It switches between them,
back and forth, as it travels.

<!-- S70 -->
By the time they arrived,
many had turned into flavours
the tank simply couldn't see.

<!-- S71 -->
By 2002, a detector in Canada
that could see every flavour
counted all the neutrinos from the Sun.

<!-- S72 -->
None were missing.
They had just changed.

<!-- S73 -->
And that has a consequence.
A particle can only switch like this
if it has mass.

<!-- S74 -->
For decades, the simplest theory
had treated neutrinos as massless.
They aren't.
That discovery won a Nobel Prize
in 2015.

<!-- S75 -->
Pauli thought his particle
might weigh about as much
as an electron.

<!-- S76 -->
Today, we know it's at least
a million times lighter.
And we still don't know
exactly how much it weighs.

<!-- S77 -->
A particle that almost never interacts
sounds useless.
It's the opposite.

<!-- S78 -->
On the twenty-third of February, 1987,
in a neighbouring galaxy,
a giant star collapsed.

<!-- S79 -->
Almost all the energy of the collapse
left as neutrinos.
They streamed straight out of the star,
while its light was still trapped inside.

<!-- S79b -->
And they probably did more than escape.
Physicists think a small share of them
was absorbed on the way out,
and that's what blew the star apart.

<!-- S80 -->
After a hundred and sixty-eight
thousand years in space,
twenty-five of them were caught
in Japan, the United States
and the Soviet Union.

<!-- S81 -->
All within thirteen seconds.
Two to three hours
before its light reached us.

<!-- S81b -->
Closer to home,
neutrinos from inside the Earth
have shown that about half of its heat
comes from radioactivity.

<!-- S82 -->
And there's one more population
of neutrinos.
The oldest of all.

<!-- S83 -->
About one second after the Big Bang,
the universe thinned out enough
for neutrinos to stop interacting.
They've been flying freely ever since.

<!-- S83b -->
Just before they broke free,
they helped decide how many neutrons survived,
and so how much helium the universe made.
About a quarter of all ordinary matter.

<!-- S84 -->
According to our best calculations,
every cubic centimetre of space
holds about three hundred
and thirty-six of them.

<!-- S85 -->
And the neutrinos from the Sun?
Only about two
in that same cubic centimetre.

<!-- S86 -->
The neutrinos from the first second
of the universe
outnumber them
a hundred and fifty to one.

<!-- S87 -->
We've seen their fingerprints
in the oldest light in the universe.
But no detector has ever caught one.
They carry far too little energy.

<!-- S87b -->
So will space keep filling up with them?
No. Every star that has ever shone
has added only a trickle.

<!-- S87c -->
The first second of the universe
made tens of billions of times more
than all the stars since.

<!-- S87d -->
And as the universe expands,
they spread thinner and thinner.
They've cooled to about two degrees
above absolute zero.

<!-- S87e -->
But they still have mass.
They move too fast to settle into clumps,
so even that tiny mass smooths out
the cosmic web, just slightly,
and that's how astronomers
are trying to weigh them.

<!-- S88 -->
So right now, while you watch this,
tens of billions of neutrinos
from the Sun
are passing through your thumbnail.

<!-- S89 -->
And all around you,
neutrinos from the first second
of the universe are drifting by,
almost without a trace.

<!-- S90 -->
And yet, a particle
that barely touches anything
has let us see inside the Sun,
inside an exploding star,
and back to the first second
of the universe.

So here's a question for you.
Did it surprise you
just how many neutrinos
are passing through you right now?
