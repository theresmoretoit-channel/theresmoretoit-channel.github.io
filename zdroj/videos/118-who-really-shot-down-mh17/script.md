<!-- S01 -->
On 17 July 2014, a Boeing 777
left Amsterdam for Kuala Lumpur.
Flight MH17.

<!-- S02 -->
Less than three hours later,
over eastern Ukraine,
it broke apart in the sky.
Two hundred and ninety-eight people died.

<!-- S03 -->
Twelve years later,
while we were preparing this video,
a clip from Russian radio
went round the internet.
In it, a retired Russian colonel
appeared to say:
"A Russian officer shot it down, yes."

<!-- S04 -->
It looked like the admission
investigators had been waiting for.

<!-- S05 -->
We'll come back to what he actually said.
Because to judge a confession,
you first need to know
what the evidence says without it.

<!-- S06 -->
So who really shot down MH17?
And how can anyone know,
when the crime scene was in a war zone,
and the truth itself became a weapon?

<!-- S07 -->
The first question was the simplest one.
What destroyed the aircraft?

<!-- S08 -->
The Dutch Safety Board answered it
in October 2015.
A warhead exploded just above
and to the left of the cockpit.

<!-- S09 -->
It sprayed the front of the plane
with hundreds of metal fragments.
The three people in the cockpit
were killed instantly.

<!-- S10 -->
Some of those fragments
had an unusual shape.
A bow tie.

<!-- S11 -->
That shape comes from one kind of warhead:
the one carried by the Buk,
a Soviet-designed surface-to-air missile.

<!-- S12 -->
Investigators found those fragments
in the wreckage,
and in the body of the captain.
And they rebuilt the front of the plane
from recovered pieces,
to check that the damage fitted.

<!-- S13 -->
So within fifteen months,
the weapon was known.
But a weapon is not a person.
It doesn't tell you who fired it.

<!-- S13b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S14 -->
For the people on board,
it came without warning.

<!-- S15 -->
A blast, a sudden loss of pressure,
violent wind and cold,
and an aircraft coming apart.

<!-- S16 -->
The fall lasted
a minute to a minute and a half.
Whether anyone stayed aware for part of it,
the investigators could neither confirm
nor rule out.

<!-- S17 -->
What they did judge likely
is that people were barely able
to understand what was happening.

<!-- S18 -->
That is why precision matters here.
Two hundred and ninety-eight people,
and their families,
are owed the right answer.

<!-- S19 -->
Then there was the crime scene.

<!-- S20 -->
The plane came down near Hrabove,
in territory held
by Russian-backed separatists,
in the middle of a war.

<!-- S21 -->
International monitors
arrived the next day.
Armed men decided where they could go.

<!-- S22 -->
The bodies were taken away by train.
The flight recorders
were handed over four days later.

<!-- S23 -->
Parts of the wreckage
were moved or cut up.
Dutch investigators could not search
the site properly for months.

<!-- S24 -->
So here is the problem.
If the people holding the crime scene
are part of the conflict,
how do you trust anything found there?

<!-- S25 -->
The answer is
that you don't have to rely on it alone.
Because a lot of the evidence
was never lying in that field.

<!-- S26 -->
Some of it was posted online
within about an hour of the crash.

<!-- S27 -->
A page on VKontakte,
Russia's version of Facebook,
called "Dispatches from Strelkov",
announced that an An-26
had been shot down near Torez.

<!-- S28 -->
Strelkov was the field name
of Igor Girkin,
the separatists' military commander.

<!-- S29 -->
An An-26 is a military transport plane.
The post added:
"We warned them.
Do not fly in our sky."

<!-- S30 -->
When it became clear
that the plane was a passenger jet,
the post disappeared.

<!-- S31 -->
So what does that prove?
Not that Girkin wrote it.
The page was run by his supporters,
and he has denied writing it.

<!-- S32 -->
Not who fired the missile, either.

<!-- S33 -->
What it does show
is that so soon after the crash,
someone on the separatist side
was claiming a shoot-down,
at that place and that time,
of what they took for a military plane.

<!-- S34 -->
Phone calls intercepted later that day
point the same way.
Separatist fighters
realised the plane had been civilian.

<!-- S35 -->
Those calls came
from Ukraine's security service,
which is one side of the war.
So the investigators had them checked
for signs of editing.

<!-- S36 -->
Then there were the photographs.

<!-- S37 -->
Earlier that day, people in Donetsk,
Torez and Snizhne
photographed and filmed
a Buk launcher,
mostly on a low loader,
moving east.

<!-- S38 -->
A single photo proves very little.
It can be misdated, staged,
or taken somewhere else.

<!-- S39 -->
So each one was checked.
Shadows against the time of day.
Buildings against satellite images.
Posts against when they first appeared.

<!-- S40 -->
The next morning,
a video from Luhansk
showed the same kind of launcher
heading for Russia,
with one missile missing.

<!-- S41 -->
And it wasn't just any launcher.
Marks on its side
matched a vehicle photographed
weeks earlier in a Russian army convoy.

<!-- S42 -->
In 2018, the international
criminal investigation,
the Joint Investigation Team,
concluded that the launcher
came from Russia's
fifty-third Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade,
based in Kursk.

<!-- S43 -->
No single piece proves the case.
What matters is how the photographs,
recordings and physical evidence
fit together,
and whether independent checks
support the same conclusion.

<!-- S44 -->
Russia offered other explanations.
Several of them.

<!-- S45 -->
In July 2014,
the Russian Defence Ministry
pointed to a Ukrainian Su-25 warplane
flying near the airliner.

<!-- S46 -->
In 2015, the Buk's Russian maker
agreed that it was a Buk,
but said it was an older missile,
fired from a village held by Ukraine.

<!-- S47 -->
In 2016, the ministry released
new radar data.
This time, there was no warplane
near MH17 at all.

<!-- S48 -->
In 2018, it produced a serial number,
and said the missile
had belonged to Ukraine.

<!-- S49 -->
Here we need to be careful.
Changing stories don't prove guilt.
People who are innocent
can get things wrong, too.

<!-- S50 -->
What the changing stories did, in practice,
was offer a choice of answers.
And when there are so many answers,
it's easy to feel there's no answer at all.

<!-- S51 -->
The investigators examined
the Russian claims.
None of them changed the conclusion.

<!-- S52 -->
Which brings us back to that colonel.

<!-- S53 -->
His name is Viktor Baranets.
Once a spokesman
for a Russian defence minister,
now a military commentator
for the newspaper Komsomolskaya Pravda.

<!-- S54 -->
For years, he had dismissed
the case against Russia.

<!-- S55 -->
Then, on the paper's radio station
in September 2026, he said:
"A Russian officer shot it down, yes,
because that pest
barged into our sky."

<!-- S56 -->
A few weeks later, the clip went viral.
It looked like the missing piece.
It even echoed the Strelkov post: "our sky".
So let's check it
the way we checked everything else.

<!-- S57 -->
In the same answer, he said
"Soviet officers" did it.
He said the plane had been warned
by radio, by manoeuvres,
and by tracer fire across its nose.

<!-- S58 -->
None of that happened to MH17.
No fighter chased it.
It was hit by a missile from the ground.

<!-- S59 -->
But it matches another shootdown,
as Moscow described it at the time.

<!-- S60 -->
On 1 September 1983,
a Soviet fighter shot down
Korean Air Lines Flight 007,
which had strayed into Soviet airspace.
Two hundred and sixty-nine people died.

<!-- S61 -->
There, "our sky" makes sense.
Baranets almost certainly
mixed up two tragedies.

<!-- S62 -->
So it isn't a confession.
Not one we can honestly use.

<!-- S63 -->
And this is the uncomfortable part.
We wanted it to be true.
It fitted what the evidence already says.

<!-- S64 -->
That is exactly when
you have to check hardest.
A claim that confirms what you believe
gets the same test
as one that denies it.

<!-- S65 -->
And the case doesn't need him.

<!-- S66 -->
So what has actually been proven?

<!-- S67 -->
In November 2022, a Dutch court
convicted three men
for their part in the downing.
Igor Girkin, Sergei Dubinsky
and Leonid Kharchenko.
Life sentences, in their absence.

<!-- S68 -->
A fourth man, Oleg Pulatov,
was acquitted.
That matters, too.
The court weighed each man separately.

<!-- S69 -->
In 2023, the investigators
reported "strong indications"
that the decision to supply the Buk
was taken by President Putin.

<!-- S70 -->
Strong indications are not proof.
And supplying a weapon
is not ordering it to be fired.
The investigators could not establish
who ordered the missile to be launched.

<!-- S71 -->
The crew were never identified
well enough to be charged.
Most likely, they took it
for a military plane.

<!-- S72 -->
The investigation was suspended.
In the investigators' words,
it had reached its limits.

<!-- S73 -->
In 2025, two international bodies,
the UN's civil aviation council
and the European Court of Human Rights,
found Russia responsible.

<!-- S74 -->
And one more failure
belongs on the list.

<!-- S75 -->
Ukraine had closed its eastern airspace
up to thirty-two thousand feet.
MH17 was flying at thirty-three thousand.

<!-- S76 -->
The Safety Board found
that the flight broke no rules,
but that Ukraine had reason
to close the airspace completely,
and that nobody had properly
weighed the risk.

<!-- S77 -->
That doesn't excuse the missile.
Several failures can sit
inside one tragedy.

<!-- S78 -->
So, who really shot down MH17?

<!-- S79 -->
A Buk missile,
from a Russian army launcher,
fired from separatist-held ground.
Three men convicted.
And the hand that fired it,
still without a name.

<!-- S80 -->
Gaps like that shouldn't be hidden.
But they don't mean
that nothing can be known.

<!-- S81 -->
None of this rests on a confession.
It rests on pieces of evidence
that were checked,
and still fit together.

<!-- S82 -->
The truth isn't the claim
that sounds right.
It's the one still standing
after we check it.
