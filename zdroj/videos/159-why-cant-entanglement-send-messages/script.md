<!-- S01 -->
In 2016, China launched a satellite
called Micius.
One of its jobs
was to make pairs of photons
and split them up.

<!-- S02 -->
One photon of each pair went down
to an observatory in one city,
the other to an observatory in another,
twelve hundred kilometres away.

<!-- S03 -->
At each end, a detector measured
the photon that arrived.
And the results were linked more tightly
than anything ordinary allows.

<!-- S04 -->
Is something passing between them?
In 2008, physicists near Geneva
tried to time it,
between two villages
eighteen kilometres apart.

<!-- S05 -->
Assuming the universe has
a preferred frame,
in which Earth moves slowly
compared to light,
they found that anything travelling
from one photon to the other
would need to go at least
ten thousand times faster than light.

<!-- S06 -->
That's a limit on something hypothetical,
not a speed anyone measured.
But it's a strange one.

<!-- S07 -->
So here's the obvious idea.
Keep one photon of each pair,
and give the other
to a friend on Mars.

<!-- S08 -->
Today a radio message to Mars
takes up to twenty-two minutes.
With entangled photons,
you measure yours, and your friend knows.

<!-- S09 -->
It doesn't work.
Not with better detectors,
not with better satellites.
Not ever, as far as physics knows.

<!-- S10 -->
And the reason is stranger
than the link itself.

<!-- S11 -->
First, what entanglement actually is.
A source makes two particles together
and sends one to Alice,
and one to Bob.

<!-- S12 -->
Each of them measures a particle
and gets one of two answers.
Call them zero and one.

<!-- S13 -->
Alice's answer is random.
So is Bob's.
But when they set their detectors
the same way, the answers match.
In the ideal case, every time.

<!-- S14 -->
Einstein never accepted
that quantum mechanics
was the complete story.
In a letter to Max Born in 1947,
he called this
spooky action at a distance.

<!-- S15 -->
But hold on.
Is that really so strange?

<!-- S16 -->
The physicist John Bell
had a colleague, Reinhold Bertlmann,
who liked to wear two socks
of different colours.

<!-- S17 -->
See that one sock is pink,
and you know the other one isn't.
Instantly, at any distance.
Nothing spooky about it.

<!-- S18 -->
So maybe particles are the same.
Maybe they carry their answers
from the start,
like socks chosen that morning.

<!-- S19 -->
In 1981, Bell used those socks
to explain why that idea fails.

<!-- S20 -->
Here's the simplest version of the test,
from the physicist David Mermin.
Two boxes, far apart,
each with a switch with three positions.
A particle arrives,
and the box flashes red or green.

<!-- S21 -->
For every particle,
Alice and Bob each pick a position
at random,
without knowing what the other picked.

<!-- S22 -->
When they happen to pick
the same position,
the colours always match.

<!-- S23 -->
So suppose each pair of particles
carries instructions.
A colour for each position,
the same list in both.

<!-- S24 -->
Take the list red, red, green.
There are nine ways
to set the two switches.
In five of them, the colours match.

<!-- S25 -->
Try any other list.
None gives fewer than five.
So if the answers are written in advance,
the colours match
at least five times out of nine.

<!-- S26 -->
Quantum mechanics predicts
exactly half.

<!-- S27 -->
Five ninths against one half.
Experiments have tested gaps like this
since 1972,
and they side with quantum mechanics.

<!-- S28 -->
In 2015, a team in Delft
closed the two biggest loopholes at once.
And in 2022, three pioneers
of these experiments,
John Clauser, Alain Aspect
and Anton Zeilinger,
shared the Nobel Prize for their own work.

<!-- S29 -->
So the answers can't all be written
in advance,
at least not in any way
that stays local.

<!-- S30 -->
That doesn't mean one particle
sends a signal to the other.
And that is exactly where
our message runs into trouble.

<!-- S31 -->
Let's try to send one.
Alice wants to send Bob a single bit.
She measures her particle
and gets whatever she gets.

<!-- S32 -->
She can't choose.
Zero or one, the result is random.
Bob gets the matching answer,
but Alice didn't put anything in it.

<!-- S33 -->
And Bob can't even tell
whether Alice measured at all.

<!-- S34 -->
Say Bob measures a thousand particles.
About five hundred zeros,
about five hundred ones.

<!-- S35 -->
Now Alice measures her side,
any way she likes.
Bob measures another thousand.
About five hundred, and five hundred.

<!-- S36 -->
Nothing on his side changes.

<!-- S37 -->
The connection only becomes visible
when Alice and Bob compare their records.
And to compare them, they have to send them,
at the speed of light or slower.

<!-- S38 -->
But Alice does control one thing.
She chooses how to set her detector.
In Mermin's boxes, that's the switch.
Her choice changes how her results
line up with Bob's.
But that only shows when they compare.

<!-- S39 -->
So could Bob notice it?
Position one means zero,
position two means one?

<!-- S40 -->
No.
Whatever Alice does to her particle,
measuring it, turning it,
or leaving it alone,
Bob still sees red half the time
and green half the time.

<!-- S41 -->
It's called
the no-communication theorem,
and physicists proved it
around 1980.

<!-- S42 -->
Now imagine Alice could force
her result,
and the link still held.
Force a zero.
Force a one.

<!-- S43 -->
Then Bob would get her bit
every time he measured.
That would be a message,
faster than light.

<!-- S44 -->
So the randomness isn't a flaw
in our equipment.
It's part of the reason
the link can't carry a message.

<!-- S45 -->
But that's standard quantum mechanics.
What if the theory itself
is very slightly wrong?

<!-- S46 -->
In 1989, Steven Weinberg,
who had won a Nobel Prize
ten years earlier,
asked exactly that.

<!-- S47 -->
Weinberg wrote down a version
with a tiny change to its mathematics,
a so-called nonlinear correction.
He wasn't claiming it was correct.
He wanted experiments to test it.

<!-- S48 -->
Experiments looked for it
with extreme precision,
and found nothing.

<!-- S49 -->
Then came a deeper problem.
In 1990, Nicolas Gisin showed
that Weinberg's theory,
with the usual rules of measurement,
would let Alice signal Bob.

<!-- S50 -->
Instantly.
At any distance.

<!-- S51 -->
Joseph Polchinski looked closer.
You could block the signal
between Alice and Bob,
but the theory still allowed communication
between branches of the wave function.

<!-- S52 -->
In principle, between branches
where an observer got different outcomes.
He called it the Everett phone.

<!-- S53 -->
Not every nonlinear theory
has to fail this way.
But the lesson was clear.
The rule against signalling
isn't an extra restriction.
It follows from standard quantum mechanics.

<!-- S54 -->
Why does that matter?
As our video on faster-than-light travel
showed, a signal faster than light
could arrive before it was sent.

<!-- S55 -->
Entanglement doesn't let information
cross that line.
It creates correlations,
but carries no signal.

<!-- S56 -->
Even quantum teleportation,
from our video on teleportation,
needs two ordinary bits,
sent the ordinary way.

<!-- S57 -->
Without them,
Bob's result is indistinguishable
from noise.

<!-- S58 -->
So that's the first mystery solved.
Entanglement can't carry a message.
But there's a second one.
The strength of the link can be measured,
and it has a surprising limit.

<!-- S59 -->
Here's a game.
Alice and Bob each get a random question,
zero or one,
and each answers zero or one.

<!-- S60 -->
They can't talk.
They win if their answers match,
except when both get question one.
Then the answers must differ.

<!-- S61 -->
With any plan agreed in advance,
the best they can do
is win three times out of four.

<!-- S62 -->
With entangled particles,
they can win about eighty-five per cent
of the time,
and experiments keep beating three in four.

<!-- S63 -->
But not by more.
In 1980, Boris Tsirelson proved
that quantum mechanics
tops out at eighty-five point four.

<!-- S64 -->
In 1994, Sandu Popescu
and Daniel Rohrlich
asked a simple question.
Why stop there?

<!-- S65 -->
They imagined a box
that wins the game every time.
It isn't a real device,
just mathematics.

<!-- S66 -->
But here's the surprise.

<!-- S67 -->
It still can't send a message.
Each side, on its own,
gives zero or one at random.
Bob sees fifty-fifty, whatever Alice asks.

<!-- S68 -->
Stronger links than nature allows,
and still nothing faster than light.

<!-- S69 -->
So why does nature stop
at eighty-five?
Not seventy-five.
Not a hundred.

<!-- S70 -->
Physicists have proposed principles.
One result shows that above about
ninety-one per cent,
problems that normally need
enormous amounts of communication
could be solved with just one bit.

<!-- S71 -->
Another, called information causality,
gives the same eighty-five point four limit.
But it doesn't fully explain
why quantum mechanics
works the way it does.

<!-- S72 -->
So far, no agreed principle explains
why nature allows exactly
these correlations, and nothing stronger.

<!-- S73 -->
So, two photons,
twelve hundred kilometres apart.
Not one bit gets through.
And no quantum link, however perfect,
gets past eighty-five per cent.

<!-- S74 -->
We know why the link
can't carry a message.
We still don't know
why it isn't stronger.

<!-- S75 -->
So here's a question for you.
Is there a deeper rule
behind eighty-five per cent,
or is that just how
our universe turned out?
Let me know in the comments.
