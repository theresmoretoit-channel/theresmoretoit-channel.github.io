# Where Did Health Insurance Come From?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Have you ever wondered why,
when you get sick,
you can simply go to a doctor?

<!-- S02 -->
You might have to pay.

You might have to wait.

You might have to deal with paperwork.

<!-- S03 -->
But the idea that treatment
is something you can claim
when you need it
seems completely normal.

<!-- S04 -->
It isn't.

In historical terms,
public health insurance
is a surprisingly recent invention.

<!-- S05 -->
To understand why,
we need to go back to a time
when getting sick
meant something very different.

<!-- S06 -->
Imagine living in Europe
around eighteen hundred.

You are a craftsman,
a farm worker,
or one of the growing number
working in a factory.

<!-- S07 -->
You get seriously ill.

You can't work.

And because you can't work,
you don't get paid.

<!-- S08 -->
There is no national health service.

No insurance card.

There may not even be
a doctor nearby.

<!-- S09 -->
If you have money,
you can pay for treatment.

If you are poor,
you depend on charity.

<!-- S10 -->
And if the illness lasts for months,
you have a much bigger problem.

You are not just sick.

You have lost your income.

<!-- S11 -->
The basic idea of sharing risk
is actually ancient.

<!-- S12 -->
In the Roman world
there were mutual associations.

Members paid in every month
and could get help
when something went wrong.

Some of them paid for funerals.

<!-- S13 -->
Medieval guilds continued this.

A guild controlled a trade,
but it was also
a primitive safety net.

A member who became ill
might receive assistance.

So might his widow.

<!-- S14 -->
So the basic principle
was already there.

Everyone contributes a little.

And when one member
suffers a misfortune,
the common fund helps them.

<!-- S15 -->
But this wasn't
modern health insurance.

The communities were small.

Membership was voluntary.

And the help was limited.

<!-- S16 -->
Then the Industrial Revolution
changed everything.

It didn't just create factories
and steam engines.

It changed the relationship
between people and risk.

<!-- S17 -->
Millions of people moved to cities.

They no longer lived in villages
where the whole family
lived nearby.

<!-- S18 -->
They became wage workers.

And for a wage worker,
illness could be
financially devastating.

<!-- S19 -->
So workers began creating
their own organisations.

Mutual aid societies.

Friendly societies.

Sickness funds.

<!-- S20 -->
They contributed
a small amount regularly.

And if one of them became ill,
the fund could provide money,
or pay for treatment.

<!-- S21 -->
This was much closer
to modern health insurance.

But it was still voluntary,
and it still didn't cover everyone.

<!-- S22 -->
By the late nineteenth century,
Germany was a great industrial power.

And its government was worried
about something else.

The growing socialist
and workers' movement.

<!-- S23 -->
Enter Otto von Bismarck.

Bismarck was not a socialist.

Quite the opposite.

<!-- S24 -->
In eighteen seventy-eight,
his government banned
socialist organisations outright.

<!-- S25 -->
He was a conservative
who wanted to preserve
the existing political order.

And he realised
something rather clever.

<!-- S26 -->
If workers had protection
against the risks
of industrial life,
they might have less reason
for revolution.

<!-- S27 -->
So Germany introduced
compulsory sickness insurance
in eighteen eighty-three.

Five years after the ban.

<!-- S28 -->
It is generally considered
one of the foundations
of modern public health insurance.

Workers and employers paid in.

The funds paid for treatment
and sickness benefits.

<!-- S29 -->
It was not free healthcare.

It was insurance.

And that distinction is important.

<!-- S30 -->
The idea was not this.

The government will simply pay
for everyone's healthcare.

<!-- S31 -->
It was this.

Everyone in the covered group
pays in, and the fund pays
when someone becomes ill.

<!-- S32 -->
This became known
as the Bismarck model.

<!-- S33 -->
The German experiment
was hugely influential.

<!-- S34 -->
Austria-Hungary introduced
sickness insurance
a few years later.

Britain introduced
the National Insurance system
in nineteen eleven.

<!-- S35 -->
And after the First World War,
Czechoslovakia inherited
a system based
on the earlier Austro-Hungarian model.

<!-- S36 -->
The general idea was spreading.

Healthcare could be treated
as a collective risk.

<!-- S37 -->
There is another famous model,
and it came from Britain.

<!-- S38 -->
During the Second World War,
the economist William Beveridge
proposed a very different approach.

<!-- S39 -->
Instead of insurance contributions,
healthcare could be funded
through general taxation.

<!-- S40 -->
The result was
the National Health Service,
created in nineteen forty-eight.

<!-- S41 -->
And now there were
two major approaches.

The Bismarck model.

You pay into health insurance funds.

<!-- S42 -->
The Beveridge model.

You pay taxes,
and the government
finances healthcare.

<!-- S43 -->
Neither system appeared naturally.

Both were political choices.

<!-- S44 -->
And both were responses
to the same problem.

Modern medicine is expensive.

Illness is unpredictable.

And almost nobody can save enough
for every possible
medical disaster.

<!-- S45 -->
So what happened
in the United States?

Well...

<!-- S46 -->
The United States
did not choose either model.

It gradually built something
much more complicated.

<!-- S47 -->
Today, Americans get healthcare
through several different systems.

There is private insurance.

There is employer-sponsored insurance.

<!-- S48 -->
There is Medicare,
mainly for older Americans.

There is Medicaid
for eligible low-income people,
with the rules varying by state.

<!-- S49 -->
There is the Veterans
Health Administration.

And millions of people
still pay large amounts themselves.

<!-- S50 -->
So when someone says this.

America has private healthcare.

That's not really accurate.

<!-- S51 -->
And when someone says this.

America has public healthcare.

That's not accurate either.

<!-- S52 -->
America has both.

The United States never completely
rejected public healthcare.

<!-- S53 -->
It built public programmes
for particular groups,
while much of the rest
of the population
relied on private insurance.

A giant historical patchwork.

<!-- S54 -->
And one part of that patchwork
is especially strange.

In much of Europe,
changing your job doesn't mean
losing your health insurance.

<!-- S55 -->
In the United States,
employer-sponsored health insurance
became the dominant way
many Americans got coverage.

<!-- S56 -->
And it happened almost by accident.

During the Second World War,
the government froze wages.

<!-- S57 -->
Factories were desperate for workers
but were not allowed to pay them more.

So they offered
health insurance instead.

<!-- S58 -->
It didn't count as wages.

And it wasn't taxed
like wages either.

By the end of the war,
the number of Americans covered
had tripled.

<!-- S59 -->
Nobody had chosen this
as a national health policy.

<!-- S60 -->
So you change jobs...

and your healthcare
changes with it.

Lose your job...

and you may have to find
another way to get insured.

<!-- S61 -->
It is the result
of historical decisions
piling up over decades.

<!-- S62 -->
Imagine you are sick.

In a typical European system,
the basic question is usually this.

Where can I get treatment?

<!-- S63 -->
In the United States,
there are several more.

Is the doctor in my network?

Does my insurance cover this?

<!-- S64 -->
Have I reached my deductible?

How much will the hospital charge?

And how much
will I actually pay?

<!-- S65 -->
The result is that two people
getting the same treatment
can have completely different
financial experiences.

<!-- S66 -->
So why did Europe go one way
and America another?

There isn't one simple answer.

History matters.

Institutions matter.

<!-- S67 -->
And timing matters most of all.

Once a country
builds a healthcare system,
millions of people, hospitals
and insurers adapt around it.

Changing it later
becomes enormously difficult.

<!-- S68 -->
Nobody invented
the correct system.

They simply started
from different places.

<!-- S69 -->
And this brings us back
to the beginning.

Health insurance feels like something
that must have always existed.

It hasn't.

<!-- S70 -->
For most of human history,
there was no health insurance.

There were families.

Communities.

Charities.

Guilds.

Mutual aid societies.

<!-- S71 -->
And people simply paid doctors
when they could.

Modern public health insurance
is largely a product
of the last hundred and fifty years.

<!-- S72 -->
It emerged because
industrial society
created a new problem.

A person could be perfectly able
to work, and one illness
could still destroy
their family's finances.

<!-- S73 -->
Insurance provided a solution.

Instead of asking this.

Can this person afford to be sick?

<!-- S74 -->
Society could ask this.

Can all of us share
the risk of becoming sick?

<!-- S75 -->
And that is what
public health insurance is.

Not free healthcare.

Not government-owned hospitals.

<!-- S76 -->
But a mechanism for turning
an unpredictable individual disaster
into a collective financial risk.

<!-- S77 -->
And once you look at it that way,
the strange thing isn't
that different countries
have different systems.

<!-- S78 -->
The strange thing is
that we ever managed
to create systems at all.

<!-- S79 -->
Because for almost all
of human history...

if you got sick,
you were basically on your own.
