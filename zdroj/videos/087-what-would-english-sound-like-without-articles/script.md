# What Would English Sound Like Without Articles?

<!-- S01 -->
I went to store and bought book.
Book was expensive, but store was crowded.

<!-- S02 -->
If English is your first language,
that probably sounds wrong.
But you understood every word of it.

<!-- S03 -->
All that's missing are three tiny words.

<!-- S03b -->
A.

<!-- S03c -->
An.

<!-- S03d -->
And the.

<!-- S04 -->
The word "the" is the most common word
in the English language.
"A" is in the top five.

<!-- S05 -->
And yet roughly a third
of the world's languages
have no articles at all.
Korean, Hindi, Finnish, Polish, Czech
and many others get by without a single one.

<!-- S06 -->
So why does English bother?
And what would happen
if we simply got rid of them?

<!-- S07 -->
Let's start with something simple.
I saw a dog.

<!-- S07b -->
I saw the dog.

<!-- S08 -->
There's a dog in both sentences.
The difference isn't about the dog.
It's about what I assume you already know.

<!-- S09 -->
"A dog" is new.
We haven't established which one.
"The dog" is one
you should be able to pick out.

<!-- S10 -->
Maybe I mentioned it before.
Maybe we're both looking at it.
Maybe it's the only dog around.

<!-- S11 -->
So articles don't tell you what exists.
They tell you how to find it.

<!-- S12 -->
Now let's take them away.
Yesterday I went to supermarket.
I bought bottle of wine and some bread.
Wine was surprisingly good.

<!-- S13 -->
It sounds strange.
But what did you actually misunderstand?

<!-- S13b -->
Probably nothing.

<!-- S14 -->
You rebuilt the missing pieces automatically.
There was a supermarket. There was a bottle.
And the wine at the end is the wine I bought.

<!-- S15 -->
But notice what you just did.
You worked out what was new
and what was already mentioned.

<!-- S16 -->
That's the real job of articles.
They keep track of what the speaker
and the listener already share.

<!-- S17 -->
Is this new?

<!-- S17b -->
Have we mentioned it?
Is it obvious from the situation?

<!-- S18 -->
English answers all of that
with one compact tool.
But it isn't the only tool.
Not even in English.

<!-- S19 -->
English often skips articles itself.
I like music.
Water is important.
Dogs are intelligent.

<!-- S20 -->
No "a". No "the".
Linguists call this the zero article.

<!-- S21 -->
And sometimes another word
is already doing the job.
I bought my book.
Nobody says "my the book".

<!-- S22 -->
"My" tells you whose book.
"One" tells you how many.
"Some" leaves the amount open.
"This" tells you which.

<!-- S23 -->
So English has several ways
to tell you how to read a noun.
Articles are just one of them.

<!-- S24 -->
And the rules aren't even consistent.
"A dog has four legs."
That's not one new dog. That's dogs in general.

<!-- S25 -->
"The lion is a dangerous animal."
Not a lion you're supposed to recognise.
Lions in general.

<!-- S26 -->
So the same little words can mean
something new, something known,
or a whole kind of thing.

<!-- S27 -->
Which is why articles are so hard to learn.
For speakers of Czech, Chinese or Japanese,
they're often one of the last things
to fall into place.

<!-- S28 -->
Take Czech.

<!-- S28b -->
Here's "Yesterday I saw a dog.
The dog ran across the park."

<!-- S29 -->
Translate it back word for word,
and you get this.
Yesterday I saw dog. Dog ran across park.

<!-- S30 -->
Exactly the broken English we started with.
Yet in Czech, it's perfectly clear.

<!-- S31 -->
Context tells you it's the same dog.
And when context isn't enough,
Czech reaches for the other tools.

<!-- S32 -->
My dog.

<!-- S32b -->
One dog.

<!-- S32c -->
Some dog or other.

<!-- S32d -->
That dog.

<!-- S33 -->
Every one of those jobs still gets done.
There's just no single word
dedicated to it.

<!-- S34 -->
Czech can even use word order.
Put a man at the end of the sentence,
and he's usually news.
Put him at the start,
and he's usually someone we already know.

<!-- S35 -->
So can English survive without articles?
Let's push a little harder.

<!-- S36 -->
I went to supermarket
and saw man talking to woman.
Woman gave man key.
Man opened door with key.

<!-- S37 -->
You can follow it.
But now you're working.

<!-- S38 -->
Which door?

<!-- S38b -->
One we've seen before, or just some door?
Is that the same woman, or another one?

<!-- S39 -->
Now with articles.
I went to the supermarket
and saw a man talking to a woman.

<!-- S40 -->
The woman gave the man a key.
The man opened the door with the key.

<!-- S41 -->
Every "a" brings something new in.
Every "the" tells you
which one I mean.

<!-- S42 -->
Pronouns help. Word order helps.
Context helps.
But articles make the nouns easy to track.

<!-- S43 -->
A tiny amount of information,
placed exactly where you need it.

<!-- S44 -->
So English without articles would work.
It would just make you work harder.

<!-- S45 -->
Which raises a better question.
If articles are this useful,
why doesn't every language have them?

<!-- S46 -->
And where did they come from
in the first place?

<!-- S47 -->
About a thousand years ago,
English didn't use them
the way it does today.

<!-- S48 -->
"The" grew out of a word that meant "that".
"A" grew out of the word for "one".

<!-- S49 -->
In fact, "an" came first.
The "n" fell away before consonants,
and "a" was left.

<!-- S50 -->
And sometimes the "n" wandered off.
An apron used to be "a napron".
An adder used to be "a nadder".

<!-- S51 -->
People heard the "n" as part of the article,
and it jumped across.

<!-- S52 -->
The shift itself makes sense.
"One dog" picks out one member of a group.
Use it often enough,
and it stops counting.
It just introduces something new.

<!-- S53 -->
"That dog" points at something
you can identify.
Use it everywhere,
and it stops pointing.
It just marks something you can identify.

<!-- S54 -->
Linguists call this grammaticalisation.
An ordinary word slowly turns
into a piece of grammar.

<!-- S55 -->
And it has happened more than once.
Latin had no articles.

<!-- S56 -->
But its daughters do.
French, Spanish, Italian.
And they built them from the same material.

<!-- S57 -->
Their "the" came
from the Latin word for "that".
Their "a" came from the Latin word for "one".

<!-- S58 -->
So articles aren't
fundamental particles of language.
They're old words that got a new job.

<!-- S59 -->
And languages don't all put that job
in the same place.

<!-- S60 -->
In Swedish, "a car" is two words.
"The car" is one.
The word for car, with "the" stuck on the end.

<!-- S60b -->
Norwegian does the same,
and then goes one step further.
"My book" is usually "boka mi".
Literally, "the book my".

<!-- S60c -->
Remember "my the book"?
Nobody says that in English.
In Norwegian, it's more or less
the normal way to say it.

<!-- S61 -->
Bulgarian puts "the" on the end too.
And Bulgarian is a Slavic language,
just like Czech.

<!-- S62 -->
Bulgarian grew an article.
Czech didn't.
And neither did most of its Slavic relatives.
Same problem, different solution.

<!-- S63 -->
So neither language is missing anything.
They just keep the information
in different places.

<!-- S64 -->
To an English speaker,
"I went to store and bought book"
feels incomplete.

<!-- S65 -->
But if you speak a language
that doesn't have articles,
you might hear something
completely different.
Maybe it doesn't sound
like broken English at all.
Maybe it sounds like English
without all those annoying
little complications.

<!-- S66 -->
Neither is more logical.
And grammar isn't a list of arbitrary rules.

<!-- S67 -->
It's a set of different answers
to the same question.

<!-- S68 -->
How can one human mind
get another to imagine the same thing?
