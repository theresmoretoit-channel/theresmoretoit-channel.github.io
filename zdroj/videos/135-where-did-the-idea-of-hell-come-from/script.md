<!-- S01 -->
Hell has an address.

<!-- S02 -->
It's a valley on the edge of old Jerusalem,
just below the city walls.

<!-- S03 -->
You can walk through it today.
In summer, it hosts concerts.

<!-- S04 -->
In 1987, Bob Dylan played there.

<!-- S05 -->
In Hebrew, it's Ge Hinnom,
the Valley of Hinnom.

<!-- S06 -->
In the Greek of the New Testament,
that became Gehenna.
In the Arabic of the Quran,
very probably, Jahannam.

<!-- S06b -->
Most English translations
turn both of those words
into one: hell.

<!-- S07 -->
Over about two thousand years,
for billions of people,
the name of that valley
came to mean one thing:
a place of fire for the wicked.

<!-- S08 -->
So how does a valley
you can walk through
become hell?

<!-- S09 -->
Here's the strange part.
In the older books of the Bible,
the people living next to that valley
don't have a hell at all.

<!-- S10 -->
Their scriptures do have a place
for the dead.
It's called Sheol.

<!-- S11 -->
It's dark. It's silent.
And everyone goes there.

<!-- S12 -->
The Bible even shows you.
King Saul, abandoned by God,
has a medium call up
the dead prophet Samuel.

<!-- S13 -->
Samuel is annoyed at being disturbed.
Then he tells the king:
tomorrow, you and your sons
will be with me.

<!-- S14 -->
The prophet,
and the king God rejected.
Same place.

<!-- S15 -->
Nobody is rewarded.
Nobody is punished.

<!-- S16 -->
In ancient Mesopotamia,
how you fared among the dead
had little to do with how you'd behaved.
It depended on how many sons you had,
because sons brought offerings.

<!-- S17 -->
A Sumerian poem spells it out.
One son, and you sit and weep.
Seven sons, and you get a throne
among the junior gods.

<!-- S18 -->
So what happened to bad people
when they died?

<!-- S19 -->
Nothing special.
Being bad wasn't what counted.

<!-- S20 -->
Hell wasn't there from the start.
It had to be built.

<!-- S21 -->
And nobody built it all at once.
You can follow it through five parts,
over thousands of years.

<!-- S22 -->
Whether any of it is true,
no historian can tell you.
How much each culture borrowed
from the last is still debated.
But the parts are there to see.

<!-- S23 -->
Part one is a court.

<!-- S24 -->
Egypt,
about three and a half thousand years ago.

<!-- S25 -->
You die,
and you're brought before the gods.
You swear you haven't committed
any of forty-two sins.

<!-- S26 -->
Then your heart is weighed
against a feather.

<!-- S27 -->
If it fails, a monster called Ammit
is waiting, part crocodile,
part lion, part hippo.
It eats the heart,
and your afterlife is over.

<!-- S28 -->
It's one of the oldest versions
of an idea we now take for granted:
how you live decides what happens next.

<!-- S29 -->
There was just one loophole.

<!-- S30 -->
Another spell spoke directly to your heart,
and begged it not to testify against you.

<!-- S31 -->
You could have it written on your scroll.
A scroll could cost
perhaps half a labourer's yearly pay.

<!-- S32 -->
So one of the first moral courts in history
already came with a paid workaround.

<!-- S33 -->
What it didn't have was forever.
Egyptian texts also describe lakes of fire,
but the fire was there to destroy you.
The worst fate was to stop existing.

<!-- S34 -->
Part two is a sentence.

<!-- S35 -->
Athens, around 380 BC.
Plato has Socrates tell a story.

<!-- S36 -->
After death, a judge inspects the soul,
stripped of every title and honour,
and sends the bad to Tartarus,
labelled curable or incurable.

<!-- S37 -->
The curable are improved by punishment.
The incurable suffer forever,
as a warning to others.

<!-- S38 -->
And Plato gives an example.
Not a legend.
Archelaus, a real king of Macedon,
said to have murdered his way to the throne.

<!-- S39 -->
Keep that in mind.
A real, powerful man,
named in a story about who gets punished.

<!-- S40 -->
Part three is the one that matters most.
It's a motive.

<!-- S41 -->
Remember, those older books
had no hell.
Justice was supposed to be done here,
in this life.

<!-- S42 -->
The Book of Deuteronomy spells out the deal.
Obey, and you're blessed.
Disobey, and the curses come for you.

<!-- S43 -->
The trouble with that deal
is that anyone can check it.

<!-- S44 -->
The Book of Ecclesiastes did.
There are just people, it says,
who get what the wicked deserve,
and wicked people
who get what the just deserve.

<!-- S45 -->
Some people do terrible things,
and die old, rich and comfortable.

<!-- S46 -->
So, more than two thousand years ago,
someone wrote down an answer.

<!-- S47 -->
It's in a text called First Enoch,
which most Bibles don't include.
An angel shows Enoch
the hollow places where the dead wait.

<!-- S48 -->
There's a section for the righteous.

<!-- S49 -->
And there's a section for sinners.
But not all sinners.

<!-- S50 -->
It's for those who die,
and, quote, "judgement has not been
executed on them in their lifetime".

<!-- S51 -->
Listen to that again.

<!-- S52 -->
Not a place for sinners.
A place for sinners
who got away with it.

<!-- S53 -->
Then the deal broke completely.

<!-- S54 -->
In 167 BC, Antiochus the Fourth,
the Greek king ruling over Judea,
moved to crush traditional Jewish worship.

<!-- S55 -->
Now it wasn't just that good people
failed to prosper.
They were being killed
for keeping the law.

<!-- S56 -->
The Second Book of Maccabees
tells of a mother and seven sons,
killed one after another,
for refusing to eat pork.

<!-- S57 -->
One son, dying, tells the king:
you will have no resurrection to life.

<!-- S58 -->
Under the old deal,
none of this made sense.
Either God had failed them,
or the story wasn't over.

<!-- S59 -->
In those same years,
the Book of Daniel was finished.
Its last chapter says:

<!-- S60 -->
"Many of them that sleep
in the dust of the earth shall awake,
some to everlasting life,
and some to shame and everlasting contempt."

<!-- S61 -->
The dead were no longer equal.
And the verdict had no end.

<!-- S62 -->
Part four is the location.

<!-- S63 -->
Back to that valley below Jerusalem.
Why that one?
Because it already had
the worst reputation in the country.

<!-- S64 -->
Jeremiah accuses people
of building a shrine there,
"to burn their sons and their daughters
in the fire".

<!-- S65 -->
Archaeology hasn't confirmed those burnings.
But the reputation didn't need proof.

<!-- S65b -->
And then Jeremiah makes a threat,
about that same spot.
It will be called the Valley of Slaughter,
and the dead will be buried there
until there's no room left.

<!-- S65c -->
The place of the crime
becomes the place of the punishment.

<!-- S66 -->
The very last verse of Isaiah
imagines people going out
to look at the corpses of the rebels:
"their worm shall not die,
neither shall their fire be quenched".

<!-- S67 -->
And First Enoch describes
an accursed valley beside a holy mountain,
"for those who are accursed for ever".

<!-- S68 -->
Fire. Corpses. A curse.
And a real place, just outside town.

<!-- S69 -->
By the time of Jesus,
Gehenna meant more than a valley.
It was the name for the place
where God punishes the wicked.

<!-- S70 -->
In the Gospel of Mark,
he warns of Gehenna,
and quotes that line from Isaiah
about the worm and the fire.

<!-- S71 -->
The word appears twelve times
in the New Testament,
eleven of them in the words of Jesus.

<!-- S72 -->
You may have heard that Gehenna
was the city's rubbish dump,
always burning.

<!-- S73 -->
It's a neat explanation.
But it's first found around 1200 AD,
in a rabbi's commentary,
and no direct evidence for it has turned up.

<!-- S74 -->
The word hell itself
comes from somewhere else entirely.

<!-- S75 -->
It's an old Germanic word,
meaning something like the concealed place.
A world of the dead.
There's no fire in the word.

<!-- S76 -->
Then the King James Bible used it
for Gehenna, for Greek Hades,
for Tartarus,
and about half the time, for Sheol.

<!-- S76b -->
So when Jesus warns of hell
in an English Bible,
the word underneath is usually Gehenna.
The name of a valley.

<!-- S77 -->
Part five is the pictures.

<!-- S78 -->
Picture hell.
You probably see torture, demons,
and the devil in charge.

<!-- S79 -->
But in the Book of Revelation,
the devil isn't running the place.
He's thrown in.

<!-- S80 -->
The oldest detailed Christian tour of hell
we have is another book,
the Apocalypse of Peter,
written between 100 and 150 AD.

<!-- S81 -->
Blasphemers hang by their tongues.
Usurers stand to their knees in filth.
Persecutors burn up to the waist.

<!-- S82 -->
It came very close to being in the Bible.

<!-- S83 -->
One of the oldest lists
of Christian scripture
accepts it alongside Revelation,
while admitting some didn't want it
heard in church.

<!-- S84 -->
In the end, it stayed out of the Bible.
Its pictures lived on.

<!-- S85 -->
They passed into a later tour of hell,
the Apocalypse of Paul,
which Dante probably knew.

<!-- S86 -->
In his Inferno,
Pope Nicholas the Third
is stuck head down in a hole.

<!-- S87 -->
He hears someone coming,
and calls out:
"Are you already standing there, Boniface?"

<!-- S88 -->
The poem is set in 1300.
Boniface the Eighth was alive,
and he was the pope.

<!-- S89 -->
Dante had reserved his place.

<!-- S90 -->
Now look at who these hells
were built for.

<!-- S91 -->
Plato's murderous king.
Enoch's sinners who died unpunished.
The persecutors of the righteous.
Dante's living pope.

<!-- S92 -->
Hell isn't just
a theory about death.
It's also a complaint about life.

<!-- S93 -->
Somebody did something terrible,
and nothing happened to them.
Hell is where the story goes to fix that.

<!-- S94 -->
That doesn't tell you whether hell is real.
Where an idea comes from
can't prove it or disprove it.

<!-- S95 -->
It only tells you
why people needed it.

<!-- S96 -->
But a list like that has a problem.
It doesn't stop at tyrants.

<!-- S97 -->
Look again at the Apocalypse of Peter.
On the same list as the persecutors,
there are women hanging by their hair,
for plaiting it to lure men into adultery.

<!-- S98 -->
A place imagined
for people who got away with murder
also had room for your neighbour.

<!-- S99 -->
And a threat that covers your neighbour
also covers you.

<!-- S100 -->
A little over half of American adults
say they believe in hell.
But the picture keeps shifting.

<!-- S101 -->
In 1999, Pope John Paul the Second
told a public audience:
"Rather than a place,
hell indicates the state of those
who freely and definitively
separate themselves from God."

<!-- S102 -->
Rather than a place.

<!-- S103 -->
It wasn't a change of doctrine.
But after two thousand years,
a pope was describing hell
without an address.

<!-- S104 -->
The valley still has one.

<!-- S105 -->
So where did the idea of hell come from?

<!-- S106 -->
A court in Egypt.
A sentence in Greece.
A motive in Judea.
A valley outside Jerusalem.
And pictures from a book
that didn't make it into the Bible.

<!-- S107 -->
And holding them together,
one old feeling:
somebody got away with it.

<!-- S108 -->
So here's a question for you.
If there's no court after death,
is it up to us
to make sure nobody gets away with it?

<!-- S109 -->
Or is some unfairness
just something we have to live with?

Let me know in the comments.
