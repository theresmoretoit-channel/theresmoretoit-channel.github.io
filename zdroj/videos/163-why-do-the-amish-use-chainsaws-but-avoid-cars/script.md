<!-- S01 -->
Imagine deciding to live without a car.
Without television.
Without computers.
Without a connection
to the public electricity grid.
And imagine your children leaving school
after the eighth grade.

<!-- S02 -->
Sounds like some kind
of dystopian religious cult?
It might seem that way.
But the Amish aren't a cult.

<!-- S03 -->
Here's the surprising part.
The Amish are not actually
against technology.
And they're not simply trying to recreate
life in the eighteenth century.

<!-- S04 -->
In fact,
their relationship with technology
is much more interesting than that.
They ask a different question:
What will this technology
do to our community?

<!-- S05 -->
The Amish are Christian
Anabaptist communities.
Their roots go back
to sixteenth-century Europe.

<!-- S06 -->
They emerged as a distinct
religious movement
in the late seventeenth century,
following a split
associated with Jakob Ammann.
Today there are more than
four hundred and twenty thousand of them,
almost all of them
in the United States and Canada.

<!-- S06b -->
Among themselves,
most Amish speak Pennsylvania German,
often called Pennsylvania Dutch.
Children learn English at school,
and the Bible is read in standard German.

<!-- S07 -->
Each Amish community follows
its own set of rules,
usually unwritten,
known as the Ordnung.
These rules govern everyday life,
including which technologies
are acceptable.

<!-- S08 -->
And because different communities
have different rules,
there is no single universal list
of technologies that all Amish people
use or reject.

<!-- S09 -->
Let's start with something familiar.
Cars.
Why do many Amish communities
prohibit their members
from owning and driving cars?

<!-- S10 -->
It's not because they don't understand
combustion engines.
Amish people can travel by car,
but they often rely on non-Amish drivers.
They can also use buses and trains.

<!-- S11 -->
The restriction is primarily
about owning and driving
their own vehicles.
Why?

<!-- S12 -->
Because a car is more than just
a means of transportation.
It allows you to travel dozens of miles
in a relatively short time.

<!-- S13 -->
You can live far away from your family.
Work somewhere else.
Spend your evenings in another town.
And gradually,
the community becomes less connected.

<!-- S14 -->
So the problem isn't necessarily
that cars are too modern.
It's that cars can fundamentally change
how the community functions.

<!-- S15 -->
Many traditional Amish communities
reject connections
to the public electricity grid.
But that doesn't mean
they never use electricity.

<!-- S16 -->
Some use batteries, generators,
solar panels, pneumatic systems,
and other sources of power.
You can even find Amish workshops
equipped with surprisingly
sophisticated machinery.

<!-- S17 -->
The important distinction is
that electricity itself
isn't necessarily forbidden.
Dependence on the outside infrastructure
can be the real concern.
That's a very different philosophy.

<!-- S18 -->
Again, there is no universal answer.
Some Amish communities
restrict agricultural machinery
and continue to rely heavily on horses.
Others are considerably
more technologically permissive.

<!-- S19 -->
Some use modified tractors,
modern woodworking equipment,
chainsaws, pneumatic tools,
and various industrial machines.

<!-- S20 -->
So this isn't a society
where everything has to be made by hand.
Far from it.

<!-- S20b -->
A chainsaw makes work easier.
A car can change where people go,
how far apart they live,
and how often they see one another.

<!-- S21 -->
Some Amish families once had
telephones at home.
Around 1910, the Lancaster Amish
banned them.
Later, many communities
let the telephone back in.
But not into the kitchen.

<!-- S22 -->
In some communities,
the solution was
a small wooden telephone booth
outside the home.
Sometimes several families shared one.

<!-- S23 -->
The telephone was available
when it was needed.
But it could not interrupt
every family meal.
And it did not make visiting
your neighbours unnecessary.

<!-- S24 -->
That is the difference.
The question was never simply
whether a telephone was useful.
It was what putting one inside the home
would change.

<!-- S24a -->
Today, the same debate is back,
this time with mobile phones.
Some communities allow them,
especially for business.
Others restrict them.
And even where they're accepted,
many try to keep them out of the home.

<!-- S24b -->
The same logic applies to medicine.
Their faith has no rule against doctors,
surgery, modern drugs or vaccines.
When seriously ill,
many Amish go to the hospital.
Still, in some communities,
many children are not vaccinated.

<!-- S24c -->
Many Amish avoid commercial insurance.
Large hospital bills are often covered
through mutual aid within the community.
They prefer to rely on each other
rather than on outside insurance.
In 1965, Congress created
a Social Security exemption
for qualifying religious groups.
Those who qualify can pay nothing in,
but must give up any claim to benefits.

<!-- S24d -->
Otherwise, they follow the law
and pay income, property and sales taxes.
Where the law clashes with their faith,
they seek exceptions.
In 1972, the US Supreme Court ruled
that states could not force Amish children
to stay in school past the eighth grade.
And their faith forbids military service.

<!-- S25 -->
Now let's talk about one of the most
famous aspects of Amish life.
Rumspringa.

<!-- S26 -->
Around the age of sixteen,
young Amish people
generally gain more freedom.
This doesn't mean they suddenly receive
permission to do absolutely anything.

<!-- S27 -->
But they have more opportunities
to experience life
outside their community.
Most of them still live at home.
Some continue living
much as they always have.

<!-- S28 -->
Others experiment with the outside world.
Modern clothes.
Cars.
Movies.
Television.
And sometimes things that would normally
be unacceptable in their community.

<!-- S29 -->
Eventually,
they face an important decision.
Will they be baptized and become
full members of the Amish church?
Or will they leave?

<!-- S30 -->
A large majority decide to stay.
According to the Young Center
for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies,
eighty-five per cent or more
of Amish youth join the church.

<!-- S31 -->
So why do so many of them stay?

<!-- S32 -->
There is no single explanation.
But imagine the difference.
Modern society offers:
Enormous personal freedom.
Thousands of opportunities.
Travel.
Career choices.
Technology.
Individual independence.

<!-- S33 -->
The Amish community offers:
Family.
Strong social relationships.
Clear rules.
A familiar environment.
Mutual assistance.
A powerful sense of identity.

<!-- S34 -->
It's a trade-off.
Freedom for stability.
Possibilities for certainty.
Individualism for community.

<!-- S35 -->
And for some people,
the second side of that equation
is surprisingly attractive.

<!-- S36 -->
It would be very easy to portray the Amish
as happy people who escaped
a corrupted modern civilisation.
But reality is more complicated.

<!-- S37 -->
They have strict rules.
Community pressure can be considerable.
Formal education typically ends
after the eighth grade.
Some communities
are extremely conservative.

<!-- S38 -->
And for those who leave after baptism,
it can mean being shunned
and losing much of their social world.
Their religious system is not something
that can simply be separated
from the rest of their lifestyle.

<!-- S39 -->
This is important.
Imagine taking the Amish way of life
and removing religion.

<!-- S40 -->
You would still have:
Community cooperation.
Restrictions on technology.
Simple living.
Strong family relationships.

<!-- S41 -->
But you would lose the central mechanism
that holds much of the system together.

<!-- S42 -->
So it's not entirely accurate to say:
The Amish simply chose
a simpler lifestyle.
They chose a particular
religious way of life,
and limiting their exposure
to the outside world is part of it.

<!-- S43 -->
The Amish did something
modern society often overlooks.
They keep asking one question:
What will this do to our community?

<!-- S44 -->
They accepted some technologies.
Rejected others.
And modified some
to fit their way of life.

<!-- S45 -->
We don't have to follow
their example literally.
But perhaps we could borrow
one simple idea.

<!-- S46 -->
Not every technology
we are capable of creating
is a technology we actually need.
Technology should serve us.
Not the other way around.

<!-- S47 -->
And if one day a washing machine
needs an internet connection
and a monthly subscription
just to wash your clothes...
Perhaps it's time to establish
our own Ordnung.

<!-- S48 -->
So here's a question for you.
If you could keep one technology
out of your home,
the way some Amish kept the telephone,
which one would it be?
Let me know in the comments.
