# What Would the World Look Like From a Fourth Spatial Dimension?
**Script v0.5**

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you're sitting at home.
The door is locked.
The windows are shut.
And suddenly, someone is standing
in your living room.

<!-- S02 -->
They didn't pick the lock.
They didn't break through the wall.
From where they stand,
nothing strange happened at all.
They just took a route you can't take.

<!-- S03 -->
Because suppose space had
one more direction.
Not time.
Not another universe.
A real direction,
like left, forward, or up.

<!-- S04 -->
It sounds like fantasy.
But the trick behind it
was worked out in 1884,
by a London headmaster.

<!-- S05 -->
His name was Edwin Abbott,
and his book was called Flatland.
Its narrator is a square.
He lives on a flat plane,
and he has no idea that "up" exists.

<!-- S06 -->
In Flatland, a house is a closed outline.
A line drawn around you is a wall.
Inside it, you are sealed off
from everyone.

<!-- S07 -->
Then, one evening, a stranger appears
inside his closed house.
His wife can't understand
how it got in.
It's a sphere, from our world.

<!-- S07b -->
The square can only see the slice
that cuts through his plane.
So the sphere looks like a circle
that shrinks to a dot, vanishes,
and grows back out of nothing.

<!-- S08 -->
And the sphere sees things
no one in Flatland can.
Every house.
Every locked chest.
Even the square's own insides.
All of it lying open to view.

<!-- S09 -->
To prove it,
the sphere touches him in the stomach.
From the inside.
Without crossing a single line.

<!-- S10 -->
Then it lifts the square out of the plane.
He doesn't pass through any wall.
He goes up,
in a direction his world doesn't have.

<!-- S11 -->
Now turn the book around.
We are the flat ones.
A fourth direction would be one
we can't point to, or even picture.
And to anyone who could move in it,
our walls would be like lines on paper.

<!-- S11b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.
It really helps the channel.

<!-- S12 -->
So what could such a being do?
Think of a safe.
Steel on every side.

<!-- S13 -->
To a four-dimensional being,
it would be open,
the way a square drawn on paper
is open to you, from above.
It could see the money inside,
take it out,
and leave the safe locked.

<!-- S14 -->
Liu Cixin takes this idea
about as far as it goes.
In Death's End,
the last book of the trilogy
that begins with The Three-Body Problem,
a spaceship crew drifts into a region
where space has four dimensions.

<!-- S15 -->
Suddenly, they can see inside objects
that were completely sealed off before.

<!-- S16 -->
It's fiction, of course.
But the geometry underneath is real.

<!-- S17 -->
Now picture a four-dimensional object
passing through our world.
We could never see all of it.
Only the slice that's in our space,
the way the square only saw
a slice of the sphere.

<!-- S18 -->
A four-dimensional ball
would appear in your room as a dot.
It would swell into a ball.
Then shrink.
And vanish.

<!-- S19 -->
A four-dimensional cube,
pushed through corner first,
would be stranger still.
A tiny pyramid
that grows,
cuts off its own corners,
and turns into an eight-sided diamond,
before shrinking away again.

<!-- S20 -->
To us, it would look like
a shape-shifting object
appearing out of thin air.
To the object,
it's just moving in a straight line.

<!-- S21 -->
And a fourth dimension
allows one more trick.
Take your left shoe.
However you turn it,
it never becomes a right shoe.

<!-- S22 -->
They're mirror images,
and in three dimensions,
no rotation gets you
from one to the other.

<!-- S23 -->
But think of a paper cutout
of the letter R.
Slide it around the table all you like,
it stays an R.
Lift it off the table,
flip it over,
and it's backwards.

<!-- S24 -->
In 1827, the mathematician
August Möbius noticed
the same thing one level up.
In a space with four dimensions,
an object could be turned
into its own mirror image.

<!-- S25 -->
Lift your left shoe
into the fourth direction,
turn it,
set it back down,
and it's a right shoe.

<!-- S26 -->
H. G. Wells turned that into a story.
In The Plattner Story, from 1896,
a schoolteacher is blown out of our space
by an explosion.

<!-- S27 -->
When he comes back,
his heart beats
on the right side of his body.
And he can only write
from right to left,
with his left hand.

<!-- S28 -->
So far, a fourth dimension
sounds like a superpower.
But there's a catch.

<!-- S29 -->
A fourth direction wouldn't just be
somewhere for us to go.
Everything else could spread into it too.
Including gravity.

<!-- S30 -->
In our world, gravity follows
a simple rule.
Twice as far away,
and it's four times weaker.

<!-- S31 -->
Here's why.
Picture the Sun's pull
spreading out in every direction,
over the surface of a growing sphere.

<!-- S32 -->
Double the distance,
and that surface is four times larger.
The same pull is spread
four times thinner.

<!-- S33 -->
Now give space a fourth direction.
The pull spreads over the surface
of a four-dimensional sphere.
And that surface grows faster.
With the cube of the distance.

<!-- S34 -->
Twice as far,
eight times weaker.
Ten times as far,
a thousand times weaker.

<!-- S35 -->
That sounds like a detail.
It isn't.

<!-- S36 -->
In 1917, the physicist Paul Ehrenfest
asked what this would do to the planets.
Working with Newton's laws,
he found there would be no stable orbits.

<!-- S37 -->
A planet stays in orbit
because two things balance.
Gravity pulls it in.
Its own motion carries it outward.

<!-- S38 -->
In our space,
if a planet drifts a little farther out,
that outward push fades faster
than gravity does.
So the planet is pulled back.
The orbit corrects itself.

<!-- S39 -->
With a fourth dimension,
in that simple picture,
both fade at exactly the same rate.
Nothing corrects anything.

<!-- S40 -->
A little too slow,
and the planet spirals into its star.
A little too fast,
and it drifts away for ever.

<!-- S41 -->
Ehrenfest argued
that atoms would have the same problem,
because the electric force
that holds them together
would spread out the same way.
Later physicists, using quantum mechanics,
came to the same conclusion,
at least with electricity as we know it.

<!-- S42 -->
So a big fourth dimension
wouldn't just give us a new direction.
It could take away the solar system.
And maybe the atoms we're made of.

<!-- S43 -->
Which means this is no longer
a story about magic.
It's a clue.

<!-- S44 -->
Earth has been circling the Sun
for four and a half billion years.
It hasn't spiralled in.
It hasn't drifted off.

<!-- S45 -->
So a large fourth dimension,
one that ordinary matter can move through
and gravity can freely spread into,
is in sharp conflict
with the universe we actually see.

<!-- S46 -->
But that's not the same as saying
there's no fourth dimension at all.
Physicists have found
at least two places it could hide.

<!-- S47 -->
The first idea is a century old.
In 1921, Theodor Kaluza
added a fourth direction of space
to Einstein's equations.

<!-- S48 -->
And in 1926, Oskar Klein suggested
why we don't notice it.
It's curled up into a circle,
far too small to see.

<!-- S49 -->
Think of a garden hose
seen from across a field.
It looks like a line.
Only up close
do you see that it's round.

<!-- S50 -->
The second idea is newer.
What if the extra direction is large,
but we're stuck to our three dimensions,
like ink on a page?

<!-- S51 -->
That answers a question
Flatland never asks.
A direction can exist,
and you still might not be able
to step into it.

<!-- S52 -->
Atoms, light, people,
all of it could be stuck.
And maybe only gravity
leaks out.

<!-- S53 -->
In 1998, three physicists pointed out
that extra dimensions like that
could be as large as a millimetre,
and nobody would have noticed.
Because nobody had measured gravity
at such small distances.

<!-- S54 -->
So people went and measured it.
Today, gravity's simple rule
has been checked at distances
thinner than a human hair.
So far, it still holds.

<!-- S55 -->
At the Large Hadron Collider,
physicists look for energy
that goes missing from a collision,
as if something had slipped
out of our three dimensions.
So far, no convincing evidence
of extra dimensions has emerged.

<!-- S56 -->
So here's where we are.
A big fourth dimension,
the kind you could walk into,
doesn't fit the universe we see.
A small one, or a hidden one,
is still an open question.
And it's one we can test.

<!-- S57 -->
Remember the square.
He spent his whole life
in a world with a missing direction,
and never noticed,
until something came to visit.

<!-- S58 -->
If our world has a missing direction,
nothing has come to visit.
Not that we've noticed.
So the real question isn't only
what the world would look like from there.

<!-- S59 -->
It's why, if it's there,
we still haven't found it.
That's where we're going next.
