<!-- S01 -->
The last B-52 left the factory in 1962.

<!-- S02 -->
In some American families,
three generations have flown in it.
From grandfather to grandson.

<!-- S03 -->
And the US Air Force
plans to keep flying it
into the 2050s.

<!-- S04 -->
That's close to a hundred years
after its first flight.

<!-- S05 -->
Along the way, it has outlived
the bomber that was meant to replace it,
a giant that flew at three times
the speed of sound.

<!-- S06 -->
And it's expected to outlive
the B-2 stealth bomber, too.

<!-- S07 -->
So why does the most powerful air force
in the world
keep flying its oldest bomber?

<!-- S08 -->
Here's the first part of the answer.

<!-- S09 -->
Every one of its would-be replacements
was built to be the best at something.

<!-- S10 -->
Faster.
Higher.
Harder to see on radar.

<!-- S11 -->
The B-52 was built
to carry a lot of weight
a very long way.

<!-- S12 -->
And being the best at something
turns out to have an expiry date.

<!-- S13 -->
Carrying a lot, a long way,
doesn't.

<!-- S14 -->
But that's only half the answer.

<!-- S15 -->
A job that never goes away
is no use
if the plane wears out.

<!-- S16 -->
And the B-52 has had
three completely different jobs.

<!-- S17 -->
To see how it survived all three,
we need to go back
to a hotel room in Ohio.

<!-- S18 -->
October 1948.
Dayton, Ohio.

<!-- S19 -->
A team of Boeing engineers
had come to show the Air Force
their design for a new bomber.

<!-- S20 -->
It had propellers.
And the Air Force
wanted to talk about jets.

<!-- S21 -->
So over one weekend,
in the Hotel Van Cleve,
the engineers redesigned it.

<!-- S22 -->
Swept wings.
Eight jet engines.

<!-- S23 -->
One of them went to a hobby shop,
bought some balsa wood,
and carved a model.

<!-- S24 -->
On Monday,
they handed over
a thirty-three-page proposal

<!-- S25 -->
and a model
you could hold in one hand.

<!-- S26 -->
That model became the B-52.

<!-- S27 -->
It first flew in 1952.
By 1962, Boeing had built
seven hundred and forty-four of them.

<!-- S28 -->
Its first job was simple,
and terrible.

<!-- S29 -->
Fly very high, very far,
into the Soviet Union,
and drop nuclear bombs.

<!-- S30 -->
Altitude was its protection.

<!-- S31 -->
Then, in 1960,
a Soviet missile shot down
an American U-2 spy plane,

<!-- S32 -->
flying far higher
than the B-52 could.

<!-- S33 -->
Flying high
had stopped being safe.

<!-- S34 -->
And that giant,
three times the speed of sound,
was already on the drawing board.
The Valkyrie.

<!-- S35 -->
It was designed to cross
into Soviet airspace
more than twenty kilometres up,
faster than a rifle bullet.

<!-- S36 -->
The perfect answer
to a question
that had just stopped mattering.
Only two were ever built.

<!-- S37 -->
The B-52 did something else.
It went low.

<!-- S38 -->
Crews trained to fly it
a few hundred feet above the ground,
under the enemy's radar.

<!-- S39 -->
Job number two.

<!-- S40 -->
It wasn't designed for that.
The air near the ground is rough,
and a big wing gets shaken for hours.

<!-- S41 -->
On one test flight in 1964,
over the Rocky Mountains,
turbulence tore most of the tail fin
off a B-52.
The crew still landed it.

<!-- S42 -->
And by the 1970s,
even flying low
was getting dangerous.

<!-- S43 -->
So in 1977,
the United States cancelled
the first version of the B-1,
the plane meant to replace the B-52.

<!-- S44 -->
One big reason
was a new weapon.
The cruise missile.

<!-- S45 -->
A cruise missile flies itself,
low and alone,
for more than two thousand kilometres.

<!-- S46 -->
So the bomber no longer had to get
anywhere near its target.

<!-- S47 -->
It just had to carry the missiles
to the edge of the map,
and let go.

<!-- S48 -->
Job number three.

<!-- S49 -->
And for this job,
the B-52 was almost perfect.

<!-- S50 -->
It can carry up to twenty
of those missiles.

<!-- S51 -->
It didn't need to be fast.
It didn't need to be invisible.

<!-- S52 -->
It needed to be big,
and to fly a long way.

<!-- S53 -->
Which is exactly
what those engineers in Dayton
had drawn in 1948.

<!-- S54 -->
Three completely different jobs.

<!-- S55 -->
And all three needed
the same thing from the plane.

<!-- S56 -->
But even the perfect truck
wears out eventually.

<!-- S57 -->
So how is a plane
built in the early 1960s
still flying at all?

<!-- S58 -->
Because a wing wears out
mostly by flying,
not by the calendar.

<!-- S59 -->
Every take-off, every gust,
every landing flexes it.
And the metal keeps count.

<!-- S60 -->
A busy airliner can fly
three or four thousand hours
in a single year.

<!-- S61 -->
A B-52 flies
a few hundred.

<!-- S62 -->
For most of the Cold War,
part of the fleet wasn't flying at all.

<!-- S63 -->
It sat on alert,
at the end of the runway,
fuelled and armed,

<!-- S64 -->
with crews sleeping nearby,
ready to take off within minutes.

<!-- S65 -->
Waiting barely wears out a wing.
Flying does.

<!-- S66 -->
By 2016,
the average B-52 had flown
about eighteen thousand hours.

<!-- S67 -->
Its wings were estimated
to last at least
thirty-two thousand.

<!-- S68 -->
So by the measure that matters,
the average B-52
had used up only about half its wing.

<!-- S69 -->
And when a fire
wrecked one in 2014,
the Air Force didn't order a new one.

<!-- S70 -->
Instead, it went to the Arizona desert,

<!-- S71 -->
picked a B-52 that had been
sitting in the sun for seven years,

<!-- S72 -->
rebuilt it,
and sent it back to work.
Its nickname is Ghost Rider.

<!-- S73 -->
A few years later,
a second one followed.

<!-- S74 -->
It helps that so many were built.
Seven hundred and forty-four.

<!-- S75 -->
Of the B-2, there were
only ever twenty-one.

<!-- S76 -->
And the B-52 is the cheapest bomber
for the Air Force to fly.

<!-- S77 -->
An hour in a B-52
costs less than an hour
in a B-1 or a B-2.

<!-- S78 -->
When the job is carrying missiles
to the edge of the map,
what matters isn't being hard to see.
It's how much you carry, and how far.

<!-- S79 -->
But here's the strange part.

<!-- S80 -->
A lot of what's inside a B-52 today
didn't exist in 1962.

<!-- S81 -->
Radios, navigation,
computers, cockpit screens, weapons.

<!-- S82 -->
All replaced,
some of them more than once.

<!-- S83 -->
A new radar is being fitted,
based on the radar
of a modern fighter jet.

<!-- S84 -->
And next come the engines.

<!-- S85 -->
The ones it flies on now
were designed in the 1950s.

<!-- S86 -->
They're being replaced
by Rolls-Royce engines
from the same family

<!-- S87 -->
that powers Gulfstream business jets.

<!-- S88 -->
Eight old engines out.
Eight new ones in.

<!-- S89 -->
Why not four bigger ones,
like a modern airliner?

<!-- S90 -->
Because that would turn
an engine swap into a redesign.

<!-- S91 -->
Losing one big engine on take-off
would pull the plane hard to one side,
so it would need a new rudder.
It would also need new cockpit controls,
changes to the wing,

<!-- S92 -->
and years of new flight tests.

<!-- S93 -->
Eight smaller engines
avoid most of that.

<!-- S94 -->
The upgraded plane gets a new letter.
The B-52J.

<!-- S95 -->
Its test flights are planned
for the end of the 2020s.

<!-- S96 -->
Meanwhile, the Air Force
is buying a brand-new stealth bomber,
the B-21 Raider.

<!-- S97 -->
And the long-term plan
is a bomber fleet
of just two types.

<!-- S98 -->
Not the B-1.
Not the B-2.

<!-- S99 -->
The newest bomber,
and the oldest.

<!-- S100 -->
One gets in, unseen.

<!-- S101 -->
The other carries a lot,
from far away.

<!-- S102 -->
So why is the B-52 still flying?

<!-- S103 -->
Not because it was the best bomber
ever built.

<!-- S104 -->
It was never the fastest,
the highest,
or the hardest to see.

<!-- S105 -->
Its replacements were built
around a technology.

<!-- S106 -->
The B-52 was built
around a job.

<!-- S107 -->
The technology kept expiring.
The job never did.

<!-- S108 -->
And everything that did go out of date,
the engines, the electronics, the weapons,

<!-- S109 -->
turned out to be the parts
you could swap.

<!-- S110 -->
What's left of the 1948 design
is the part that's hardest to replace.

<!-- S111 -->
A big, strong wing
that carries a lot,
a long way.
