<!-- S01 -->
Some politicians win elections
by promising absurdly simple answers
to absurdly complicated problems.

<!-- S02 -->
And it works.

<!-- S02b -->
Not on a few people.
On enough people to win.

<!-- S03 -->
You might expect that to happen
when the stakes are low.
When a country can afford a gamble.

<!-- S04 -->
That is not when it happens.

<!-- S05 -->
It happens when enough people
stop believing that anyone
already in charge can fix anything.

<!-- S06 -->
Trump.

<!-- S06b -->
The AfD.

<!-- S06c -->
Fico.

<!-- S07 -->
Three names, three countries,
and almost nothing else in common.

<!-- S08 -->
In November 2024,
Donald Trump won the American
popular vote with 49.8 percent,
and 312 electoral votes.

<!-- S09 -->
In February 2025,
the AfD came second in Germany
with 20.8 percent.
In 2021 it had 10.4.

<!-- S10 -->
It doubled.

<!-- S11 -->
And in Slovakia, Robert Fico
became prime minister
for the fourth time.

<!-- S12 -->
Now here is the part
that makes the easy explanation
fall apart.

<!-- S13 -->
Fico's party is called
Direction — Social Democracy.

<!-- S14 -->
It sat inside the family
of European socialist parties.

<!-- S15 -->
Not on the right.
On the left.

<!-- S16 -->
The AfD is radical right.
Trump is Republican.
Fico ran as a social democrat.

<!-- S17 -->
Their policies disagree.
Their voters disagree.
On most questions
they would disagree with each other.

<!-- S18 -->
And the machinery underneath them
is nearly identical.

<!-- S18b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S19 -->
So what is the machinery?

<!-- S20 -->
Political scientists have a name
for the thing all three are doing,
and it is narrower
than the way we use the word.

<!-- S21 -->
Populism, in the research sense,
is not a policy programme.

<!-- S22 -->
It is one claim about who you are.

<!-- S23 -->
Society splits into two groups.
The ordinary people.
And an elite that has taken the system
and now runs it for itself.

<!-- S24 -->
That is it.
That is the whole idea.

<!-- S25 -->
Which is why it can sit
inside almost any politics.

<!-- S26 -->
A left-wing populist and
a right-wing populist
will name a different elite.

<!-- S27 -->
But they build the same sentence.

<!-- S28 -->
And the sentence goes like this.

<!-- S29 -->
You have a problem.
The people in charge caused it.
I am the one who will end it.

<!-- S30 -->
One problem.

<!-- S30b -->
One villain.

<!-- S30c -->
One solution.

<!-- S31 -->
Now, you would think
this only works on invented problems.

<!-- S32 -->
It is the opposite.

<!-- S33 -->
The story is at its strongest
when the problem is completely real.

<!-- S34 -->
Housing that nobody young can afford.
Healthcare that costs more every year.
Wages that stopped keeping up.

<!-- S35 -->
Infrastructure that is quietly rotting.
A border that is not under control.
An economy that no longer feels
the way it did.

<!-- S36 -->
None of that has to be exaggerated.
Much of it can be measured.

<!-- S37 -->
The populist doesn't need to invent it.
The populist only needs to explain it.

<!-- S38 -->
And the explanation is always
one word long.

<!-- S39 -->
Immigrants...
Brussels...
Washington...
Corporations...
Billionaires...
The establishment.

<!-- S40 -->
The word changes by country.
The shape never does.

<!-- S41 -->
This is where it gets uncomfortable,
because there is a measurement for it.

<!-- S42 -->
In 2017, four economists
took 220 European regions
and followed them
through the financial crisis.

<!-- S43 -->
They wanted to know
what actually moved the votes.

<!-- S44 -->
Where unemployment rose,
votes for anti-establishment
and populist parties rose with it.

<!-- S45 -->
That part is not surprising.

<!-- S46 -->
This is.

<!-- S47 -->
The same regions also lost trust
in their national parliaments
and in European institutions.

<!-- S48 -->
But attitudes towards immigrants
hardly moved at all.

<!-- S49 -->
On culture, they did not harden.
If anything, Europeans
came out of the crisis
slightly more open.

<!-- S50 -->
Read that again.

<!-- S51 -->
Across those regions,
the thing that tracked
the populist vote
was not how people felt
about immigrants.

<!-- S52 -->
It was how they felt
about their own institutions.

<!-- S53 -->
The argument everybody was having
and the thing actually moving the votes
were two different objects.

<!-- S53b -->
That is one study,
one continent, one crisis.
It is not the last word.

<!-- S53c -->
But it is a measurement,
and it points somewhere specific.

<!-- S54 -->
That brings us to the part
that established parties
do not enjoy discussing.

<!-- S55 -->
A populist needs votes.
Those votes are currently
sitting somewhere else.

<!-- S56 -->
So somebody has to give them a reason
to leave the party they already know.

<!-- S57 -->
And that reason can be four words.

<!-- S58 -->
You had your chance.

<!-- S59 -->
Look at what happened
to American trust in government.

<!-- S60 -->
In 1958, 73 percent of Americans
said they trusted the government
in Washington to do what is right.

<!-- S61 -->
By September 2025, it was 17.

<!-- S62 -->
Two percent said just about always.
Fifteen said most of the time.

<!-- S63 -->
That is not a bad year.
That is nearly seventy years
of something draining away.

<!-- S64 -->
And once it drains,
something strange becomes possible.

<!-- S65 -->
A candidate who would have been dismissed
as too extreme, too strange,
too obviously unqualified,
becomes worth a try.

<!-- S66 -->
Not because people love the candidate.

<!-- S67 -->
Because of a different sentence.

<!-- S68 -->
What do we have to lose?

<!-- S69 -->
That is one of the most powerful
sentences in politics,
and it is not an argument for anyone.

<!-- S70 -->
It is an argument against everyone else.

<!-- S71 -->
Which is the whole trick,
and almost nobody says it out loud.

<!-- S72 -->
A populist does not have to convince you
that the simple solution will work.

<!-- S73 -->
That would be hard.
Solutions can be checked.

<!-- S74 -->
The populist only has to convince you
that the current one has already failed.

<!-- S75 -->
And that job is much easier,
because quite often
it is simply true.

<!-- S76 -->
There is a second advantage,
and it is structural.

<!-- S77 -->
Imagine two politicians
explaining why housing is expensive.

<!-- S78 -->
The first one says:
zoning rules, construction rates,
interest rates, demographic change,
local opposition, decades of shortfall.

<!-- S79 -->
The second one says:
they made housing unaffordable,
and I will fix it.

<!-- S80 -->
Which sentence is still in your head?

<!-- S81 -->
A complicated explanation needs context.
A slogan needs nothing at all.

<!-- S82 -->
Thirty seconds of video
will carry problem, villain and solution
with room to spare.

<!-- S83 -->
It will not carry a supply curve.

<!-- S84 -->
And here is the trap
inside that advantage.

<!-- S85 -->
A country can have
an immigration problem,
and a housing problem,
and an ageing population,
and weak growth, all at once.

<!-- S86 -->
Those things push on each other.
Fixing one can worsen another.

<!-- S87 -->
There may be no single villain.
There may be no single fix.

<!-- S88 -->
Reality doesn't fit neatly
into a campaign slogan.

<!-- S89 -->
But a slogan fits neatly
into a person's memory,
and that is the competition
we actually hold.

<!-- S90 -->
So why does this keep coming back?

<!-- S91 -->
Because the conditions do.

<!-- S92 -->
Problems appear.
Parties promise.
Repairs take longer than terms.
Some work. Some don't.
New problems arrive.

<!-- S93 -->
And eventually enough people
are tired enough to gamble.

<!-- S94 -->
And by then, politics
has quietly changed subject.

<!-- S95 -->
It stops being about policy,
which can be argued with.

<!-- S96 -->
It becomes about trust,
which cannot.

<!-- S97 -->
And trust has a number too.

<!-- S98 -->
In May 2024, 35 percent of Democrats
trusted the federal government.
Eleven percent of Republicans did.

<!-- S99 -->
By September 2025, it was
9 percent of Democrats
and 26 percent of Republicans.

<!-- S100 -->
Sixteen months,
and the two lines crossed
straight over each other.

<!-- S101 -->
The occupant changed.

<!-- S102 -->
So that number was never
measuring performance.
It was measuring identity.

<!-- S103 -->
And that is why facts, on their own,
do not repair any of this.

<!-- S104 -->
If the other side is not merely wrong
but corrupt, then meeting them halfway
is not compromise.

<!-- S105 -->
It is surrender.

<!-- S106 -->
So is populism the disease?

<!-- S107 -->
Maybe not.

<!-- S108 -->
A political system that people trust
can absorb enormous disagreement
without breaking.

<!-- S109 -->
When that trust goes,
every failure becomes evidence.
Every compromise looks like weakness.

<!-- S110 -->
Every insider becomes a suspect,
and every outsider becomes a candidate.

<!-- S111 -->
Which leaves one uncomfortable thought.

<!-- S112 -->
Populists usually do not manufacture
the frustration that carries them in.

<!-- S113 -->
They arrive after it.

<!-- S114 -->
Sometimes the system
produced it by itself,
over years, in public,
while everyone watched.

<!-- S115 -->
So telling people not to vote
for a particular person
was never going to be enough.

<!-- S116 -->
If the reason is still there
when he goes,
somebody else simply takes the slot.

<!-- S117 -->
And perhaps the honest question
for any democracy is not
why people voted for the populist.

<!-- S118 -->
It is what happened before they did.
