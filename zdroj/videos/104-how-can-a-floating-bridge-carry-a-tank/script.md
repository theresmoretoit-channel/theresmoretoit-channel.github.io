<!-- S01 -->
A main battle tank
weighs more than sixty tonnes.

And here it is, crossing a river
on a bridge made of floating metal boxes.

<!-- S02 -->
No pillars.
No foundations in the riverbed.
Just sections resting on the water,
locked together, with a road on top.

<!-- S03 -->
It looks like something
that should work perfectly well...
right up until the tank arrives.

<!-- S04 -->
So how does floating metal
carry sixty tonnes?

<!-- S05 -->
The tank adds sixty tonnes of weight.
So the bridge must displace
sixty tonnes of extra water.

<!-- S06 -->
In a river, that's sixty cubic metres.
A block of water ten metres long,
six metres wide,
and one metre deep.

<!-- S07 -->
That sounds like a lot.
But a bridge is long.

<!-- S08 -->
And that block isn't pushed aside
by one box.
It's shared between all the sections
the tank's weight reaches.

<!-- S09 -->
The tank drives on.
The pontoons settle lower.
They push aside more water,
and the water pushes back harder.

<!-- S10 -->
No pumps. No computer.
The bridge finds its new balance
all on its own.

<!-- S11 -->
It's the same rule
that keeps a steel ship afloat,
and Archimedes wrote it down
more than two thousand years ago.

<!-- S12 -->
Near Seattle, a motorway crosses
Lake Washington on seventy-seven
concrete pontoons,
more than two kilometres long.

Same physics.
But this bridge is designed
to last for decades.

<!-- S13 -->
So floating is the easy part.

<!-- S14 -->
Here's the hard part.
The British Army says eight
of its amphibious bridging vehicles
can build a bridge
a hundred metres long
in about thirty minutes.

<!-- S15 -->
If an army can build a bridge
in thirty minutes...
why can't the enemy
simply destroy it?

<!-- S16 -->
We'll get there.
But before the enemy gets a say,
the bridge has to survive
the tank itself.

<!-- S17 -->
Put a heavy truck on a narrow boat,
and the boat may float just fine.
It just floats on its side.

<!-- S18 -->
A bridge section is wide and flat.
When one edge dips,
that edge pushes aside more water,
and the extra lift pushes it back up.

<!-- S19 -->
And the sections are locked together.
The tank isn't standing on one box.
It's standing on a long strip
that shares its weight
along the bridge.

That's why one common design
is called a ribbon bridge.

<!-- S20 -->
But a ribbon bends.
The joints flex,
the deck dips under every axle,
and the whole thing rides the waves.

Engineers don't try to stop that.
They keep the movement within limits.

<!-- S21 -->
And here's a catch.
A tank and a truck
can weigh exactly the same,
and still not be equally safe to cross.

<!-- S22 -->
A tank spreads its weight
along two long tracks.
A truck puts all of it
onto a few axles.

<!-- S23 -->
Military bridges also use a system
called Military Load Classification.
Because a vehicle's weight alone
doesn't tell you how it loads a bridge.

<!-- S24 -->
And then there's the river.
The current pushes on every pontoon,
so the bridge has to be held in place,
with anchors, cables,
or small boats pushing upstream.

<!-- S25 -->
A floating bridge can have
more than enough buoyancy...
and still fail,
because the river pushed it sideways.

<!-- S26 -->
Which brings us back to the question.
Why would an army rely
on something this delicate?

<!-- S27 -->
Because a river stops an army
better than almost anything else.
And a retreating defender
will often blow up the bridges
behind it.

<!-- S28 -->
So the attacker brings their own.

<!-- S29 -->
Some bridges travel folded on trucks.
Each section slides into the water
and unfolds on its own.

<!-- S30 -->
Others are vehicles
that become the bridge.
They drive into the river,
swing out their floats,
and lock onto the next one.

<!-- S31 -->
In 2016, British and German crews
linked thirty of them into a bridge
three hundred and fifty metres long
across the Vistula, in Poland.

<!-- S32 -->
But thirty minutes is the assembly.
Not the operation.

<!-- S33 -->
The equipment has to reach the river.
The banks need a way on and off.
And everything that wants to cross
has to wait its turn.

<!-- S34 -->
That waiting
is the real weakness.

<!-- S35 -->
In May 2022, Russian forces
tried to cross the Siverskyi Donets river
near the village of Bilohorivka,
in eastern Ukraine.

<!-- S36 -->
The crossing site was not a surprise.
Ukrainian commanders had expected
an attempt in this area.

<!-- S37 -->
Russian forces tried to hide
their preparations
with smoke and burning trees.
But Ukrainian reconnaissance
confirmed the bridge was going up.

<!-- S38 -->
Artillery opened fire.
Airstrikes followed.

<!-- S39 -->
Images later showed
three destroyed pontoon bridges
and dozens of wrecked military vehicles.

<!-- S40 -->
British defence intelligence assessed
that Russia had lost significant
armoured manoeuvre elements
of at least one battalion tactical group.

<!-- S41 -->
Notice what failed.
Not the buoyancy.
The pontoons could float.
But that wasn't enough
to make the crossing succeed.

<!-- S42 -->
The crossing couldn't be protected.
And a bridge tells the enemy
exactly where you're going to be.
Every vehicle has to pass
through one narrow point...
and wait its turn to do it.

<!-- S43 -->
A pontoon bridge isn't just a road.
It's a target
with a queue in front of it.

<!-- S44 -->
So yes, the enemy can destroy it.
And often does.

<!-- S45 -->
But destroying a bridge once
isn't the same as stopping the crossing.

<!-- S46 -->
A damaged section
can sometimes be swapped out.
Engineers can repair the crossing,
or build a new one somewhere else.
In some systems, the same modules
can be reconfigured as rafts,
ferrying vehicles across one at a time.

<!-- S47 -->
So the crossing becomes a race.
One side tries to cut the flow
of fuel, ammunition and reinforcements.
The other tries to restore it faster.

<!-- S48 -->
Engineers may use darkness and smoke,
or set up alternative crossing sites.
But none of that guarantees safety.

<!-- S49 -->
Speed is one of its few protections.
But only until the enemy finds it.

<!-- S50 -->
So floating was never the hard part.
The hard part is staying alive
long enough to matter.

<!-- S51 -->
And that race keeps getting faster.
If the enemy can find a new bridge
within minutes,
half an hour to build one
starts to look slow.

<!-- S52 -->
The bridge floats.
The tank rolls.
And for a brief moment,
a river becomes a road.
