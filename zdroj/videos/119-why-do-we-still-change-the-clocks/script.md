<!-- S01 -->
Twice a year,
most of America moves its clocks
by one hour.

<!-- S02 -->
In March, we lose an hour of sleep.
In November, the hour comes back.
And every time, the same argument starts.

<!-- S03 -->
It's bad for our health.

<!-- S03b -->
It's pointless.
Just pick one time and keep it.

<!-- S04 -->
Politicians seem to agree.
In 2022, the United States Senate
passed a bill to stop changing the clocks.
Without a single objection.

<!-- S05 -->
It went nowhere.
Four years later, the House passed
another bill,
to make daylight saving time permanent.
It's now stuck in the Senate.
And the clocks are still changing.

<!-- S06 -->
So if changing the clocks
is such a terrible idea,
why is it so difficult to stop?

<!-- S07 -->
America has actually tried.
In 1974, it stopped changing the clocks.
The experiment didn't last a year.

<!-- S08 -->
To see why,
we need to start with the problem
the clocks were trying to solve.

<!-- S09 -->
The problem is simple.
Daylight changes through the year.
Our schedules don't.

<!-- S10 -->
School starts at eight.
Work starts at nine.
In June and in December alike.

<!-- S11 -->
But the Sun doesn't follow a timetable.
In Seattle, the shortest day of the year
has about eight and a half hours of light.
The longest has sixteen.

<!-- S12 -->
In Miami, the difference is much smaller.
Ten and a half hours in winter,
not quite fourteen in summer.

<!-- S13 -->
The farther north you live,
the more the day stretches and shrinks
around a schedule that never moves.

<!-- S14 -->
In summer, that means something odd.
On standard time, the Sun in Boston
rises just after four in the morning.
Hours of daylight,
while almost everyone is asleep.

<!-- S15 -->
Daylight saving time is a trick
for moving some of that light
from the early morning to the evening.
Not by changing the Sun.
By changing what we call the hour.

<!-- S15b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S16 -->
And as a national law,
it started as a war measure.

<!-- S17 -->
On 30 April 1916,
Germany moved its clocks forward
to save coal for the war.
Britain followed three weeks later.

<!-- S18 -->
The United States joined in 1918,
and gave it up a year later.
It was so unpopular with farmers
that Congress repealed it
over the President's veto.

<!-- S19 -->
In the Second World War it came back,
all year round,
under the name "War Time".

<!-- S20 -->
After the war, every state and city
did more or less what it wanted.
In the 1960s, one bus ride,
just thirty-five miles
from Ohio into West Virginia,
went through seven changes of time.

<!-- S21 -->
In 1966, Congress finally set
one set of rules for the whole country.
States could opt out entirely,
but they couldn't pick their own dates.

<!-- S22 -->
And so a wartime measure
quietly became part of normal life.

<!-- S23 -->
The cost is real, though.
Our bodies run on an internal clock,
and that clock is set mostly by light.

<!-- S24 -->
When the clocks jump forward in March,
the alarm rings an hour earlier,
as far as our body is concerned.
For days, many people sleep worse.

<!-- S25 -->
Some studies have found a brief rise
in car crashes and heart attacks
right after that change.

<!-- S26 -->
So the obvious answer is:
stop changing the clocks.
Pick one time, and keep it all year.

<!-- S27 -->
Fine.

<!-- S27b -->
But which one?

<!-- S28 -->
Permanent standard time
keeps winter mornings as they are.
But every summer evening
loses an hour of light,
and in the north, the Sun rises
around four in the morning.

<!-- S29 -->
Permanent daylight saving time
keeps the long summer evenings.
But in winter, the mornings get dark.
Really dark.

<!-- S30 -->
In Indianapolis, the Sun would rise
after nine in early January.
In Detroit and Seattle, around nine.

<!-- S31 -->
And America knows what that feels like,
because it tried.

<!-- S32 -->
In January 1974,
in the middle of an oil crisis,
the United States switched to
daylight saving time all year round,
to save energy.

<!-- S33 -->
When the law was signed,
about four in five Americans supported it.
Then came the dark January mornings,
and children walking to school
before sunrise.

<!-- S34 -->
Within weeks, support fell
to around two in five.

<!-- S35 -->
By October, Congress voted
to bring back standard time for the winter.
It had been planned for two years.
Year-round daylight saving time
lasted ten months.

<!-- S36 -->
And here's the strange part.
Today, the law lets any state choose
permanent standard time.
Arizona and Hawaii already have it.

<!-- S37 -->
But nineteen states have passed laws
for the other one,
permanent daylight saving time.
And that, they can't have
without Congress.

<!-- S38 -->
So people agree that they hate the switch.
They don't agree on what to switch to.

<!-- S39 -->
And it's not just an American problem.
In 2011, Russia stopped changing its clocks
and kept summer time all year.

<!-- S40 -->
In Moscow, the winter Sun
now rose at around ten in the morning.
Three years later,
Russia went the other way,
to permanent winter time.

<!-- S41 -->
In 2018, the European Union
asked its citizens what they thought.
More than four and a half million answered,
and eighty-four per cent of them
wanted the changes to end.

<!-- S42 -->
It wasn't a representative poll.
Most of the answers came from Germany.
The result showed how strongly
the people who took part felt about it.

<!-- S43 -->
The following spring,
the European Parliament voted to do it.
All that was left was for the countries
to agree which time each would keep.

<!-- S44 -->
And that's where it stopped.
Governments worried that neighbours
would choose differently,
and Europe would end up
as a patchwork of time zones.

<!-- S45 -->
That was in 2019.
Europe is still changing its clocks.

<!-- S46 -->
So maybe we're asking the wrong question.
Maybe the clock isn't the thing
we should be moving.

<!-- S47 -->
Look at Spain.
Madrid lies west of London.
By the Sun, it belongs
in London's time zone.

<!-- S48 -->
But in 1940, Spain moved its clocks
to Central European Time,
the time of Paris and Berlin.
And it never moved them back.

<!-- S49 -->
So in summer, the Sun over Madrid
is highest at around a quarter past two,
and it sets close to ten in the evening.

<!-- S50 -->
But Spanish life stayed with the Sun.
Lunch at two.
Dinner at nine, or later.
By the clock, that's late.
By the Sun, it isn't.

<!-- S51 -->
Spain is a reminder that the clock
and the schedule are two different things.
You can move the day
without touching the clock.

<!-- S52 -->
And in small ways, we already do.
Summer hours at the office.
Flexible start times.
Shift work.

<!-- S53 -->
So why not do that everywhere?
Start work at seven in summer,
and at nine in winter,
and never touch the clocks again?

<!-- S54 -->
Because a society is not one person
adjusting their alarm clock.

<!-- S55 -->
If your office starts earlier,
your children's school has to as well.
And their bus.
And the daycare.
And the shop you stop at on the way.

<!-- S56 -->
And the train timetable.
And the company in another state
you have a call with every morning.

<!-- S57 -->
Every one of those
would have to be moved separately,
by someone,
and agreed with everyone else.

<!-- S58 -->
Changing the clocks does all of that
in a single step.
At two in the morning on a Sunday,
everyone following the same rules
changes together.

<!-- S59 -->
It's clumsy.
But it's brutally simple.

<!-- S60 -->
Well... simple to coordinate.
Anyone who has written software
that deals with time
knows how much trouble one hour can cause.

<!-- S61 -->
In November, the hour from one to two
happens twice.
In March, the hour from two to three
doesn't happen at all.

<!-- S62 -->
Calendars, recurring meetings,
alarms, payroll, night shifts...
all of them have to know about it.

<!-- S63 -->
And the rules keep changing.
The database that computers use
to keep track of time zones
is updated again and again,
whenever some government, somewhere,
changes its clocks.

<!-- S64 -->
So why do we still change the clocks?

<!-- S65 -->
Not because anyone thinks it's perfect.
Because daylight changes,
and our schedules don't.

<!-- S66 -->
We could change our schedules
instead of changing our clocks.
And sometimes, we already do.

<!-- S67 -->
But a society is millions of people,
businesses, schools, trains and computers,
trying to agree on when the day begins.

<!-- S68 -->
Daylight changes.
Our schedules don't change nearly as easily.

<!-- S69 -->
Maybe daylight saving time
isn't a perfect solution.
Maybe there isn't one.

<!-- S69b -->
But of all the imperfect ones,
switching twice a year
might just be the least bad.

<!-- S70 -->
The strange thing isn't
that we change the clocks.
It's that moving one hour on a clock
can be easier than changing
the daily schedule of an entire society.
