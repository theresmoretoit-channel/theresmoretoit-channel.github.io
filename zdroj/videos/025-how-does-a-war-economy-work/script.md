<!-- S01 -->
Imagine a country at peace.
Factories make cars and refrigerators.
Farmers grow food.
Companies build houses.
People go to work, earn money,
and spend it on things they want.

<!-- S02 -->
And then a war begins.

<!-- S03 -->
Suddenly the government needs
millions of uniforms.
Trucks.
Artillery shells.
Aircraft, ships and tanks.

<!-- S04 -->
But there is a problem.
Where do all these things come from?

<!-- S05 -->
You cannot simply print
a trillion dollars
and magically create
a trillion dollars' worth of tanks.

<!-- S06 -->
A war economy is, fundamentally,
a giant redistribution
of a country's resources.
And that creates some deeply
counterintuitive problems.

<!-- S07 -->
In a normal economy,
production is driven
by what civilians want.
People want houses,
so companies build houses.

<!-- S08 -->
During a major war,
the government becomes
one of the largest customers
in the entire economy.

<!-- S09 -->
Instead of asking
what consumers want,
the economy increasingly asks
what the war effort needs.

<!-- S10 -->
A factory that used to build cars
might start building military trucks.
A steel mill that supplied
construction companies
might be supplying armour plate.

<!-- S11 -->
The machinery hasn't necessarily changed.
The destination of the resources has.

<!-- S12 -->
But where do the resources come from?

<!-- S13 -->
A country has only so much steel.
So much copper, oil and electricity.
So much factory capacity.
So many skilled workers.
And so much time.

<!-- S14 -->
If the military suddenly needs
much more of all of that,
somebody else has to get less.

<!-- S15 -->
Suppose the government spends
billions on weapons.
The money itself isn't the weapon.

<!-- S16 -->
The real question is
what happens to the steel,
the workers and the machines
that build those weapons.

<!-- S17 -->
If those resources were making
civilian goods before,
then civilian output has to shrink...
unless the country can expand
what it is able to make in total.

<!-- S18 -->
So a war economy
is not about spending more money.
It is about moving real resources
from one part of the economy
to another.

<!-- S19 -->
And then there is labour.

<!-- S20 -->
A modern war can require
millions of soldiers.
But soldiers don't make cars,
build houses, or run factories.

<!-- S21 -->
So the economy faces
a rather strange problem.
The war needs workers.
And the war also takes workers away.

<!-- S22 -->
Governments respond in various ways.
Women enter industries
that were dominated by men.
Older workers stay employed.
Working hours go up.

<!-- S23 -->
Britain is a particularly
interesting example.

<!-- S24 -->
When the Second World War ended
in nineteen forty-five,
Britain had won.

<!-- S25 -->
But victory did not mean
the country was suddenly rich again.
Quite the opposite.

<!-- S26 -->
Years of total war
had consumed enormous resources.
Britain had accumulated large debts,
and its industry was still
committed to the war effort.

<!-- S27 -->
The war was over.
But rationing continued.

<!-- S28 -->
People still needed ration books
for many basic goods.
And not just for a few months.

<!-- S29 -->
Rationing in Britain
finally ended in nineteen fifty-four.
Almost nine years after the war it fought.

<!-- S30 -->
Which illustrates
an important principle.
A country can win a war
and still come out of it
economically exhausted.

<!-- S31 -->
Military victory
and economic prosperity
are not the same thing.

<!-- S32 -->
Now we get to
the most confusing part.

<!-- S33 -->
Imagine a factory builds a tank.
That production counts towards
gross domestic product.

<!-- S34 -->
The tank is sent to the battlefield.
It gets destroyed.

<!-- S35 -->
The government orders another tank.
More production is recorded.

<!-- S36 -->
So production is happening.
But has society become richer?

<!-- S37 -->
Gross domestic product
counts the second tank.
It never subtracts the first one.

<!-- S38 -->
The measure isn't useless.
It measures economic production.
But production is not the same thing
as wealth, or as living standards.

<!-- S39 -->
And that gap
can grow surprisingly wide.

<!-- S40 -->
A war can create something
that looks exactly like a boom.
Factories run at full capacity.
Unemployment falls.
Industrial output explodes.

<!-- S41 -->
So someone looks at all that
and says:
war stimulates the economy.

<!-- S42 -->
But there is a question hiding
underneath that sentence.
Stimulates it to do what?

<!-- S43 -->
The economy has been mobilised.
It has not discovered
a magical way
to create unlimited resources.

<!-- S44 -->
So how does a government
actually pay for all of this?

<!-- S45 -->
Taxes.
Borrowing.
War bonds.
And, in some circumstances,
creating additional money.

<!-- S46 -->
But there is no free lunch.
If a government tries to use
more resources
than the economy can physically provide,
something has to give.

<!-- S47 -->
Imagine there are a hundred units
of civilian goods available.
But people suddenly have
much more money in their pockets.

<!-- S48 -->
The factories have been redirected,
so there are still
only a hundred units.

<!-- S49 -->
More money chasing
the same amount of goods
means prices tend to rise.

<!-- S50 -->
Which is why war economies
so often end up rationing things.

<!-- S51 -->
Rationing is basically
the government saying:
you have money,
but you cannot buy
as much as you want.

<!-- S52 -->
Why?

<!-- S52b -->
Because money
is not the scarce resource.
The physical goods are.

<!-- S53 -->
There may be enough money
for everyone to buy petrol.
There simply isn't enough petrol.

<!-- S54 -->
So instead of letting the price
rise without limit,
the government limits
how much each person can buy.

<!-- S55 -->
And whenever legal supply is restricted
while demand stays high,
something else tends to appear.
The black market.

<!-- S56 -->
One of the clearest examples
of wartime mobilisation
was the United States
during the Second World War.

<!-- S57 -->
Factories turned out aircraft, ships,
trucks, tanks, engines and ammunition.
Entire production lines
were reorganised.

<!-- S58 -->
The important point is that
the United States did not simply
print money and become
more productive.

<!-- S59 -->
It mobilised what it already had.
People. Factories. Raw materials.
Energy. Technology. Logistics.
And it built more capacity as well.

<!-- S60 -->
And then comes another problem.
The war is over.

<!-- S61 -->
Millions of soldiers come home.
Factories that built weapons
need something new to build.
Military spending falls.

<!-- S62 -->
People want houses, cars,
refrigerators and clothes.
But the economy cannot
switch back overnight.

<!-- S63 -->
A wartime economy
has enormous inertia.
Factories have specialised equipment.
Workers have specialised skills.
Governments have accumulated debt.

<!-- S64 -->
Getting from war back to peace
can be almost as difficult
as getting from peace to war.

<!-- S65 -->
So what is the lesson
underneath all of it?

<!-- S66 -->
Every ton of steel
that goes into a tank
cannot also build a railway.

<!-- S67 -->
Every worker
in an ammunition factory
cannot also build a house.

<!-- S68 -->
War does not remove scarcity.
It changes
where the scarce things go.

<!-- S69 -->
Which is the paradox.
A war economy can be
extremely efficient at one thing.
Turning a country's resources
into military power.

<!-- S70 -->
But that does not
automatically make it richer.
A successful war economy
can consume
wealth that took decades to build.

<!-- S71 -->
Which is why
two sentences can both be true
at the same time.
The economy is booming.
And people are getting poorer.

<!-- S72 -->
There is no contradiction.
The first one is about production.
The second is about
what anybody actually gets to use.

<!-- S73 -->
Because in the end,
war is not a machine
for creating resources.
It is a machine for consuming them.
