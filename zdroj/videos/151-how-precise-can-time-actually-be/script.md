<!-- S01 -->
In a laboratory in Boulder, Colorado,
there is a clock whose frequency
is known so precisely
that even over
the entire age of the universe,
the uncertainty would add up
to less than a second.

<!-- S02 -->
And it isn't the end of the road.
Every generation of clocks
has been better than the last.

<!-- S03 -->
So here's the obvious question.
How far can this go?

<!-- S04 -->
Is there a point where the problem
isn't the clock any more,
but time itself?

<!-- S05 -->
In classical physics,
time was treated as perfectly sharp.
Only our clocks were clumsy.

<!-- S06 -->
But in 2025, a group of physicists
in Rome and Budapest
published a calculation
that puts a crack in that assumption.

<!-- S07 -->
If one particular idea
about quantum mechanics is right,
then every clock in the universe
picks up a tiny, random uncertainty.

<!-- S08 -->
Not because it's badly built.
Because, in these models,
time itself would jitter.

<!-- S09 -->
And there's a twist
nobody would have guessed.

<!-- S10 -->
The clocks that suffer most
aren't the cheap ones.
They're the smallest ones.

<!-- S11 -->
To see why,
we need three pieces.
How a clock works.
What gravity does to time.
And a very old problem
at the heart of quantum mechanics.

<!-- S12 -->
A clock doesn't measure time directly.
It counts something that repeats.

<!-- S13 -->
A pendulum swings.
A quartz crystal vibrates.

<!-- S14 -->
The clock in Boulder
counts the waves of a laser
locked to strontium atoms.
About four hundred and twenty-nine
trillion of them every second.

<!-- S15 -->
Count enough repetitions,
and you have a second.
The more steadily the thing repeats,
the better the clock.

<!-- S16 -->
And this is where gravity
enters the story.

<!-- S17 -->
Einstein showed that clocks
don't all run at the same rate.
A clock deeper in a gravitational field
ticks more slowly
than a clock higher up.

<!-- S18 -->
And we can measure it.
In 2010, physicists raised
one atomic clock
by just thirty-three centimetres,
and saw it run faster.

<!-- S19 -->
In 2022, the team in Boulder
went further.
They saw the difference
inside a single cloud of atoms,
across one millimetre.

<!-- S20 -->
The top of the cloud
aged faster than the bottom.
By about one part
in ten billion billion.

<!-- S21 -->
So the rate of a clock
depends on the gravity around it.
And if gravity itself fluctuated,
at random,
in the way some quantum models predict,
time would fluctuate with it.

<!-- S22 -->
Why would gravity fluctuate?
The answer comes
from that very old quantum problem.

<!-- S23 -->
In quantum mechanics,
a particle doesn't have to be
in one place.
It can be in a superposition.
Here and there, at the same time.

<!-- S24 -->
The equations of quantum mechanics
let that superposition
go on forever.
They never pick an answer.

<!-- S25 -->
And yet, whenever we look,
we see one answer.
Here. Or there.
Never both.

<!-- S26 -->
What makes nature choose
is called the measurement problem.
And after a hundred years,
physicists still disagree about it.

<!-- S27 -->
One family of answers
is called collapse models.
They say nature makes the choice itself,
all the time,
with nobody watching.

<!-- S28 -->
In the first complete collapse model,
from the 1980s,
a single particle gets pinned down
about once every three hundred
million years.

<!-- S29 -->
But a cat is made of roughly
a billion billion billion particles.
Together, they get pinned down
around a hundred billion times a second.

<!-- S30 -->
In these models, that's why atoms
can be in two places at once,
and cats can't.

<!-- S31 -->
Now the gravity part.
Roger Penrose and Lajos Diósi
suggested where these collapses
might come from.

<!-- S32 -->
A mass in two places at once
means two different gravitational fields
at once.

<!-- S33 -->
Penrose argued that nature
can't keep that up for long.
Gravity forces the choice.

<!-- S34 -->
And the 2025 paper,
with Diósi as one of its authors,
makes a surprising connection.

<!-- S35 -->
The random noise in collapse models
can be interpreted
as random fluctuations of gravity.

<!-- S36 -->
And that works not just
for Penrose's model,
which was already tied to gravity,
but also for the most studied one,
which never was.

<!-- S37 -->
Now put the pieces together.

<!-- S38 -->
Gravity sets the rate of a clock.
If gravity fluctuates,
the rate of every clock fluctuates too.
A tiny bit faster.
A tiny bit slower.
At random.
And how much, as we'll see,
depends on the size of the clock.

<!-- S39 -->
This doesn't mean time comes
in little pixels.
The models don't predict
a smallest possible tick.

<!-- S40 -->
It's more like a walk
where every step
is a tiny bit off,
in a random direction.

<!-- S41 -->
Each step barely matters.
But the steps don't cancel out
completely.
The uncertainty piles up.

<!-- S42 -->
Run the clock four times longer,
and its typical drift doubles.

<!-- S43 -->
Even a flawless clock
would pick up this uncertainty.
No colder lab,
no better engineering,
would remove it.

<!-- S44 -->
And here's the twist.
Shielding doesn't matter.
Size does.

<!-- S45 -->
In the most studied collapse model,
the fluctuations change from place to place
over about one ten-millionth of a metre.
Roughly the size of a virus.

<!-- S46 -->
A clock that small
feels the full fluctuation.

<!-- S47 -->
A bigger clock spans many places at once,
each with a slightly different change.
They mostly average out.

<!-- S48 -->
And a pulsar,
a spinning dead star
twenty kilometres across,
would be almost completely immune.

<!-- S49 -->
In Penrose's version,
the scale is about a hundred times smaller.
But the pattern holds.
Bigger clocks average more of it away.

<!-- S50 -->
So the most fundamentally uncertain clock
isn't the cheapest one.
It's the smallest one.

<!-- S51 -->
So how big is the drift?

<!-- S52 -->
With the standard settings
of the most studied collapse model,
a clock of the size
where the effect is strongest,
running for a whole year,
would pick up an uncertainty
of about ten to the minus twenty-eight
seconds.

<!-- S53 -->
That's a ten-thousandth
of a trillionth
of a trillionth of a second.
Other settings that experiments still allow
could make it a few hundred times larger,
or a hundred times smaller.

<!-- S54 -->
Today's best optical clocks
have ordinary timing fluctuations
of around ten to the minus seventeen
seconds after just one second.

<!-- S55 -->
Over a day,
their noise is about
a million billion times larger
than the predicted effect.

<!-- S56 -->
Even a clock that had been running
since the Big Bang
would have picked up only
a few trillionths
of a trillionth of a second.

<!-- S57 -->
And Penrose's version
predicts a drift
a few hundred times smaller still.

<!-- S58 -->
So the authors are clear.
For every clock that exists,
this effect is completely negligible.

<!-- S59 -->
Which raises a fair question.
If no clock today can measure it,
why does it matter?

<!-- S60 -->
Because it isn't really
a question about clocks.
It's a question about whether
quantum mechanics is the whole story.

<!-- S61 -->
If collapse models are wrong,
this source of uncertainty
simply disappears.

<!-- S62 -->
If they're right,
there's a fundamental limit
to how precisely any clock
can track time.
Not set by engineers.
Set by nature.

<!-- S63 -->
And collapse models
can be tested in other ways.
In 2020, researchers working
deep beneath a mountain in Italy
ruled out the simplest version
of the Diósi–Penrose model,
the one with no adjustable settings.

<!-- S64 -->
Other versions remain possible.
And the experiments keep getting better.

<!-- S65 -->
So, how precise can time actually be?

<!-- S66 -->
Maybe not infinitely precise.

<!-- S67 -->
And if that's true,
then even a perfect clock
is drifting away from something.

<!-- S68 -->
So here's a question for you.
If a flawless clock still drifts,
what exactly is it drifting away from?
