# Why Does Russia Keep Choosing Confrontation?
**Script v0.4**

<!-- S01 -->
In February 2022,
Russia launched a full-scale invasion
of Ukraine.
One of the reasons Moscow gave
was NATO creeping towards its borders.

<!-- S02 -->
Just over a year later,
Finland joined NATO.

<!-- S03 -->
Finland's border with Russia
is more than thirteen hundred
kilometres long.
Overnight, the land border
between Russia and NATO roughly doubled.

<!-- S04 -->
Then Sweden joined too.
A country that had not fought a war
since 1814.

<!-- S05 -->
A war partly justified by NATO's expansion
left Russia with far more NATO
on its border than before.

<!-- S06 -->
And that is not the only strange thing.
Russia is the largest country on Earth.
It covers more than a tenth
of all the land on the planet.

<!-- S07 -->
It has oil, gas, forests, farmland,
and some of the largest mineral reserves
in the world.

<!-- S08 -->
Yet measured by nominal GDP,
its economy is roughly
the size of Italy's.
And Russia has more than twice
as many people.

<!-- S09 -->
So why does a country with so much
keep choosing confrontation
with neighbours it could trade with?
Even when the price
is paid by Russia itself?

<!-- S10 -->
The easy answer is one man.
Vladimir Putin.

<!-- S11 -->
But Poland and the Baltic states
were asking to join NATO
long before he came to power.

<!-- S12 -->
The pattern is older
than any one president.
And to see who it really serves,
we have to start much earlier.

<!-- S12b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S13 -->
For five centuries,
Moscow grew outwards.
A small principality became an empire
that reached the Pacific.

<!-- S14 -->
Much of that growth came by conquest.
And to hold it all together,
power was gathered at the centre,
in the hands of the tsar.

<!-- S15 -->
Geography mattered.
Much of western Russia
is a wide, open plain.
No mountains, no sea
between Moscow and an invading army.

<!-- S16 -->
And armies came.
Polish troops occupied Moscow in 1610.
Napoleon reached it in 1812.
Hitler's armies came close in 1941.

<!-- S17 -->
The Second World War
cost the Soviet Union
around twenty-seven million lives.

<!-- S18 -->
That fear is real.
And it deserves to be taken seriously.

<!-- S19 -->
But fear does not decide
what a country does with it.
You can defend your borders
without claiming the right
to run your neighbours.

<!-- S20 -->
Here is where the trouble starts.
If you only feel safe
when your neighbours are weak,
you will try to keep them weak.

<!-- S21 -->
More influence. More control.
A buffer of countries
that cannot choose against you.

<!-- S22 -->
But your neighbours have fears too.
And now you are the thing they fear.

<!-- S23 -->
So they look for protection.
Alliances. Armies.
Partners far away.

<!-- S24 -->
And you see their defence
as proof that the world
is closing in on you.

<!-- S25 -->
Each side's search for safety
makes the other side less safe.
Political scientists call it
the security dilemma.

<!-- S26 -->
Finland shows how fast it can turn.
For decades, only about one Finn in four
wanted to join NATO.

<!-- S27 -->
Within three months of the invasion,
three in four did.

<!-- S28 -->
Nobody had to talk Finland into it.
Moscow did that by itself.

<!-- S29 -->
In the twentieth century,
the empire became the Soviet Union.
A nuclear superpower,
with world-class science,
and the first human in orbit.

<!-- S30 -->
But it was run the same way.
Power at the centre,
and no room for opposition.

<!-- S31 -->
A system that could mobilise
almost anything...
except the truth about its own mistakes.

<!-- S32 -->
In 1991, it fell apart.
Russia was left with about
three quarters of the territory,
and only half of the people.

<!-- S33 -->
Fourteen other republics
became independent countries.
Most had no wish
to be ruled from Moscow again.

<!-- S34 -->
The decade that followed was brutal.
Between 1991 and 1998,
Russia's economy shrank
by around forty per cent.

<!-- S35 -->
Hyperinflation wiped out
the savings of millions.
Life expectancy for Russian men
fell below sixty.

<!-- S36 -->
Meanwhile, a handful of insiders
picked up some of the biggest companies
for a fraction of their value.

<!-- S38 -->
For millions of people,
freedom arrived together with chaos,
crime, and humiliation.

<!-- S39 -->
And when freedom feels like chaos,
a promise of order
becomes very attractive.
Even if it costs some freedom.

<!-- S40 -->
On the last day of 1999,
Vladimir Putin became acting president.
He promised exactly that.
Order, and a strong state.

<!-- S41 -->
And for a while, it seemed to work.
Oil rose from under twenty dollars
a barrel to over a hundred.
Wages and pensions grew year after year.

<!-- S42 -->
The state took back control
of the biggest oil and gas companies,
and of television.
And many Russians accepted
less political competition
in exchange for stability.

<!-- S43 -->
But there is a trap in that bargain.
Institutions too weak to challenge
a popular leader today
are too weak to stop
a destructive one tomorrow.

<!-- S44 -->
There was also a wound to heal.
In 2005, Putin called the collapse
of the Soviet Union
the greatest geopolitical catastrophe
of the twentieth century.

<!-- S45 -->
In that view, the neighbours
are not simply independent countries.
They are a lost sphere of influence.
And their choices become questions
of Russian prestige.

<!-- S46 -->
Georgia, 2008.
Crimea, 2014.

<!-- S47 -->
In 2021, Putin published an essay
arguing that Russians and Ukrainians
are one people.
Months later,
the tanks crossed the border.

<!-- S48 -->
But Ukraine is not a lost province.
It is a country,
with its own history and its own choices.
Living next door
does not give anyone the right to rule.

<!-- S49 -->
So far, this sounds like a story
about ideas.
But there is a second mechanism,
and it is about how decisions get made.

<!-- S50 -->
Three days before the invasion,
Russian television broadcast
a meeting of the Security Council.

<!-- S51 -->
One by one, the top officials
stood up and backed the decision
to recognise two breakaway regions
in eastern Ukraine.

<!-- S52 -->
When the foreign intelligence chief
stumbled over his words,
Putin cut him off.
Speak plainly, he told him.

<!-- S53 -->
It was a glimpse of a system
where the safest thing
to tell the leader
is what he already believes.

<!-- S54 -->
When bad news is dangerous,
bad news stops travelling upwards.
Loyalty becomes worth more
than judgement.

<!-- S55 -->
Russia still has brilliant experts.
But expertise is not much use
when an inconvenient conclusion
can cost you your career.

<!-- S56 -->
A war that Moscow reportedly expected
to take Kyiv within days
is now in its fifth year.

<!-- S57 -->
And there is a third mechanism.
The one that explains
why confrontation keeps coming back.

<!-- S58 -->
A government in conflict
with the outside world
can treat its critics as traitors.

<!-- S59 -->
Since 2012, Russian law has branded
more than a thousand journalists,
academics, activists, and organisations
as foreign agents.

<!-- S60 -->
Economic trouble can be blamed
on hostile foreign powers.
And loyalty to the government
can be sold as loyalty to the nation.

<!-- S61 -->
That does not mean every conflict
is staged as a distraction.
The point is simpler.

<!-- S62 -->
Once a system depends on an enemy,
peace becomes politically awkward.

<!-- S63 -->
Which brings us back to the question
of who this pattern serves.
Not what is good for Russia.
What is good for the people who run it.

<!-- S64 -->
A country benefits from trade,
investment, and neighbours
who are not afraid of it.

<!-- S65 -->
A ruling circle with few limits
on its power needs something else.
Control. Loyalty.
And no one able to replace it.

<!-- S66 -->
Oil and gas make that easier.
In some years, they brought in
about half of all federal revenue.

<!-- S67 -->
A state that lives off exports
needs its taxpayers less.
What it needs is control of the pipes.

<!-- S68 -->
And the bill for confrontation
is paid by the country,
not by the people who chose it.

<!-- S69 -->
Before the invasion, Russia supplied
about forty-five per cent of the gas
the European Union imported.
By 2025, it was about thirteen.

<!-- S70 -->
Hundreds of thousands of Russians
left the country,
many of them young and skilled.

<!-- S71 -->
And Russia now leans heavily
on a single large partner.
China.

<!-- S72 -->
Russia sells it mostly oil and gas.
China sells back cars, machines,
and electronics.

<!-- S73 -->
China buys a lot of Russian oil.
But it has other suppliers.
Russia, cut off from Europe,
has far fewer buyers.

<!-- S74 -->
A war meant to prove
Russia's independence
has left it more dependent than before.

<!-- S75 -->
So is this simply Russia's fate?
The old empire, the open plain,
a national character that cannot change?

<!-- S76 -->
Not really.
Geography explains the fear.
History explains the habits.
Neither one makes the choices.

<!-- S77 -->
The pattern keeps returning
because it serves a particular system.
One that needs an enemy abroad
to justify its power at home.

<!-- S78 -->
And that raises a harder question.
If the pattern comes from the system,
what would it take to change it?
And how long would that take?

<!-- S79 -->
And that is where we go next.
Because replacing one leader
may be the easy part.
Changing the system around him
is another story.
