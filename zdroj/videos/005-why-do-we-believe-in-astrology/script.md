<!-- S01 -->
You know your star sign.
Maybe you are a Leo.
Maybe a Scorpio. Maybe a Capricorn.
And you have probably read your horoscope
at least once and thought:
"Okay. That actually sounds like me."

<!-- S02 -->
But here is a strange question.
Why?
Why should the position of Mars,
Venus or Jupiter on the day you were born
have anything to do with who you are?

<!-- S03 -->
And if the stars really do decide
what you are like,
why can't astrologers even agree
on which sign you belong to?

<!-- S04 -->
Because there is a rather large problem
hiding in the sky.
It is called Ophiuchus.
The zodiac supposedly has twelve signs.
Aries. Taurus. Gemini.
Cancer. Leo. Virgo.

<!-- S05 -->
Libra. Scorpio. Sagittarius.
Capricorn. Aquarius. Pisces.
Nice and tidy.
Twelve signs. Twelve months.
Everything fits.
Except the Sun does not actually pass
through twelve constellations.

<!-- S06 -->
It passes through thirteen.
The thirteenth is Ophiuchus.
The Serpent Bearer.
And yes, the Sun really does move
across it every year,
for nearly three weeks,
from the end of November.

<!-- S07 -->
So if your personality is decided
by the constellation behind the Sun
on the day you were born,
what happens if you were born
while the Sun was in Ophiuchus?

<!-- S08 -->
Are you a Scorpio?
A Sagittarius?
Or the forbidden thirteenth sign?
Western astrology has an answer,
and it is a perfectly good one.

<!-- S09 -->
Its twelve signs were never based
on the boundaries of the constellations.
There is no thirteenth sign to add,
because the signs of astrology
are not the constellations in the sky.

<!-- S10 -->
Because the real constellations
do not sit in twelve equal slices
of thirty degrees each.
They are different sizes,
and the Sun spends
different amounts of time in them.
So the real sky looks something like this.

<!-- S11 -->
And the astrological zodiac
looks like this.
The second one is wonderfully neat.
The first one is what the sky really does.

<!-- S12 -->
So what is a sign, if it is not a star?
It is an angle.
Thirty degrees of a circle
that starts where the Sun stands
on the first day of spring.
Pinned to our seasons,
not to anything out there.

<!-- S13 -->
And that matters,
because the Earth is wobbling.
Very slowly.
Its axis of rotation precesses,
like a spinning top,
and one full wobble takes
roughly twenty-six thousand years.

<!-- S14 -->
So the first day of spring
creeps along the sky,
about one degree every seventy-two years,
away from the stars
it once lined up with.

<!-- S15 -->
Western astrology does not mind.
Its signs follow the seasons,
and the seasons have not moved.

<!-- S16 -->
But the astrology practised across India
made the opposite choice.
It kept its signs tied to the stars,
and it corrects for the drift.

<!-- S17 -->
That correction has a name and a number.
Today it is about twenty-four degrees.
Which is most of a whole sign.

<!-- S18 -->
So take one person.
One birth, one sky, one set of planets.
Western astrology calls them a Leo.
Indian astrology calls them a Cancer.

<!-- S19 -->
Work out how often that happens
and it comes to roughly
four people out of every five.
Four in five would get a different sign
depending on which astrologer they asked.

<!-- S20 -->
Which gives the question we started with
a much sharper edge.
If two systems cannot even agree
on which thirty-degree slice is yours,
the real question is why
thirty degrees of anything
should describe a person at all.

<!-- S21 -->
Fine, says astrology.
The signs are only the frame.
The important thing
is the exact position of the planets.
All right. Let us calculate a horoscope.

<!-- S22 -->
Give an astrologer your date of birth,
your exact time of birth,
and the place,
and they can calculate your birth chart.

<!-- S23 -->
Where the Sun, the Moon
and the planets stood
is worked out astronomically.
That is the easy part.

<!-- S24 -->
A computer can tell you
where every planet was that morning,
to a fraction of a second of arc.

<!-- S25 -->
But then comes the interesting bit.
The astrologer interprets them.
Mars means one thing.
Venus means another.
Saturn means something else.

<!-- S26 -->
Then you add houses.
And aspects. And the ascendant.
And suddenly a list of coordinates
has become a personality profile.

<!-- S27 -->
The planets are real.
The calculations are real.
The interpretation is something else.
It is a system of symbolic meanings.
And here is the question.
Why should those meanings
match a human personality?

<!-- S28 -->
What connects the position of Saturn,
more than a billion kilometres away,
with your temperament?

<!-- S29 -->
There is no known mechanism.
And gravity does not help.
Jupiter does pull on a newborn, of course.
But gravity is just gravity.

<!-- S30 -->
It carries no message saying
this child will be optimistic
and that one will be ambitious.

<!-- S31 -->
And even if it somehow did,
it still would not explain
why astrologers give each planet
a completely different
psychological meaning.

<!-- S32 -->
That is a rather serious problem
for a system that claims
to tell you who you are.
And there is another
wonderfully arbitrary part.
Why the exact moment of birth?

<!-- S33 -->
Imagine two babies.
One is born a minute before midnight.
The other a minute after.
Two minutes apart.

<!-- S34 -->
And suddenly the planets have moved
just enough to give them
different horoscopes.
But what about the moment of conception?
What about the first heartbeat?

<!-- S35 -->
Or the moment the doctor
actually wrote the time down?
Which one is cosmically important?
Nature does not appear to contain
a magical switch labelled:
"Personality. Calculate now."

<!-- S36 -->
And yet astrology treats that one moment
as enormously significant.
Fine, you might say.
But my horoscope is always accurate.
All right. Let us try something.

<!-- S37 -->
Here is a horoscope.
You are generally a thoughtful person,
but you sometimes doubt yourself.
You value close relationships,
although you also need time alone.

<!-- S38 -->
You can be very determined
when something matters to you.
And at times you are
rather too critical of yourself.

<!-- S39 -->
How accurate is that?
Pretty good?
Maybe surprisingly good?
Here is the problem.
That description was not written for you.
It could have been written
for almost anyone.

<!-- S40 -->
And that is not an accident.
It is called the Barnum effect.
Or the Forer effect,
after the psychologist who tested it
in nineteen forty-eight.

<!-- S41 -->
He handed thirty-nine students
what he told them
were personal personality profiles.
He had assembled the text
out of a newsstand astrology book.

<!-- S42 -->
Every student got exactly the same one.
Then he asked them to rate it,
from zero to five,
for how well it described them.
The average was four point two six.

<!-- S43 -->
Only then did he tell them the truth.
And many of them still said:
"But that is me."
So are horoscopes just random?
Not exactly.
This is where we have to be fair.

<!-- S44 -->
A professional astrologer
is not sitting there pressing
a button marked "random horoscope".

<!-- S45 -->
The positions really are calculated.
The chart really is built
according to an elaborate set of rules.

<!-- S46 -->
But those rules rest on one assumption.
That the positions mean something
about a person.
And that is the assumption
that needs evidence.

<!-- S47 -->
Because you could replace
the whole system with a text generator.
Pick a tendency. Add some ambiguity.
Mention relationships, or a career,
or personal growth.

<!-- S48 -->
And you get something like this.
"The coming weeks may bring
some uncertainty in your relationships,
but they could also open
an opportunity for personal growth."

<!-- S49 -->
Did Mars cause that sentence?
No. I just wrote it.
And yet, does it sound
like it could be your horoscope?
This is where the argument
stops being philosophical.
Because we can actually test it.

<!-- S50 -->
If astrology works,
then an astrologer should be able
to match a birth chart to a person
better than chance can.

<!-- S51 -->
In nineteen eighty-five,
a double-blind test of astrology
was published in Nature.
Astrologers helped design it,
and they said in advance
what would count as success.
Their own figure was one in two.

<!-- S52 -->
Each of them was handed a birth chart
and three personality profiles,
and asked which of the three
belonged to that chart.

<!-- S53 -->
Guessing alone would get one in three.
The original analysis found
nothing better than chance.
Forty right out of a hundred and sixteen.

<!-- S54 -->
But the statistics were later disputed.
Add up the answers a different way
and a faint signal appears,
right on the edge of meaning anything.

<!-- S55 -->
Which is why one experiment
is never the last word.
Including this one.
So the question is not
what a single study found,
but whether astrologers beat chance
when you keep testing them.

<!-- S56 -->
More than forty such tests exist.
Nearly seven hundred astrologers,
over a thousand birth charts.
Pooled together, the effect
is indistinguishable from nothing.

<!-- S57 -->
And there is a second measurement
that needs nobody to be right.
Give one chart to two astrologers
and ask them what it says.

<!-- S58 -->
Across twenty-five studies,
their agreement comes out at about
one tenth, on a scale
where one would be perfect.

<!-- S59 -->
For a test you would actually use
on a person, psychologists want
eight tenths or better.
Before you can ask whether astrology
is true, it has to say
the same thing twice.

<!-- S60 -->
And that is the key distinction.
It does not matter
whether an astrologer can produce
an explanation that sounds convincing.

<!-- S61 -->
What matters is whether it predicts
something before we know the answer.
That is how you tell a real effect
from a good story.

<!-- S62 -->
And here is where this
gets much more interesting.
Because people who believe in astrology
are not stupid.

<!-- S63 -->
Astrology appeals to something
our brains are extremely good at.
Finding patterns.
Finding meaning.

<!-- S64 -->
Remembering the hits.
Forgetting the misses.
And building a coherent story
out of incomplete information.

<!-- S65 -->
If your horoscope says
you may meet a difficult situation
this week,
and something annoying happens
on Thursday, you remember it.

<!-- S66 -->
If nothing happens, you do not.
And if it says you may go through
a period of emotional uncertainty,
that cannot be wrong.
You can always find something.

<!-- S67 -->
So let us go back to the beginning.
You were told that your personality
was written in the sky
on the day you were born.

<!-- S68 -->
But the signs are not the constellations.
There are thirteen of those
on the Sun's path, not twelve,
and they are not equal pieces of sky.
Two whole schools of astrology
disagree by nearly a sign
about which one is yours.

<!-- S69 -->
No known force carries a personality
from Saturn to a delivery room.
And a description written for everybody
can feel like it was written for you.

<!-- S70 -->
So does that prove
that every astrologer is a fraud?
No.
It proves something much simpler.
The claims of astrology need evidence.

<!-- S71 -->
And when they are tested carefully,
astrology does not behave
like something that can predict a person.

<!-- S72 -->
And perhaps that is the most fascinating
thing about astrology.
Not that Mars controls your personality.
It does not.

<!-- S73 -->
Not that Saturn knows
what will happen to your career.
It does not.
The fascinating part is that your brain
can look at a handful of vague sentences
and say: "That is me."

<!-- S74 -->
We are pattern-recognition machines.
We see faces in clouds.
We see shapes in random noise.
We connect unrelated events.

<!-- S75 -->
And sometimes
we see meaning in the planets.
The stars are real.
The planets are real.
The mathematics is real.
The question is whether the story
we have built around them is.

<!-- S76 -->
So the next time your horoscope tells you
that something important
is about to happen,
remember.
The universe probably
is not sending you a message.
Your brain is.
