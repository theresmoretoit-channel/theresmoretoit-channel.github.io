<!-- S01 -->
Have you ever noticed something strange
about vegetables?
Whenever someone says:
"You should eat more vegetables."
What do they actually mean?

<!-- S02 -->
Tomatoes.
Cucumber.
And, in the better case...
a pepper.
That's it.

<!-- S03 -->
Apparently the entire plant kingdom
has been reduced to three things
you can slice into thin pieces
and put next to a schnitzel.

<!-- S04 -->
And I'm getting a little tired of it.
Especially the tomatoes.

<!-- S05 -->
Because unless you have a really good one,
picked when it is actually ripe...
a supermarket tomato can taste
like someone described a tomato to water.

<!-- S06 -->
And the cucumber isn't much better.

<!-- S06b -->
Crunchy?
Yes.

<!-- S06c -->
Refreshing?
Sure.

<!-- S06d -->
Does it actually taste of anything?
Well...
sometimes.

<!-- S07 -->
So why do we eat these things all the time?
And more importantly...
is there actually any other vegetable?
Spoiler:
there are quite a few.

<!-- S08 -->
But before we get to those...
that thing about the tomato.
It is not just a feeling.
Somebody went and measured it.

<!-- S09 -->
Researchers in Florida and China
took three hundred and ninety-eight
different tomato varieties
and analysed what is actually in them.

<!-- S10 -->
Then they sat panels of people down
to taste a selection of them,
to find out which of those chemicals
we actually notice.

<!-- S11 -->
The answer was not subtle.
Modern commercial varieties have
significantly less of the compounds
that make a tomato taste of tomato.

<!-- S12 -->
So let's back up.
Why are tomatoes and cucumbers everywhere?
They are incredibly convenient.
You don't have to cook them.
You don't even really have to prepare them.

<!-- S13 -->
Wash.
Slice.
Put on plate.
Congratulations.
You have vegetables.
They also work with almost everything.
Meat?
Cucumber and tomato.
Sandwich?
Cucumber and tomato.

<!-- S14 -->
Burger?
Tomato.
Salad?
Tomato and cucumber.
Breakfast?
You guessed it.
And if you're lucky...
someone brings a pepper.
It's basically the default
vegetable starter pack.

<!-- S15 -->
But there is one problem.
The vegetables that are easiest to eat
aren't necessarily the vegetables
with the most interesting flavours.

<!-- S16 -->
A tomato can be absolutely fantastic.
A really ripe one, grown well,
can be sweet, acidic, aromatic
and incredibly flavourful.

<!-- S17 -->
But that is not necessarily
what we get in a supermarket.

<!-- S18 -->
A commercial tomato has to survive
harvesting, packaging, transport,
storage, and sitting on a shelf
without turning into tomato soup.

<!-- S19 -->
So there are good reasons to choose
varieties that are firm and durable.
And sometimes that comes
at the expense of flavour.

<!-- S20 -->
Some of that is the price of durability.
But one of the losses
was not a trade at all.
It was an accident.

<!-- S21 -->
Growers wanted tomatoes that ripen evenly.
No dark green patch around the stem.
The whole field ready on the same day.

<!-- S22 -->
So breeders selected a variety
that goes uniformly pale green
before it ripens.
And that trait is essentially one gene.

<!-- S23 -->
Here is the part nobody expected.
That gene builds the green machinery
inside the fruit itself.

<!-- S24 -->
An unripe tomato is not just waiting.
It is quietly photosynthesising.
It makes part of its own sugar
while it hangs on the plant.

<!-- S25 -->
Switch that gene off, and you get
exactly what was ordered:
a tomato that ripens beautifully evenly.

<!-- S26 -->
You also get less sugar,
and less of the pigment
that makes it red.
Nobody chose that.
They chose the way it looks,
and the sweetness quietly left with it.

<!-- S27 -->
And then we finish the job at home.
The aroma compounds in a tomato
are damaged below twelve degrees.
And some of the genes that make them
never properly switch back on.

<!-- S28 -->
So the fridge doesn't pause a tomato.
It takes something away
that doesn't fully come back.
And the cucumber has its own version
of the same story.

<!-- S29 -->
A wild cucumber is bitter.
Aggressively bitter.
It is full of a defence chemical
that discourages most things
from eating it.

<!-- S30 -->
Domestication switched that off
in the fruit.
We bred the bitterness out on purpose,
and it worked.

<!-- S31 -->
We asked agriculture for vegetables
that are firm, even, cheap,
and that don't fight back.
We got exactly that.

<!-- S32 -->
So if your entire experience
with vegetables is supermarket cucumber
and supermarket tomato...
I can understand why you might conclude:

<!-- S33 -->
"I don't like vegetables."
Maybe you don't dislike vegetables.
Maybe you just dislike these two.
Because here's the funny thing.
Vegetables aren't one thing.

<!-- S34 -->
There are dozens and dozens
of completely different plants
we eat as vegetables.
And they don't all taste remotely alike.

<!-- S35 -->
Take carrots.
You can eat them raw.
But roast them with a little oil
and they turn sweeter
and far more interesting.
Or broccoli.
You don't have to boil it
until it becomes a sad green cloud.

<!-- S36 -->
Roast it.
Grill it.
Add garlic.
Add some spices.
And it becomes an entirely different food.

<!-- S37 -->
Cauliflower?
Same story.
Roasted cauliflower can have
a surprisingly rich, nutty flavour.

<!-- S38 -->
And then there is cabbage.
Fresh.
Cooked.
Fermented.
Suddenly we're not talking
about a boring side dish anymore.

<!-- S39 -->
Personally, I think vegetables
are much more interesting
when they actually have an opinion.
Radishes.
Onions.
Garlic.
Horseradish.
Chilli peppers.

<!-- S40 -->
These don't sit quietly on your plate.
They arrive and announce:
"Hello. I have a flavour."

<!-- S41 -->
And if you like spicy food,
chillies are almost cheating.
A tiny amount can completely change
an otherwise boring meal.

<!-- S42 -->
And there are endless varieties.
Mild ones.
Smoky ones.
Ridiculously hot ones that exist mainly
to make you question your life choices.

<!-- S43 -->
This is another thing I find strange.
When people talk about eating vegetables,
they often imagine them raw.

<!-- S44 -->
A few slices of cucumber.
A tomato.
Maybe some lettuce.
And that's supposed to be exciting.
But cooking changes vegetables dramatically.

<!-- S45 -->
Roast a beetroot.
Grill a pepper.
Caramelise an onion.
Bake a whole cauliflower.

<!-- S46 -->
And suddenly vegetables aren't
just something sitting beside
your actual food.
They are the food.

<!-- S47 -->
And this might actually explain
why some people say:
"I don't like vegetables."
Maybe they were simply introduced to them
in the worst possible way.

<!-- S48 -->
Boiled broccoli.
Overcooked cauliflower.
Frozen spinach.
A cucumber that has spent
three days in the fridge.

<!-- S49 -->
And then someone says:
"You need to eat your vegetables."
That's not exactly
a great marketing campaign.

<!-- S50 -->
Imagine doing the same thing with potatoes.
Boil them without salt.
Don't season them.
Leave them until they're cold.

<!-- S51 -->
And then tell a child:
"See? Potatoes are healthy."
They might never eat a potato again.

<!-- S52 -->
The good news is that you don't need
to force yourself to eat
vegetables you hate.

<!-- S53 -->
There isn't a law saying:
"Every human must consume
exactly one cucumber
and half a tomato every evening."

<!-- S54 -->
You can choose.
And the choice is much wider
than the three things on the plate.
When did anyone last offer you
kohlrabi?
Or celeriac?
Or asparagus?

<!-- S55 -->
These are not exotic.
They are in the same shop,
a few steps away,
and nobody ever mentions them.

<!-- S56 -->
And any of them can be eaten
raw, roasted, grilled, fermented,
pickled, or stuffed
into something else entirely.

<!-- S57 -->
And just to be fair...
I'm not saying tomatoes
and cucumbers are bad.
A really good tomato?
Fantastic.
A fresh cucumber on a hot summer day?
Also fantastic.

<!-- S58 -->
The problem is not that
tomatoes and cucumbers exist.
The problem is that we have somehow
turned them into the default
definition of vegetables.

<!-- S59 -->
As if the vegetable department
had two products...
and occasionally a pepper
gets through security.

<!-- S60 -->
And there is actually
a bigger lesson here.
If somebody tells you:
"You should eat more vegetables."
The answer doesn't have to be:
"Oh no. More cucumber."

<!-- S61 -->
It can be:
"What vegetables do I actually like?"
Maybe it's roasted carrots.
Maybe it's spicy peppers.
Maybe it's sauerkraut.
Maybe it's something
you've never even tried.

<!-- S62 -->
Because vegetables aren't a punishment.
And they certainly don't have to
taste like water.

<!-- S63 -->
So next time someone puts a plate
in front of you containing tomato,
cucumber, and, in the better case,
a pepper...

<!-- S64 -->
remember:
this isn't the entire vegetable kingdom.
It's barely the tutorial.

<!-- S65 -->
And somewhere out there
there is a vegetable
you might actually enjoy eating.
You just have to find it.
