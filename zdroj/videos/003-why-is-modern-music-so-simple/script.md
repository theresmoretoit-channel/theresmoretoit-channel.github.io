# Why Is Modern Music So Simple?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Have you ever listened to a modern pop song and thought:

That's it?

Four chords.

A simple beat.

A short melody.

<!-- S02 -->
The same chorus repeated over and over again.

And yet somehow… it has hundreds of millions of streams.

<!-- S03 -->
Meanwhile, you might listen to older music
and hear complicated chord changes,
long instrumental sections,
unexpected key changes,
and melodies that seem to go somewhere.

<!-- S04 -->
So what happened?

Did musicians simply become less talented?

Did streaming services turn every song
into a three-minute advertisement for the chorus?

<!-- S05 -->Or is something more interesting happening?

Because modern music may be simpler in some ways
and far more complicated in others.

<!-- S06 -->
And there's another problem.

When we compare modern music with music from the past,
we may not actually be comparing the same thing.

<!-- S07 -->
Why does modern music often sound so simple?

And perhaps more importantly:

Is it actually simpler at all?

<!-- S08 -->
First, we need to define what we mean by simple.

Because music can be simple in one dimension
and incredibly complicated in another.

<!-- S09 -->
Take harmony.

A huge number of popular songs are built around just a few chords.

Sometimes four.

Sometimes three.

<!-- S10 -->
The same chord progression may repeat for almost the entire track.

The melody might also be relatively simple.

<!-- S11 -->
Short phrases.

Repeated patterns.

And a chorus designed to get stuck in your head after hearing it once.

<!-- S12 -->The rhythm is often predictable too.

A steady beat.

A regular tempo.

Verse.

Chorus.

Bridge.

Final chorus.

<!-- S13 -->
None of this means the song is bad.

Writing something complicated is relatively easy.

Writing something simple that millions of people remember is much harder.

<!-- S14 -->
But there is another side to modern music.

The composition may be simple.

The production often isn't.

<!-- S15 -->
A modern pop song can contain dozens,
or even hundreds, of individual sounds and layers.

<!-- S16 -->
Synthesisers.

Samples.

Vocal harmonies.

Effects.

Compression.

Tiny sounds appearing for only a fraction of a second.

<!-- S17 -->
The listener may not consciously notice most of them.

But they are there.

So perhaps the complexity hasn't disappeared.

<!-- S18 -->
Perhaps it has moved.

From the notes themselves…

to the sound of the recording.

<!-- S19 -->Still, there is something real happening.

And here's where it gets complicated.

Some aspects of popular music
have become simpler, or more restricted.

<!-- S20 -->Certain harmonic patterns have become less common,
while others have become increasingly familiar.

But that doesn't mean music as a whole
became less diverse.

<!-- S21 -->In fact, some measures of musical diversity
tell a very different story.

We'll come back to that.

<!-- S22 -->
The famous four-chord progression is almost a cliché at this point.

You can hear similar harmonic patterns
across pop, rock, country and dance music.

<!-- S23 -->It sounds like this.

<!-- S24 -->
Why?

Well, one reason is simple.

They work.

Certain chord progressions create a satisfying feeling of tension and release.

<!-- S25 -->
They are familiar.

They are emotionally flexible.

And they leave plenty of room for something else.

The hook.

<!-- S26 -->
Think of it like the background of a painting.

Nobody complains that the sky is too simple
if the important thing is happening in the foreground.

<!-- S27 -->
But technology has changed something fundamental.

Imagine buying an album in nineteen seventy-five.

<!-- S28 -->You spend money on it.

You take it home.

You are probably willing to give the music some time.

<!-- S29 -->
Maybe the interesting part starts after a minute.

Maybe the strange intro only makes sense
when you hear the entire album.

<!-- S30 -->
Now imagine a streaming service.

You have access to millions of songs.

And if you don't like what you hear…

you can skip.

Immediately.

<!-- S31 -->
There is almost no cost.

And that changes the incentives.

A song now has to compete
with virtually every other song ever recorded.

<!-- S32 -->
The first few seconds matter.

A memorable sound.

A recognisable voice.

A beat.

Something that says:

Don't skip me.

<!-- S33 -->And this is one reason
why modern songs often get to the point faster.

Long instrumental introductions have become less common.

<!-- S34 -->Researchers measured more than three hundred
American top ten singles,
released between nineteen eighty-six and twenty fifteen.

<!-- S35 -->The wait before the first vocal
had fallen from over twenty seconds
to about five seconds.

A drop of nearly eighty per cent.

<!-- S36 -->And over the same period,
the average tempo rose by about eight per cent.

<!-- S37 -->The same researchers also measured the titles.

Song names got shorter.

More and more of them are a single word.

<!-- S38 -->And in pop, the title is usually the hook —
the line you actually sing.

After the vocals start,
it now arrives about eighteen per cent sooner.

<!-- S39 -->And there is something else.

Sixty years ago, an instrumental could top the charts.

Today that almost never happens.

<!-- S40 -->Almost everything in the charts is sung.

And the singing is about anything at all —
love, money, grief, a Tuesday afternoon.

<!-- S41 -->
The song is not necessarily trying to be lazy.

It is competing in an environment
where the listener can leave at any moment.

<!-- S42 -->
And now music exists inside an enormous system of data.

Streaming platforms can measure what people skip,
what they replay,
and where they stop listening.

<!-- S43 -->This doesn't mean an algorithm tells musicians which chords to use.

But data changes the environment.

This is not a conspiracy.

It's evolution.

<!-- S44 -->
Then social media added something else.

Sometimes a song doesn't become popular
because people love the whole song.

Sometimes they love fifteen seconds of it.

<!-- S45 -->
One particular line.

One drop.

One strange sound.

One moment that works perfectly in a short video.

<!-- S46 -->If one section can spread across millions of videos,
that section becomes incredibly valuable.

Almost like a tiny musical logo.

<!-- S47 -->
Now we come to the most important problem.

Was old music actually better?

Or more complicated?

<!-- S48 -->
Well…
we have a very serious case of survivorship bias.

When we think about the music of the nineteen sixties,
we remember The Beatles.

<!-- S49 -->
The Rolling Stones.

Pink Floyd.

Led Zeppelin.

But those decades also produced thousands of songs that nobody remembers.

<!-- S50 -->
Simple songs.

Repetitive songs.

Terrible songs.

Songs that were probably annoying even when they were new.

<!-- S51 -->
They simply disappeared.

Time acted like a filter.

The music that survives tends to be the music that people continued to value.

<!-- S52 -->
So when we compare today's charts with the past,
we are often comparing:

the average music of today

with

the best music that survived sixty years.

<!-- S53 -->
That's not exactly a fair fight.

Imagine someone from the year twenty seventy-six
listening only to the greatest songs from our time.

<!-- S54 -->
They may conclude that the twenty-twenties
were an incredible golden age of music.

They might never even discover half of the songs we currently complain about.

<!-- S55 -->And complicated music never disappeared.

It may just not be sitting
at the top of the charts.

<!-- S56 -->When researchers analysed the chords and textures
of fifty years of American chart music,
they found that variety did not steadily decline.

<!-- S57 -->It fell to a low point in the mid-eighties,
and then rose again —
to a peak in the early two-thousands.

<!-- S58 -->
In classical music, complexity often lived in harmony.

In progressive rock, in unusual time signatures.

In modern electronic music,
it may live somewhere completely different.

<!-- S59 -->
Sound design.

Rhythmic layering.

The construction of a beat.

The manipulation of a human voice.

<!-- S60 -->
A producer might spend hours creating a sound that lasts two seconds.

The final song may use only four chords.

<!-- S61 -->And this kind of craft is no longer locked inside a studio.

Today, almost anyone can make music.

A computer can contain an entire virtual orchestra.

<!-- S62 -->A studio that once filled a room
now fits inside a laptop.

Anyone with a computer can make a record.

<!-- S63 -->And that is one reason why a single streaming service
now carries over a hundred million tracks.

<!-- S64 -->And enough software to keep you busy
until the heat death of the universe.

<!-- S65 -->But much of that software already contains accumulated knowledge.

Presets, drum samples, mastering chains —
decades of studio techniques, packaged into tools.

<!-- S66 -->So the songwriter no longer has to carry all the complexity.

The song can be simple
because the sound around it doesn't have to be.

<!-- S67 -->Those same four chords, on their own.

<!-- S68 -->And the same four chords, produced.

<!-- S69 -->
It's a bit like comparing an old mechanical watch
with a modern smartphone.

The watch may be mechanically fascinating.

<!-- S70 -->
The smartphone may look like a simple black rectangle.

But simplicity on the surface
doesn't necessarily mean simplicity underneath.

<!-- S71 -->
Perhaps the final answer is much older
than streaming or artificial intelligence.

Humans like patterns.

We like familiarity.

We like repetition.

<!-- S72 -->
When the brain can predict what comes next,
it receives a small reward.

But if the music is completely predictable, it becomes boring.

<!-- S73 -->
So successful music often lives in a very interesting zone.

Familiar enough to understand.

Different enough to remain interesting.

<!-- S74 -->So…
why is modern music so simple?

Partly because streaming rewards immediacy,
and partly because simple music has always worked.

<!-- S75 -->But perhaps mainstream music isn't becoming simpler.

Perhaps it is becoming more optimised.

<!-- S76 -->
Optimised for attention.

Optimised for memorability.

Optimised for replay.

And optimised for surviving in a world
where millions of other songs are only one click away.

<!-- S77 -->
Meanwhile, somewhere on the internet,
someone is probably writing a seventeen-minute song
in eleven-eight time…
and putting it online for twelve people to discover.

<!-- S78 -->
And honestly?

That's probably fine too.

Because music has never been just one thing.

<!-- S79 -->
The real question may not be:

"Why is modern music so simple?"

Maybe the better question is:

"Simple compared to what?"

<!-- S80 -->
Because if we compare today's average pop song
with the greatest music of the past,
modern music can look disappointingly simple.

<!-- S81 -->
But if we compare average music from every era…

we might discover something slightly uncomfortable.

<!-- S82 -->
Maybe people have always been writing catchy, repetitive songs.

We just forgot most of them.
