# Why Might Aliens Look Surprisingly Human?

<!-- S01 -->
The crab has evolved
at least five different times.

<!-- S02 -->
Different groups of crustaceans,
each on its own branch,
kept ending up with the same body.
A flat, wide shell.
A tail tucked underneath.

<!-- S03 -->
It happened so often
that biologists gave it a name.
Carcinisation.

<!-- S04 -->
And it's become a running joke.
Nature keeps trying to make a crab.

<!-- S05 -->
But behind the joke
is a serious idea.

<!-- S06 -->
Evolution doesn't have to invent
everything from scratch.
When it faces the same problem,
it often finds the same answer.

<!-- S07 -->
Look at the eye of an octopus.
A lens, an iris, a retina.
It looks a lot like yours.

<!-- S08 -->
But our last common ancestor
was a small, worm-like animal
that had no such eye.
Octopuses and humans built theirs
independently.

<!-- S09 -->
And by one estimate,
eyes have evolved
on their own
more than forty times.

<!-- S10 -->
Flight evolved four times.
In insects, in pterosaurs,
in birds and in bats.

<!-- S11 -->
And look at these three.
A shark. An ichthyosaur. A dolphin.

<!-- S12 -->
A fish, a reptile and a mammal.
The same shape,
because water pushes back
on all of them the same way.

<!-- S13 -->
It works on land too.
This is a wolf skull.

<!-- S13b -->
And this is the skull of a Tasmanian tiger.

<!-- S14 -->
They look remarkably alike.
But the Tasmanian tiger
was a marsupial.
It carried its young in a pouch.

<!-- S15 -->
It was more closely related
to a kangaroo
than to any wolf.

<!-- S16 -->
This is called
convergent evolution.

<!-- S16b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S17 -->
Which raises a question.
In another video, we asked
how strange aliens could be.

<!-- S18 -->
But what if evolution
on another planet
keeps finding the same answers too?

<!-- S19 -->
Could an intelligent alien
end up looking...
surprisingly familiar?

<!-- S20 -->
Let's start with the simplest thing.
You have a left side and a right side.

<!-- S21 -->
That sounds trivial.
But almost every animal
that actively moves around
is built the same way.

<!-- S22 -->
If you mostly move in one direction,
it pays to have a front.

<!-- S23 -->
The front meets the world first.
So that's where the senses go.
Eyes, ears, a nose.
Whatever the animal has.

<!-- S24 -->
And it pays to have the brain
right next to them,
so the signals don't have far to go.

<!-- S25 -->
Biologists call this cephalisation.
Building a head.

<!-- S26 -->
You can see it in worms, insects,
octopuses and us.

<!-- S27 -->
An alien that walks, swims or flies
through its world
faces the same problem.
And physics is the same there too.

<!-- S28 -->
Now, the part that matters most
for a technological species.

<!-- S29 -->
A dolphin has a large brain.
It's clever, it's social,
it learns.

<!-- S30 -->
But a dolphin will never
light a fire,
or forge a knife.
It has no hands to make one with.

<!-- S31 -->
Crows are clever too.
Some of them carve twigs into hooks.
But everything they make,
they make with their beak.

<!-- S32 -->
Humans got lucky.
Our legs carry us around,
which leaves our hands free
to do everything else.

<!-- S33 -->
Many researchers think
it worked like a loop.
Better hands made better tools.
Better tools rewarded better brains.
And better brains
made better tools again.

<!-- S34 -->
So an alien that builds things
probably doesn't need hands like ours.

<!-- S35 -->
It might have tentacles.
Or claws. Or a trunk.
Or four legs and two arms.

<!-- S36 -->
But it would probably need
something that moves it around,
and something else that handles the world.

<!-- S37 -->
And even that isn't enough.

<!-- S38 -->
An octopus has eight
incredibly skilful arms.
It opens jars.
It escapes from aquariums.

<!-- S39 -->
But most octopuses
survive only a year or two,
and they're mostly loners.

<!-- S40 -->
Everything an octopus learns
dies with it.
Nobody passes it on.

<!-- S41 -->
A technological species
needs hands and brains.
But it also needs time,
and others to learn from.

<!-- S42 -->
And now, the twist.
There's an even bigger reason
an alien we meet might look familiar.

<!-- S43 -->
We won't be meeting
all the life in the galaxy.

<!-- S44 -->
Imagine a galaxy full of life.
Countless microbes.
Endless oceans of simple creatures.
Maybe some clever animals.

<!-- S45 -->
And just a handful of species
that build radios and spaceships.

<!-- S46 -->
If we ever meet an alien civilisation,
it will come from that handful.

<!-- S47 -->
We won't travel across the galaxy
to meet alien grass.
Alien grass won't call us.

<!-- S48 -->
We'll meet the ones
that can sense their surroundings,
handle things precisely,
work together
and build machines.

<!-- S49 -->
In other words,
the ones that solved
the same problems we did.

<!-- S50 -->
That's a filter.
And it doesn't select
for any random shape.
It selects for a toolkit.

<!-- S51 -->
That doesn't mean
the alien will look human.
It means some of its problems
may look familiar.

<!-- S52 -->
So what else might be in that toolkit?

<!-- S53 -->
Probably some kind of vision.
Wherever there's a star,
light carries an enormous amount
of information about the world.

<!-- S54 -->
The eyes might see infrared,
or ultraviolet.
There might be three of them,
or a hundred.

<!-- S55 -->
But using two eyes a little apart
is a cheap way to judge distance.
It's one reason many hunters,
like cats and owls,
have eyes facing forward.

<!-- S56 -->
And now something almost unsettling.

<!-- S57 -->
Put the eyes at the front.
Put the mouth and the nose there too,
because that's where the food goes in.

<!-- S58 -->
Add a social species
that needs to understand
each other's moods.

<!-- S59 -->
What do you get?

<!-- S60 -->
Something that looks
an awful lot like a face.

<!-- S61 -->
Not because aliens copied us.
Because social animals
face the same communication problem.

<!-- S62 -->
In 1982, a palaeontologist
named Dale Russell
tried a thought experiment.

<!-- S63 -->
What if the dinosaurs
had never died out?

<!-- S64 -->
He took a small, clever dinosaur,
a relative of Troodon,
and imagined it evolving
for another sixty-six million years.

<!-- S65 -->
The result stood upright.
It had a big head,
forward-facing eyes,
and hands with three fingers.

<!-- S66 -->
He called it the dinosauroid.

<!-- S67 -->
And many scientists criticised it.
They said it was far too human.
Basically, a person
in a dinosaur costume.

<!-- S68 -->
And that's the real debate.

<!-- S69 -->
The biologist Stephen Jay Gould argued,
if you rewound the tape of life
and played it again,
you'd almost never get humans.

<!-- S70 -->
Others, like Simon Conway Morris,
argue the opposite.
Convergence is so common
that something like us
was likely to appear.

<!-- S71 -->
They might both be right.

<!-- S72 -->
The details wouldn't repeat.
Gravity alone would change everything.

<!-- S73 -->
On a heavy world,
an alien might be short and massive.
On a light one, tall and slender.

<!-- S74 -->
It might have six limbs,
four eyes and an exoskeleton,
and look nothing like us.

<!-- S75 -->
And still have a front,
something to process information,
something to move with,
and something to build with.

<!-- S76 -->
Human in architecture,
not in appearance.

<!-- S77 -->
And that might be the answer
to both questions.

<!-- S78 -->
Life in the universe
could be wildly creative.
Things without heads,
without bodies,
without anything we'd call a face.

<!-- S79 -->
But the aliens that build spaceships
may have had to solve
the same problems we did.

<!-- S80 -->
Give life a different world,
and you might get
something unimaginable.

<!-- S81 -->
Give it the same physics
and the same problems,
and it might find
some of the same solutions.

<!-- S82 -->
Maybe the universe doesn't have
one blueprint for intelligent life.

<!-- S83 -->
Maybe it just has
a surprisingly small toolbox.
