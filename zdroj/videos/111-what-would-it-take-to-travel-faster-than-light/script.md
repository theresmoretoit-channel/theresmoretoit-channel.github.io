# What Would It Take to Travel Faster Than Light?
**Script v0.6**

<!-- S01 -->
Take one kilogram.
A bag of sugar.
Now push it to ninety-nine per cent
of the speed of light.

<!-- S02 -->
The energy you'd need
is about a hundred and thirty megatons.
More than twice the largest bomb
ever detonated.

<!-- S03 -->
For one kilogram.
A spacecraft weighs tonnes.
Push a single tonne to that speed,
and you'd use nearly a year
of all the energy humanity consumes.

<!-- S04 -->
And ninety-nine per cent isn't the limit.
Go to ninety-nine point nine,
and the bill is
three and a half times higher.
Every extra nine costs more than the last.

<!-- S05 -->
This isn't a guess.
At the Large Hadron Collider,
protons are pushed so hard
that they fall short of light
by only about three metres per second.

<!-- S06 -->
Roughly the speed of a jogger.
And no amount of extra energy
will close that last gap.
The closer you get, the more it costs,
without any limit at all.

<!-- S07 -->
So a bigger engine won't do it.
The equations of relativity
don't give a ship
a way past the speed of light.

<!-- S08 -->
And we'd need one.
The nearest star beyond the Sun
is more than four light-years away,
so even light takes four years to get there.
Crossing our own galaxy
takes light more than a hundred thousand years.

<!-- S09 -->
Our actual spacecraft are far slower,
as we saw in an earlier video.

<!-- S09b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S10 -->
Relativity does offer one consolation.
At ninety-nine per cent of light speed,
time on board runs slower.
The crew would reach the nearest star
in about seven months of their own time.

<!-- S11 -->
But back on Earth,
more than four years would pass.
Cross the galaxy that way,
and by the time you arrived,
more than a hundred thousand years
would have passed back home.

<!-- S12 -->
So here is the question.
What would we have to invent
to travel faster than light?
The answer, it turns out,
probably isn't an engine.

<!-- S13 -->
Let's start with something odd
that the universe already does.
Space itself can stretch.

<!-- S14 -->
The universe is expanding,
and galaxies more than
about fourteen billion light-years away
are moving away from us
faster than light.

<!-- S15 -->
We can still see some of them.
Nothing is breaking the speed limit.
Those galaxies aren't racing through space.
The space between us is growing.

<!-- S16 -->
So what if a ship didn't move through space,
but let space move it?

<!-- S17 -->
In 1994, a young Mexican physicist,
Miguel Alcubierre,
was working on his doctorate in Wales.
He found a solution
to Einstein's equations of gravity
that looked a lot like
the warp drive of science fiction.

<!-- S18 -->
In front of the ship,
space contracts.
Behind it, space expands.
The ship sits in a calm bubble
in the middle.

<!-- S19 -->
Inside the bubble,
the ship isn't moving fast at all.
Nobody on board would feel a thing.
But the bubble itself could cross
the distance to a star
faster than light.

<!-- S20 -->
Instead of driving faster,
you move the road.

<!-- S21 -->
But a solution to an equation
is not a machine.
It tells you what space
would have to look like.
It doesn't tell you
how to make it look that way.

<!-- S22 -->
And to make it look that way,
Alcubierre's bubble needs
something very strange.
Negative energy.

<!-- S23 -->
Less energy than empty space.
Less than nothing.

<!-- S24 -->
Quantum physics does allow a little of it.
Put two metal plates
a tiny fraction of a millimetre apart,
and they pull towards each other.

<!-- S25 -->
Physicists explain this force
by saying the gap between the plates
holds slightly less energy
than the empty space outside.
It was predicted in 1948
and measured precisely in 1997.

<!-- S26 -->
But the amounts are minuscule.
Take plates a metre across,
a thousandth of a millimetre apart.
They pull on each other
with the weight of about a tenth of a gram.

<!-- S27 -->
And the energy missing from the gap
is equal to the mass
of about three protons.

<!-- S28 -->
Quantum theory also seems to limit it.
The more negative the energy,
the smaller the region it can fill,
and the shorter it can last.

<!-- S29 -->
And Alcubierre's bubble needs a lot.
In 1997, two physicists worked out
the minimum for his original design,
with a bubble two hundred metres wide,
moving at the speed of light.

<!-- S30 -->
Under their assumptions,
the negative energy came out
at about ten billion times
the mass of the entire visible universe.

<!-- S31 -->
Later designs brought that number down
enormously.
A few even avoid negative energy altogether.
But so far, none of those
has been convincingly shown
to go faster than light.

<!-- S32 -->
And there's another catch.
The front edge of the bubble
is out of reach for anyone inside.
So the crew couldn't switch the bubble on,
or steer it.

<!-- S33 -->
The road would have to be laid
before the trip ever began.

<!-- S34 -->
So maybe we shouldn't move the road.
Maybe we should skip it.

<!-- S35 -->
In 1985, Carl Sagan was finishing a novel
called Contact.
His heroine had to cross
twenty-five light-years to the star Vega,
and come back.

<!-- S36 -->
In his draft, she flew into a black hole
and came out near Vega.
Sagan asked a friend,
the physicist Kip Thorne,
to check the physics.

<!-- S37 -->
Thorne's answer was that a black hole
wouldn't work.
Anyone who fell in
would never come out.
But there was another shape
in Einstein's equations.

<!-- S38 -->
Imagine two points on a sheet of paper.
The shortest way between them
is a straight line.

<!-- S39 -->
Now fold the paper,
so the two points touch.
Nothing moved faster.
The geometry changed.

<!-- S40 -->
That's a wormhole.
A tunnel through spacetime,
connecting two distant places.

<!-- S41 -->
Wormholes appear in some solutions
to Einstein's equations.
But the simplest ones collapse
faster than anything could get through.

<!-- S42 -->
So Thorne and his student, Michael Morris,
asked a different question.
Forget whether wormholes exist.
What would it take to keep one open,
wide enough for a person?

<!-- S43 -->
Their paper came out in 1988.
A serious physics paper,
which started with a question
about a novel.
The novel later became a film,
with Jodie Foster.

<!-- S44 -->
And the answer looked familiar.
The throat of the tunnel
would have to be held open
by exotic matter.
Negative energy again,
like the gap between those metal plates.

<!-- S45 -->
But not three protons' worth.
In the simplest models,
a throat just two metres wide
needs negative mass
roughly comparable to Jupiter.

<!-- S46 -->
Cleverer shapes need far less.
But every version anyone has found
still needs some.

<!-- S47 -->
In the novel, Sagan got around that.
The tunnels were built
by an ancient civilisation, long since gone.
We don't have that option.

<!-- S48 -->
And no traversable wormhole
has ever been observed.
We don't know if one could exist,
let alone how to build one.

<!-- S49 -->
So, what would we actually have to invent?
Three things, at least.

<!-- S50 -->
First, a way to reshape spacetime itself.
Not to move through it faster,
but to bend it,
on purpose and on demand.

<!-- S51 -->
Second, the energy to do it.
For the original warp drive
and for wormholes,
that means energy of a kind
we can barely make in a laboratory,
in amounts that, in some estimates,
dwarf everything in the universe.

<!-- S52 -->
And third, a reason to think it's allowed.
Relativity has passed every test
we've ever given it.

<!-- S53 -->
The clocks on GPS satellites
run fast by about
thirty-eight millionths of a second a day,
exactly as relativity predicts.
Without correcting for that,
GPS navigation would quickly
become unreliable.

<!-- S54 -->
That same theory is the one
that sets the speed limit.
And nothing in it hands us a way around it.

<!-- S55 -->
So faster-than-light travel
doesn't look like an engineering problem.
It looks like a physics problem.

<!-- S56 -->
We have two ideas,
the warp drive and the wormhole.
Neither one is a blueprint.
We don't know if either can be built.

<!-- S57 -->
But there's a problem
even deeper than energy,
or exotic matter,
or engineering.

<!-- S58 -->
In that same year, 1988,
Thorne and two colleagues showed
that a wormhole built for travel
could, in principle,
be turned into a time machine.

<!-- S59 -->
And if faster-than-light signals
obey the usual rules of special relativity,
they can be arranged to arrive
before they were sent.

<!-- S60 -->
So what if the universe doesn't just make
faster-than-light travel difficult?

<!-- S61 -->
What if it makes it incompatible
with cause and effect itself?

<!-- S62 -->
That's where we're going next.
