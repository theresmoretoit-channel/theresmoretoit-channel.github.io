# If Our Brains Could Do Telepathy

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine sitting in a room
with somebody else.

You say nothing.

You are not even looking at each other.

<!-- S02 -->
And yet you know exactly
what the other person is thinking.

Not because you can read their face.

Not because you know them well.

You simply know.

<!-- S03 -->
A thought passes
from one brain into another.

This is what we call telepathy.

<!-- S04 -->
And although people have talked about it
for hundreds of years,

nobody has ever reliably shown
that a human brain
can do anything of the kind.

<!-- S05 -->
But today we are going to do
something a little different.

We are not going to ask
whether telepathy exists.

<!-- S06 -->
We are going to ask something else.

What would have to be different

for telepathy to be
biologically possible?

<!-- S07 -->
And we may find that stronger
brain signals would not be enough.

Because telepathy
would not be one ability.

It would be an entire
communication system.

<!-- S08 -->
Our brain is not electrically dead.

Quite the opposite.

Every second, an enormous number
of electrical and chemical processes
are running inside it.

<!-- S09 -->
Neurons pass information
to each other

using electrical impulses
and chemical messengers.

<!-- S10 -->
And because electric currents
create electromagnetic fields,

the brain produces
electromagnetic activity too.

<!-- S11 -->
We can even measure it.

Electrodes resting on the scalp
pick up the rhythms of the cortex.

<!-- S12 -->
So there is a sensible question here.

Could one brain send information out,
and another brain receive it?

<!-- S13 -->
But this is where we run into
a fundamental problem.

The fact that something
is transmitting a signal

does not mean
that anybody can receive it.

<!-- S14 -->
Picture an ordinary radio.

A transmitter can broadcast
at, say, one hundred megahertz.

<!-- S15 -->
But if you put a rock beside it,
the rock will hear no music.

Not because the signal is not there.

Because the rock has no receiver.

<!-- S16 -->
And the idea of natural telepathy
has very much the same problem.

Even if we somehow amplified
the electromagnetic signals
of the brain,

we would still need
a biological receiver.

<!-- S17 -->
Something able to catch the signal.

And then something
just as important.

Something able to recognise
what the signal means.

<!-- S18 -->
This is perhaps the biggest problem
of the whole idea.

Suppose we could measure
a person's brain activity perfectly.

<!-- S19 -->
We see billions of neurons.

We see which regions are active.

We see the electrical signals.

<!-- S20 -->
And still none of that tells us
what the person is thinking.

Because a thought
is not a simple electrical signal.

<!-- S21 -->
When I think about an apple,

there is no single neuron in my brain
that says, apple.

Individual cells can be surprisingly
picky about what they respond to.

But not one of them
carries the whole thought.

<!-- S22 -->
It is an enormous combination
of neural activity.

It is tied to the shape of the apple.

Its colour.

Its taste.

Its smell.

<!-- S23 -->
Memories.

Language.

Emotions.

And above all,
the whole of my previous life.

And that is exactly why every brain
is a little bit different.

<!-- S24 -->
So if evolution wanted
to build telepathy,

it would have to solve something
much like the invention of language.

<!-- S25 -->
There would have to be structure.

Not a secret alphabet
where one pattern means danger

and another one means food.

<!-- S26 -->
Something looser than that.

A pattern that another brain
could learn to recognise.

Because a receiver does not have to
translate symbols one at a time.

<!-- S27 -->
Your eye does not turn photons
into some universal code
before you can see.

It is simply built,
and then trained,
to read whatever arrives.

<!-- S28 -->
Which opens a rather nice question.

Would two telepaths have to learn
how to speak to each other?

Or would part of that language
already be there at birth?

<!-- S29 -->
With ordinary animal signals,
it is usually both.

A vervet monkey is born already
knowing how its alarm calls sound.

But it has to learn
what each one of them is for.

<!-- S30 -->
And step by step,
a real language of the brain
could come into being.

And this is where evolution
becomes genuinely interesting.

<!-- S31 -->
Because a language does not have to be
built out of sound.

We humans use sound mainly because
we have vocal cords and ears.

<!-- S32 -->
Another species could use light.

Ultrasound.

Chemicals.

Electrical signals.

Or, in principle,
electromagnetic signals.

<!-- S33 -->
So let us imagine
a completely different creature.

Part of its brain is a structure
that works as a biological antenna.

<!-- S34 -->
Something like our ear,

except that instead of sound
it picks up electromagnetic signals.

And another part of the brain
works as a transmitter.

<!-- S35 -->
Suddenly we have a chain.

One brain transmits.

The signal crosses the space between.

The other brain receives.

<!-- S36 -->
That is no longer magic.

That is a communication system.

And if such a system
lasted long enough,

evolution could start improving it.

<!-- S37 -->
Better sensitivity.

Greater range.

Better filtering of noise.

Faster decoding.

And in the end,
perhaps very complex communication.

<!-- S38 -->
But why would any of this
evolve in the first place?

That is an important question.

Evolution has no plan.

<!-- S39 -->
It does not build abilities
because they would be interesting.

An ability spreads
when it improves the chances
of surviving or reproducing.

<!-- S40 -->
And telepathy could be
an enormous advantage.

Picture a group of hunters.

No need to shout.

No need to wave.

No need to look at each other at all.

<!-- S41 -->
The whole group could coordinate
almost instantly.

Or a parent could warn its young
about a predator,

without making a single sound.

<!-- S42 -->
Or you could talk in the dark.

Behind an obstacle.

In a dense forest.

The advantages could be huge.

<!-- S43 -->
Although the physics
would have something to say about it.

Take water.

<!-- S44 -->
Sea water conducts electricity,
and it swallows radio waves.

The higher the frequency,
the shorter the reach.

<!-- S45 -->
That radio from a moment ago
would be gone
within a few centimetres of sea.

Which is why our own submarines
listen on extremely low frequencies,

and why a three-letter message
could take a quarter of an hour.

<!-- S46 -->
So a species in the ocean
would not simply have telepathy.

It would have the kind
that its water allowed.

Slow. Short. Simple.

The medium would be shaping
the mind that used it.

<!-- S47 -->
And then comes the problem.

Picture a city
where everybody has telepathy.

And everybody is using it at once.

<!-- S48 -->
It would not be
a polite how are you today.

It would be a million brains
transmitting a million signals.

It would be complete chaos.

<!-- S49 -->
So a telepathic brain would need
something we already have ourselves.

A filter.

<!-- S50 -->
Our own brain does not consciously
take in everything either.

We do not notice every sound.

Every touch.

Every smell.

Every image.

<!-- S51 -->
The brain chooses
what to pay attention to.

There would have to be a mechanism
that says, this thought I want to send.

And at the same time,
this one I do not.

<!-- S52 -->
And underneath that one
sits a second problem.

You think about someone.

But how would your brain know
where to send the thought?

<!-- S53 -->
Radio has a frequency.

A telephone has a number.

The internet has an address.

A telepathic mind would need
something of the same kind.

<!-- S54 -->
So does every mind
carry a signature of its own?

A frequency?

A pattern that nobody else has?

<!-- S55 -->
Or would you have to do
something stranger than that,

and somehow tune yourself
to one particular mind?

<!-- S56 -->
Nature already does half of this.

The electric pulse of a knife fish
carries its species, its sex,

and quite probably
which fish it is.

An identity, written into the signal.

<!-- S57 -->
And here telepathy
starts to get frightening.

Because if evolution built telepathy,

it would probably have to build
something like telepathic privacy.

<!-- S58 -->
Imagine somebody
who can hear all your thoughts.

Not only the ones
you mean to say out loud.

<!-- S59 -->
But also whatever crosses your mind
for half a second.

Random associations.

Memories.

Doubts.

Fear.

Wishes.

<!-- S60 -->
Thoughts that do not mean
that you actually want them.

Suddenly it would matter enormously
to separate what I am thinking

from what I want to tell you.

<!-- S61 -->
And perhaps this is where
one of the most important abilities
of a telepathic brain would appear.

A mental firewall.

<!-- S62 -->
And science fiction
has already run this experiment.

Not the experiment
of how telepathy might work.

A different one.

What it would do to a society.

<!-- S63 -->
In the trilogy
Remembrance of Earth's Past,
by the writer Liu Cixin,

there is a species
called the Trisolarans.

They cannot conceal their thoughts
the way that people can.

<!-- S64 -->
For them, thinking something
and saying it
are not two different acts.

For a human
that is almost unimaginable.

<!-- S65 -->
We are used to an enormous gap
between what I think,

what I say,

and what I actually do.

<!-- S66 -->
Take that gap away,

and a great deal of what we build
on top of it goes with it.

Lying.

Bluffing.

Diplomacy.

Even keeping a secret to be kind.

<!-- S67 -->
And now an interesting question.

Suppose we built an implant
that reads the activity of your brain…

turns it into data…

<!-- S68 -->
sends that data across the internet…

and a second implant turns it back
into activity in another brain.

Is that telepathy?

<!-- S69 -->
Strictly speaking, no.

It is technological communication
between brains.

But for the person using it?

The difference might be
almost invisible.

<!-- S70 -->
You do not have to speak.

You do not have to type.

You do not have to look at a screen.

<!-- S71 -->
You simply think,
send me that photograph.

And the other person receives it.

<!-- S72 -->
And this may be
the more realistic road.

We do not have to wait for evolution
to grow a biological antenna.

Technology could do the same thing
a great deal faster.

<!-- S73 -->
The implant can be our antenna.

The computer can be the translator.

The internet can be the channel.

<!-- S74 -->
And a second implant can be
a loudspeaker
wired straight into a brain.

Suddenly we have something that,
from the point of view of the user,

begins to look a great deal
like telepathy.

<!-- S75 -->
But we would not have to send
whole thoughts.

We could send only a piece of one.

<!-- S76 -->
And that is exactly how it started.

Because a crude version of this
has already been done.

No implants. Just electrodes
and a magnetic coil.

<!-- S77 -->
More than ten years ago,
a person in India
imagined moving their hands.

Electrodes picked it up.

The signal travelled
across the internet.

<!-- S78 -->
And in France,
a magnetic pulse at the back
of somebody's head

made them see a flash of light.

A flash meant one.

No flash meant zero.

<!-- S79 -->
A four-letter word
took a hundred and forty of those.

The receiving brain saw flashes.

It never knew
that anything had been said.

The computer put the word together
afterwards.

<!-- S80 -->
Since then, three people have played
a shared game of falling blocks
that way, brain to brain.

One bit each, one turn at a time.

It is slow. It is clumsy.

But it is real.

<!-- S81 -->
And that is probably the road.

A signal.

Then a word.

Then an image.

Then a concept.

And perhaps, one day,
a whole mental state.

<!-- S82 -->
But let us go back to evolution.

Imagine a planet where life has used
this kind of communication
from the very beginning.

<!-- S83 -->
Animals with biological transmitters
and biological receivers.

Predators that hunt without a sound.

Prey that can warn the others.

<!-- S84 -->
Social groups
that can share what they know.

And slowly, an intelligent species.

<!-- S85 -->
Its first word was never spoken.

Because it never needed words.

Its language was not made of sound.

It was mental.

<!-- S86 -->
And its culture would have grown
in a completely different direction
from ours.

<!-- S87 -->
Perhaps there would be
no such thing as a secret.

Imagine a civilisation
where sharing your thoughts
is the natural thing to do.

<!-- S88 -->
Lying could be far harder.

But a different kind of manipulation
would appear instead.

<!-- S89 -->
Because even if you cannot
hide a thought…

you may still be able to shape
how the other one reads it.

<!-- S90 -->
So there would be
telepathic propaganda.

Telepathic advertising.

Telepathic art.

Perhaps even telepathic war.

<!-- S91 -->
And of course,
a telepathic password.

Because if somebody
can read your mind,

you need a way of deciding
who you actually let in.

<!-- S92 -->
So what would we really need?

If we wanted genuine
biological telepathy,

we would need at least six things.

<!-- S93 -->
First, a transmitter.

The brain would have to make a signal
that carries information.

<!-- S94 -->
Second, a receiver.

Another brain would have to catch it.

<!-- S95 -->
Third, structure.

The signal would have to be
something a brain could learn.

<!-- S96 -->
Fourth, a decoder.

The brain would have to recognise
what it was reading.

<!-- S97 -->
Fifth, a filter.

It would have to decide
what to send and what to keep.

<!-- S98 -->
And sixth, an address.

It would have to know
which mind it was talking to.

<!-- S99 -->
And that is not
a simple change to one gene.

That is practically
a whole new sense.

<!-- S100 -->
So if we discovered tomorrow
that human brains transmit

far stronger electromagnetic signals,

that would not automatically mean
that we had discovered telepathy.

<!-- S101 -->
It would only mean
that we have a transmitter.

We would still need a receiver.

We would need structure and decoding.

We would need a way to aim it.

<!-- S102 -->
And above all we would need a way

to turn the electrical activity
of one brain

into something that another brain
genuinely recognises as a thought.

<!-- S103 -->
And that is exactly why
the idea of telepathy
is so fascinating.

<!-- S104 -->
Because real telepathy
would not be a stronger brain wave.

It would not even be
one single ability.

It would be a whole system.

<!-- S105 -->
And in the end,
a new way of perceiving the world.

Just as we have sight,
hearing, smell and touch,

we would have one more sense.

Another mind.

<!-- S106 -->
And perhaps the most interesting
question begins right there.

If we could one day link human brains
with technology,

would we still be individuals?

<!-- S107 -->
Or would we slowly start turning
into something

that we cannot even name today?
