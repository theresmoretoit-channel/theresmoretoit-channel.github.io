<!-- S01 -->
People say that exercise is good for you.

<!-- S02 -->
But have you ever stopped to ask why?
Why does your body actually need you to move?

<!-- S03 -->
We do not have to walk to get water.
We do not have to go looking for food.
We do not have to walk miles to work.
We do not even have to stand up
to change the channel.

<!-- S04 -->
And yet doctors keep saying
the same thing.
Move more.

<!-- S05 -->
So what exactly goes wrong when we don't?

<!-- S06 -->
In 1966, five healthy young men
agreed to an experiment.
They were twenty years old.

<!-- S07 -->
For three weeks, they stayed in bed.
Not ill. Not injured.
Just horizontal.

<!-- S08 -->
Then the researchers measured
how much oxygen their bodies
could use at full effort.

<!-- S09 -->
It had fallen by twenty-seven per cent.
In three weeks.

<!-- S10 -->
The heart was the main reason.
Every beat was moving
about a quarter less blood
than it had three weeks earlier.

<!-- S11 -->
And then the study did something
almost no study does.
It waited.

<!-- S12 -->
The same five men came back
thirty years later.
They were fifty now.
Three decades of ordinary ageing
had cost them twelve per cent.

<!-- S13 -->
Three weeks in bed at twenty
had done more damage
than thirty years of getting older.

<!-- S14 -->
That number is really the whole video.
Because it tells you
what kind of thing a body is.

<!-- S15 -->
A body is not a lump of matter
that holds its shape
until something breaks it.

<!-- S16 -->
It is a system
that keeps asking one question.
How much am I being asked to do?

<!-- S17 -->
Muscle is the obvious case.
Stop loading it,
and it does not simply stay as it was.
It gets taken apart.

<!-- S18 -->
In bed rest studies,
leg muscle can go
at roughly half a per cent a day.

<!-- S19 -->
Bone does the same thing, more slowly.
Astronauts in orbit lose bone density
at something like
one to two per cent a month.

<!-- S20 -->
And here is the detail
that gives the game away.
They do not lose it evenly.

<!-- S21 -->
The arms, which never carried
their own weight anyway,
lose much less of it.
The hips and the spine,
which used to, lose much more.

<!-- S22 -->
The body is not decaying.
It is measuring.
And it is measuring
where the load actually goes.

<!-- S23 -->
And you do not need
a hospital bed or a spacecraft
to watch this happen.

<!-- S24 -->
Ten healthy young men
were asked to do one thing.
Walk less.

<!-- S25 -->
They went from
about ten thousand steps a day
to roughly thirteen hundred.
For two weeks.

<!-- S26 -->
By the end of it,
their muscles had become
measurably worse at pulling sugar
out of the blood.

<!-- S27 -->
About a sixth worse,
on the measure the researchers used.
Their fitness had dropped as well,
and so had the lean mass in their legs.

<!-- S28 -->
Nobody was ill.
Nobody was in bed.
They had simply stopped
walking to things.

<!-- S29 -->
So why would a body be built like that?

<!-- S30 -->
Because for almost all of our history,
there was no way to opt out.

<!-- S31 -->
If they wanted food,
they had to get to it.
If they wanted to be somewhere else,
they walked.

<!-- S32 -->
They carried things.
They worked with their hands.
They walked, ran and climbed.

<!-- S33 -->
And this is the part
worth holding on to.
Movement was not exercise.
It was just what a day looked like.

<!-- S34 -->
And we can put a number on that.
The Hadza are a hunting
and gathering people in Tanzania.
Forty-six of them
wore heart rate monitors.

<!-- S35 -->
They spent about two and a quarter hours
of every day
at moderate to vigorous effort.

<!-- S36 -->
The health guidelines
most countries publish
ask for a hundred and fifty minutes of that.
Per week.

<!-- S37 -->
The Hadza pass the weekly target
in two days.

<!-- S38 -->
Meanwhile, a study that counted steps
on seven hundred thousand phones
put the global average
at about five thousand a day.

<!-- S39 -->
Now think about an ordinary working day.

<!-- S40 -->
You get up.
You sit in the car.
You sit at work.
You sit at a screen.

<!-- S41 -->
You sit down for lunch.
You sit in the car going home.
You sit in front of another screen
in the evening.

<!-- S42 -->
Not one moment of it told your body
that it needed to be capable of anything.

<!-- S43 -->
Now, this is the point
where this kind of story
usually goes wrong.

<!-- S44 -->
Because it is very easy to turn it into:
everything old was good,
everything new is bad.

<!-- S45 -->
It was not.
Hunter-gatherers were not healthier
at everything.
Infections, injuries and childbirth
were dangerous
in ways they are not for us.

<!-- S46 -->
And there is one result
that complicates the story even further.

<!-- S47 -->
When researchers measured
how much energy the Hadza
actually burn in a day,
it came out about the same as ours.

<!-- S48 -->
Same body size, same daily total.
All that walking
did not show up as a bigger number.

<!-- S49 -->
So this is not a story about burning more.
The body seems to spend
a similar budget either way.
It is a story about
what it is being asked to do with it.

<!-- S50 -->
Which brings up the strangest part.
If movement matters that much,
why does it not feel like it?

<!-- S51 -->
You know when you are hungry.
You know when you are thirsty.
You know when you have not slept.

<!-- S52 -->
All of those come with an alarm,
because in each case
the trouble arrives within days.

<!-- S53 -->
There is no equivalent alarm
for not moving.
What your body does instead
is quietly lower the target.

<!-- S54 -->
Capacity you are not using
is expensive to keep.
So it is let go.

<!-- S55 -->
Which means everything still feels fine.
Because fine is now measured
against a day of sitting down.

<!-- S56 -->
You only find out
when you ask for something
you have not asked for in years.
Two flights of stairs. A run for a bus.

<!-- S57 -->
And then there is the second half of it,
which is almost unfair.

<!-- S58 -->
We did not only evolve to move.
We evolved to avoid moving
when we did not have to.

<!-- S59 -->
Energy was scarce.
Anything you spent walking for no reason
was energy not spent on staying alive.

<!-- S60 -->
So the voice that says
not today
is not laziness that arrived with the sofa.
It is an old instinct
that used to be right.

<!-- S61 -->
Now for a question
that is pure speculation.
And it is worth saying so out loud.

<!-- S62 -->
What if sitting had always been normal?
Not for decades.
Not for a few generations.
But for hundreds of thousands of years.

<!-- S63 -->
Food always within reach.
Transport everywhere.
Survival depending on
how well you handled information.

<!-- S64 -->
Given that much time,
evolution would have something
to work with.

<!-- S65 -->
Maybe the muscle would go.
Maybe the skeleton
would be built lighter.
Maybe the whole way we store
and spend energy would shift.

<!-- S66 -->
We do not know
what that human would look like.
Anyone who tells you they do is guessing.

<!-- S67 -->
But the guess is not the point.
The point is the timescale.
Our surroundings changed
in about three generations.
Evolution does not work at that speed.

<!-- S68 -->
So we ended up with bodies
shaped by a world
where movement was unavoidable,
living in a world
where it is optional.

<!-- S69 -->
And then we act surprised
that we have to go to a gym,
or run, or ride a bike,
just to keep the thing working.

<!-- S70 -->
Which is why the sentence
exercise leads to health
is slightly the wrong shape.

<!-- S71 -->
When you go for a run,
you may not be adding a bonus
on top of a normal body.

<!-- S72 -->
You may be handing your body back
the one input it was built around.

<!-- S73 -->
So perhaps the question was never
why is exercise healthy?

<!-- S74 -->
Perhaps it is:
why would we expect
a body built for movement
to work properly without it?

<!-- S75 -->
Movement is not something
we added to the human body.
Movement is part of the human body.

<!-- S76 -->
And exercise is good for you
might just be a long way of saying:
this is what normal feels like.
