<!-- S01 -->
Imagine someone alive
five thousand years ago.

<!-- S02 -->
No electricity.
No books.
No schools.

<!-- S03 -->
By our standards,
an extraordinarily simple life.

<!-- S04 -->
So you might expect
an extraordinarily simple language.
Something like: I hunt. You hunt.
Tomorrow we hunt.

<!-- S05 -->
That is not what we find.

<!-- S06 -->
Latin, Old English, Old Norse.
All of them had grammatical machinery
that modern English has mostly lost.

<!-- S07 -->
And you don't need an ancient language
to see how odd this gets.
Modern English will do.

<!-- S08 -->
The verb is "go".
The past tense is "went".

<!-- S09 -->
Why isn't it "goed"?

<!-- S10 -->
Because "went" never belonged to "go".
It was the past tense of a different verb.

<!-- S11 -->
"Wend". To turn, to make your way.
You can still wend your way home.

<!-- S12 -->
Two verbs. Two meanings.
And at some point English quietly took
the past tense of one of them
and handed it to the other.

<!-- S13 -->
"Go" lost its own past.
"Wend" grew a tidy new one: "wended".

<!-- S13b -->
Today several hundred million people
simply learn it: go, went, gone.
Nobody stops to think about the history
in the middle of a sentence.

<!-- S14 -->
Nobody voted on that.
Nobody wrote it down as a rule.
It happened, and then it stayed.

<!-- S15 -->
Which is this entire video,
hiding inside one irregular verb.

<!-- S16 -->
Because the first mistake we make
is treating language as technology.

<!-- S17 -->
When we meet a complicated machine,
we assume a simpler one came first.

<!-- S18 -->
The earliest computers were crude.
Later ones were not.
Every version adds capability.

<!-- S19 -->
So it's tempting to tell
the same story about speech.
Primitive sounds first.
Grammar bolted on afterwards.

<!-- S20 -->
But nobody designed English.
Nobody designed Latin.

<!-- S21 -->
There was no committee sitting around
three thousand years ago, agreeing
that the language needed more endings.

<!-- S22 -->
Languages don't get released.
They get used.

<!-- S23 -->
And every generation alters them
slightly, without meaning to.
Sounds shift. Words drift.
Endings wear down.

<!-- S24 -->
Nobody is in charge of any of it.

<!-- S25 -->
Which is also how one language
turns into many.

<!-- S26 -->
Take a population that all speaks
the same way, and split it.
Half of them move somewhere else.

<!-- S27 -->
At first, nothing happens.
They still speak the same language.

<!-- S28 -->
Then the small changes begin.
A sound shifts on one side
of the mountain and not the other.

<!-- S29 -->
A word picks up a new meaning here
and keeps the old one there.
An ending survives in one group
and quietly dies in the second.

<!-- S30 -->
And because the two groups
are no longer talking,
none of it ever gets compared.

<!-- S31 -->
After a few generations,
you notice an accent.

<!-- S32 -->
After a few hundred years,
you notice the grammar.

<!-- S33 -->
After a thousand,
you may need a translator.

<!-- S34 -->
And at no point did anyone
decide to start a new language.

<!-- S35 -->
So if languages are always moving,
why don't they simply wear down
into something easy?

<!-- S36 -->
Sometimes they do.
English is the usual example.

<!-- S37 -->
Old English had grammatical gender.
Its nouns changed their endings
depending on the job they were doing
in the sentence.

<!-- S38 -->
Almost all of that is gone.
A modern English noun
barely changes at all.

<!-- S39 -->
Which really is simplification.
In that corner of the grammar.

<!-- S40 -->
But the information didn't evaporate.
It moved.

<!-- S41 -->
"The dog bit the man."

<!-- S42 -->
"The man bit the dog."

<!-- S43 -->
Same words. Same endings.
Entirely different afternoon.

<!-- S44 -->
Old English had more freedom
in word order, because the endings
carried information about
who did what to whom.

<!-- S45 -->
Modern English has far less of it.
Word order is holding that job now,
and it never gets a day off.

<!-- S46 -->
Dropping one mechanism
and leaning harder on another
is not the same as getting simpler.

<!-- S46b -->
And English didn't only lose things.
While the endings were wearing away,
it was assembling something else:
a small army of helper verbs.

<!-- S46c -->
"I go." "I am going."
"I have gone." "I had been going."
"I am going to go."

<!-- S46d -->
Modern English does that work
with auxiliary verbs,
where Old English leaned on endings.
The endings left through one door,
and the auxiliaries walked in
through another.

<!-- S47 -->
Complexity rarely disappears.
It moves house.

<!-- S47b -->
By the way, if you'd like to see
where the next question takes us,
consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S48 -->
So how would you even decide
that one language is simpler
than another?

<!-- S49 -->
There is no dial somewhere reading:
language complexity,
seventy-three per cent.

<!-- S49b -->
Look at the range humans have produced.
English. Arabic. Mandarin.
Hindi. Japanese. Swahili.

<!-- S50 -->
A language can have punishing
verb endings and a word order
you could set your watch by.

<!-- S51 -->
Another can have almost no endings
and a syntax that forgives nothing.

<!-- S52 -->
One may have a plain tense system
and an elaborate way of marking
how you know what you are claiming.

<!-- S52b -->
And some will pack into a single word
what another language would spread
across half a sentence.

<!-- S53 -->
Whatever you measure,
you are measuring one room
of a very large house.

<!-- S54 -->
Which brings us back
to our ancient human.

<!-- S55 -->
Latin nouns had a set of case endings.
Six of them. Or five, or seven,
depending on which grammar book
you happen to open.

<!-- S56 -->
And when linguists compare the
descendants and work backwards,
the ancestor they arrive at
looks elaborate too.

<!-- S57 -->
Proto-Indo-European.
Nobody has ever found a text in it.

<!-- S58 -->
It is a reconstruction, not a record.
Assembled by comparing the languages
that came out of it.

<!-- S59 -->
So why would anyone that long ago
need grammar like that?

<!-- S60 -->
Because a society without technology
is not a society without complications.

<!-- S61 -->
Imagine having to say:
"The man you saw by the river
yesterday took my spear."

<!-- S62 -->
Who.

<!-- S62b -->
Which man.

<!-- S62c -->
When.

<!-- S62d -->
Where.

<!-- S62e -->
Whose spear.

<!-- S63 -->
And a question that some languages
oblige you to answer every time:
did you see it happen,
or did somebody tell you?

<!-- S63b -->
Get that wrong in some languages
and you have not been impolite.
You have been ungrammatical.

<!-- S64 -->
Language is not mainly a tool
for describing technology.
It is a tool for handling other people.

<!-- S65 -->
And people were never simple.
We make promises. We tell stories.
We negotiate. We remember. We gossip.
We lie.

<!-- S66 -->
No smartphone required.

<!-- S67 -->
All of which makes language look
much less like engineering,
and much more like evolution.

<!-- S68 -->
Evolution doesn't design anything.
It edits whatever is already there.

<!-- S69 -->
It keeps the compromises.
It carries the leftovers.
It is stuck with decisions made
by creatures that died long ago.

<!-- S70 -->
Languages behave the same way.
They inherit structures, bend them,
mislay a few, and keep the strange
ones entirely by accident.

<!-- S71 -->
"Went" is a leftover.
Silent letters are leftovers.
Irregular verbs are a museum
that every child has to memorise.

<!-- S72 -->
And that leads to the part
people find hardest to accept.

<!-- S73 -->
There is no direction.

<!-- S74 -->
No rule says a language
ought to get simpler.
No rule says it ought to get richer.

<!-- S75 -->
The Slavic languages show it neatly.
Most of them kept a rich case system.

<!-- S76 -->
Bulgarian lost the old one on its nouns
almost completely, and grew something
else instead: a definite article
stuck on the end of the word.

<!-- S77 -->
Same ancestor.
Opposite directions.
Neither one is the upgrade.

<!-- S77b -->
And the same thing happened
right across the world.

<!-- S77c -->
People separate.
Languages drift.

<!-- S77d -->
Sometimes they meet again
and start borrowing from each other.

<!-- S77e -->
It was never a ladder.
It is a tree, and it is still growing.

<!-- S78 -->
Latin was not complicated because
the Romans had discovered
a superior way to communicate.

<!-- S79 -->
And modern English is not simpler
because we are cleverer.

<!-- S80 -->
They are different outcomes
of different histories.

<!-- S81 -->
So perhaps the question
was wrong from the start.

<!-- S82 -->
Instead of asking why some languages
are so complicated, we could ask
why we ever expected a simple one.

<!-- S83 -->
Language was never designed.
No plan. No final version.
No committee tidying up the mess.

<!-- S84 -->
It only has to work well enough
for people to understand each other.
Everything after that is history.

<!-- S88 -->
Language doesn't evolve
towards perfection.
It evolves towards
whatever people keep using.
