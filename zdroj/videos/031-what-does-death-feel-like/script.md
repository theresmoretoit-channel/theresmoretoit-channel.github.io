<!-- S01 -->
Have you ever wondered
what it will feel like
when you die?

<!-- S02 -->
Will there be darkness?
Silence?
Will you somehow remain conscious,
floating in nothingness?
Will you wait?

<!-- S03 -->
And if you believe in an afterlife,
perhaps you imagine
waking up somewhere else.

<!-- S04 -->
Every one of those answers
sounds like a different thing.
But they all have
the same thing hidden inside them.
Let's take them one at a time.

<!-- S05 -->
Darkness.
That's usually the first answer.
But darkness is something you see.

<!-- S06 -->
Shut your eyes right now
and you still get darkness.
A dim, shifting grey,
delivered by a visual system
that is still running.

<!-- S07 -->
Silence, then.
But silence is something you hear.

<!-- S08 -->
An empty room?
An empty room
still has someone standing in it.
And an endless wait?
Waiting requires
someone who is waiting.

<!-- S09 -->
There it is.
Every one of those answers
quietly puts someone
into the picture.

<!-- S10 -->
Someone who sees the dark.
Someone who hears the silence.
Someone who waits.

<!-- S11 -->
So before we ask
what death feels like,
we have to ask
a much harder question.
What happens to that someone?

<!-- S12 -->
Here is the answer
most people reach for next.
"After I die, I'll sleep forever."

<!-- S13 -->
But eternal sleep
would still imply a sleeper.
And sleep has a feature
that death doesn't.
You wake up.

<!-- S14 -->
Imagine falling asleep tonight.
Then imagine that, somehow,
eight hours simply disappear
from your subjective experience.

<!-- S15 -->
You don't experience
eight hours of nothing.
You experience one moment
before sleep...
and then another moment
when you wake up.

<!-- S16 -->
That gap isn't an experience.
It's a missing one.

<!-- S17 -->
All right. Try it directly.
Try to imagine nothing.
Really nothing.

<!-- S18 -->
Most of us immediately
picture a black screen.
But that's not nothing.
There is a screen.
There is darkness.

<!-- S19 -->
And, most importantly,
there is you, looking at it.

<!-- S20 -->
Try again.
Imagine an empty universe.
Nothing in it at all.
But who is observing it?

<!-- S21 -->
Our brains are simply not very good
at simulating their own absence.
And there is a good reason for that.

<!-- S22 -->
Your conscious experience
normally has a very strong
sense of continuity.
There was a moment ago.
There is now.
And there will be
a moment after this one.

<!-- S23 -->
Memory gives you a past.
Perception gives you the present.
Prediction gives you a future.
And that is what gives you the feeling
of being one continuous person.

<!-- S24 -->
Neuroscientists have a phrase for it.
Your brain is a prediction machine.
It is always running a model
of what comes next.

<!-- S25 -->
So when you try to imagine
your own death,
it does what it always does.
It builds a sequence.

<!-- S26 -->
Me. Then death.
Then time. Then more time.
Then more time.

<!-- S27 -->
But there is a problem.
After the end of consciousness,
there is nobody there
to experience that sequence.

<!-- S28 -->
Your brain keeps adding time
because that's what brains do.
It is trying to simulate a future
in which the simulator
itself no longer exists.

<!-- S29 -->
There is one situation
that gets close to this,
and millions of people
go through it every year.
General anaesthesia.

<!-- S30 -->
The brain isn't simply switched off.
Parts of it stay active,
still processing information,
while conscious experience is gone.

<!-- S31 -->
You can be unconscious for six hours.
Then you open your eyes.
And subjectively?

<!-- S32 -->
There isn't a six-hour-long
experience of darkness.
There is no memory
of an empty black room.
It feels more like this.

<!-- S33 -->
Before... and after.

<!-- S34 -->
And somebody actually counted.
Britain gives around three million
general anaesthetics a year.

<!-- S35 -->
About one patient in twenty thousand
reports being aware.

<!-- S36 -->
Of course, anaesthesia isn't death.
It is temporary and controlled.
And most importantly,
anaesthesia has an awakening.

<!-- S37 -->
Death doesn't.

<!-- S38 -->
But it gives us an intuition
for something
very difficult to imagine.
Subjective time can disappear.

<!-- S39 -->
So let's go back
to the simplest possible
version of death.
Your brain stops functioning.

<!-- S40 -->
Cut the blood supply,
and consciousness can be lost
within about ten seconds.

<!-- S41 -->
The processes that create
perception, memory and thought stop.
The integration that produces
conscious experience is gone.

<!-- S42 -->
And that is what
every one of those answers missed.

<!-- S43 -->
Death is not a state you experience.
It is the absence of experience.

<!-- S44 -->
Here's a slightly morbid
way of putting it.
Imagine your brain
as a gigantic computer.

<!-- S45 -->
Processing units.
Memory.
And a huge network
letting the parts talk to each other.

<!-- S46 -->
The conscious experience isn't a file
sitting somewhere on the hard drive.
It's the running system.

<!-- S47 -->
With death, the hardware itself fails.
The connections are destroyed.
The processes stop.
And eventually there isn't even
a computer left
to display a black screen.

<!-- S48 -->
Death isn't a computer
showing a black screen forever.
It's the computer being gone.

<!-- S49 -->
And we have no reliable evidence
for a consciousness that continues
independently of the brain.
Not yet, at least.

<!-- S50 -->
Which raises a much better question.
If there is nothing there to feel,
why does it frighten us so much?

<!-- S51 -->
Part of the answer
is hiding in our own language.
We say this.
"I will be dead forever."

<!-- S52 -->
But notice what the sentence does.
It puts you into an enormous timeline,
as a person who remains there,
experiencing the passage of time
while being dead.

<!-- S53 -->
The universe can continue
for billions or trillions of years.
None of it reaches someone
whose consciousness no longer exists.

<!-- S54 -->
There is no experience of ten years.
There is no experience
of ten billion years.
There is simply no further experience.

<!-- S55 -->
And here is the strangest part.
You have already been through this.

<!-- S56 -->
Think about the time
before you were born.
You don't remember it.
Not because your memory is damaged.
Not because you forgot.

<!-- S57 -->
You simply weren't there.

<!-- S58 -->
You didn't spend billions of years
waiting to be born.
You didn't experience darkness.
You weren't bored.
You weren't lonely.

<!-- S59 -->
You weren't thinking this.
"When is this going to end?"
There was no you
to experience any of it.

<!-- S60 -->
And that argument is not new.
A Roman poet wrote it down
about two thousand years ago.

<!-- S61 -->
Lucretius asked his readers
to look back
at the eternity before their birth,
and to notice
that it had cost them nothing.

<!-- S62 -->
So why does the eternity
on the other side
feel so completely different?

<!-- S63 -->
Because the past is finished,
and the future is the thing
our brain is constantly
trying to control.

<!-- S64 -->
So losing it feels like losing
something that already belongs to us.
But it doesn't.
There is no future experience
waiting for you after death.

<!-- S65 -->
There is one more distinction,
and it may be
the most useful one here.

<!-- S66 -->
When people say this.
"I'm afraid of death."
They may actually mean this.
"I'm afraid of dying."

<!-- S67 -->
Those are not the same thing.

<!-- S68 -->
Dying can involve pain,
fear,
confusion,
loss of control,
separation from people you love,
or simply the knowledge
that you're about to lose
everything familiar.

<!-- S69 -->
Those are real experiences.
And medicine can sometimes
do a great deal to reduce them.

<!-- S70 -->
But being dead is different.
Once consciousness has ended,
there is no longer
anyone experiencing pain.
There is no fear.
No boredom.
No loneliness.
No waiting.

<!-- S71 -->
A conscious brain can even imagine
"Nothing will happen to me."

<!-- S72 -->
But it struggles with
"There will be no me
to whom anything happens."

<!-- S73 -->
So the brain quietly puts itself
back into the picture.
An observer.
A black room.
A silent universe.
An endless passage of time.

<!-- S74 -->
And we end up
with exactly what death isn't.
An experience.

<!-- S75 -->
There is something almost elegant
about the symmetry.
Before your existence began,
there was no subjective time.
Then suddenly, there was
a first moment you could experience.

<!-- S76 -->
And eventually,
there will be a last one.
After that, the universe continues.
But your timeline doesn't.
There is simply no further point
on it.

<!-- S77 -->
Which quietly changes the question.
Instead of asking
"What will I experience after death?"

<!-- S78 -->
the more useful question might be
"What am I experiencing now?"

<!-- S79 -->
Because this part is real.
This moment exists.
The people around you exist.
The things you enjoy exist.
The things you still want to see,
learn, build and experience exist.

<!-- S80 -->
There may be no second run.
No backup.
No save game.
No loading screen after the end.

<!-- S81 -->
And that can sound frightening.
But it can also make
the one thing we actually have,
this life,
matter enormously.

<!-- S82 -->
Perhaps the strangest thing
about death is not
that the lights go out.
It is that there is
nobody left to notice.

<!-- S83 -->
We are trying to imagine the absence
of the person doing the imagining.
And that may simply be something
consciousness cannot do.
