# How Did Voyager Send Pictures Home?
**Script v0.4**

<!-- S01 -->
In August 1989,
a spacecraft flew past Neptune,
more than four billion kilometres
from Earth,
and took pictures of it.

<!-- S02 -->
Its camera had no memory card,
and no sensor like the one in your phone.
It was built around a television tube,
designed in the 1970s.

<!-- S03 -->
And yet the pictures arrived.
Thousands of them.
So how do you send a photograph home
from the edge of the Solar System?

<!-- S04 -->
To answer that, we have to go back
twelve years before Voyager's launch,
to the first close-up pictures
of another planet.

<!-- S05 -->
In July 1965,
a small probe called Mariner 4
flew past Mars
and took twenty-two pictures.

<!-- S06 -->
Its camera was a vidicon,
a kind of television camera tube.
It didn't produce a photo.
It produced a signal,
rising and falling
as the picture was scanned line by line.

<!-- S07 -->
But the probe's radio was far too weak
to send a live television signal.
So it did something clever.
It chopped every picture
into two hundred by two hundred dots.

<!-- S08 -->
The brightness of each dot
became a number from zero to sixty-three.
Six bits per dot.

<!-- S09 -->
The numbers went onto a tape recorder,
and then home by radio,
at eight and a third bits per second.
At that speed,
one picture took about eight hours.

<!-- S10 -->
And when the numbers reached Earth,
the engineers at JPL
didn't want to wait for the computers.
They printed the raw numbers
on long strips of paper,

<!-- S11 -->
laid them side by side,
and coloured them in by hand with pastels,
like a paint-by-numbers kit.
The first close-up image of Mars
was a drawing made from data.

<!-- S12 -->
And that's the chain this story is about.
A picture becomes a signal.
The signal becomes numbers.
The numbers travel by radio.
And on Earth, they become a picture again.

<!-- S13 -->
So was Mariner 4 a digital camera?
Not really.
Its camera was analogue.
Everything after the camera was digital.

<!-- S14 -->
A digital camera in the modern sense
came ten years later,
and not in space.
In a lab at Kodak.

<!-- S15 -->
In December 1975,
an engineer called Steven Sasson
built a camera around a new kind of chip,
a CCD.

<!-- S16 -->
A CCD isn't scanned by an electron beam.
It's a grid of tiny light sensors,
each measuring its own patch of light.
The grid is built into the chip itself.

<!-- S17 -->
Sasson's grid was
a hundred by a hundred.
Black and white.
Recording one picture onto a cassette tape
took twenty-three seconds.

<!-- S18 -->
Kodak never sold it.
But it answers a question
people often ask.
Did we have digital cameras in the 1970s?
Yes. In a lab.

<!-- S19 -->
Voyager's cameras, though,
belonged to the other branch.
The one that started with Mariner.

<!-- S19b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.
It really helps the channel.

<!-- S20 -->
Voyager 1 and Voyager 2
were launched in 1977.
Each carried two cameras.
A wide-angle one for big scenes,
and a narrow-angle one for detail.

<!-- S21 -->
Both were slow-scan vidicons,
modified versions of the cameras
that had flown on earlier Mariners.

<!-- S22 -->
Light from a planet
left a pattern of charge
on a thin layer of selenium and sulphur.
That layer could hold the image
for more than a minute and a half.

<!-- S23 -->
Then an electron beam swept across it.
Eight hundred lines,
and eight hundred points on each line.

<!-- S24 -->
Reading out one frame
took forty-eight seconds.
Each point became a number
from zero to two hundred and fifty-five.
Eight bits.

<!-- S25 -->
That's sixteen times as many dots
as Mariner 4,
and four times as many shades.
More than five million bits
in a single picture.

<!-- S26 -->
By today's standards, that's tiny.
About six hundred and forty kilobytes
of raw image data,
less than one photo from your phone.
And the radio link added extra bits
to protect it from noise.

<!-- S27 -->
But taking the picture
was never the hard part.
Getting it home was.

<!-- S28 -->
In 1979, at Jupiter,
Voyager could send a hundred
and fifteen thousand bits per second.
One picture in under a minute.

<!-- S29 -->
When Earth was out of view,
or the link was busy,
pictures went onto a tape recorder,
with room for about a hundred of them.

<!-- S30 -->
But every time the distance doubles,
the signal reaching Earth
gets four times weaker.

<!-- S31 -->
At Neptune, Voyager 2 would be
about six times as far from Earth
as it had been at Jupiter.
Without changes, it would manage
around three thousand bits per second.

<!-- S32 -->
At that rate, a single picture
would take almost half an hour.

<!-- S33 -->
So the engineers improved both ends.
On Earth, the largest dishes
of NASA's Deep Space Network
were rebuilt, from sixty-four metres
to seventy.

<!-- S34 -->
And for the Neptune flyby,
they listened together
with other radio telescopes,
including the twenty-seven dishes
of the Very Large Array in New Mexico.

<!-- S35 -->
The other end was harder.
Voyager 2 was already beyond Saturn,
and nobody could touch it.
So they changed its software.

<!-- S36 -->
Voyager's computers were tiny.
Its flight data computer
ran about eighty thousand instructions
a second.
Your phone manages billions.

<!-- S37 -->
Engineers on Earth wrote a new program
and uploaded it to the backup computer.
From then on, Voyager 2 didn't send
the brightness of every dot.

<!-- S38 -->
It sent how much each dot differed
from the one next to it.
In dark space and smooth clouds,
those differences are small numbers,
and small numbers need fewer bits.

<!-- S39 -->
A typical picture shrank by sixty percent.
And a new error-correcting code
cut down the extra bits
that protected it from noise.

<!-- S40 -->
At Neptune, in 1989,
Voyager 2 sent more than
twenty thousand bits per second.
Almost seven times
what it would have managed.
A picture in about a minute and a half.

<!-- S41 -->
So, did we have digital cameras
in the 1970s?
In a lab at Kodak, yes.
But the cameras that photographed
Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune
were television tubes.

<!-- S42 -->
What made them digital
was turning that signal into numbers.
The numbers. The tape. The radio.
And software that could be rewritten
from billions of kilometres away.

<!-- S43 -->
And the most famous picture
those cameras ever took
wasn't of a planet they visited.

<!-- S44 -->
By 1990, Voyager 1 had passed
Jupiter and Saturn,
and was heading out of the Solar System.
Its planetary work was done.

<!-- S45 -->
Back in 1981, the astronomer Carl Sagan,
a member of the imaging team,
had proposed one last picture.
Turn the cameras around,
and look back at home.

<!-- S46 -->
On the 14th of February 1990,
Voyager 1 took sixty frames.
A portrait of the Solar System,
from beyond Neptune.

<!-- S47 -->
Six planets made it into the frames.
Venus, Earth, Jupiter,
Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.

<!-- S48 -->
Voyager was about
six billion kilometres away.
From there, Earth covered
about an eighth of a single pixel.

<!-- S49 -->
The streak of light around it
isn't something out in space.
It's sunlight, scattered inside the camera.

<!-- S50 -->
Three exposures through colour filters
were combined into the image
we now call the Pale Blue Dot.
Every person who has ever lived
lived somewhere inside it.

<!-- S51 -->
Thirty-four minutes after
the pictures of Earth were taken,
Voyager 1's cameras were switched off,
to save power.
They were never turned on again.

<!-- S52 -->
The pictures waited on the tape recorder.
The last of the data reached Earth
on the 1st of May 1990.
Almost eleven weeks later.

<!-- S53 -->
A television tube.
Eight bits per dot.
A transmitter of about twenty watts.

<!-- S54 -->
And a family portrait
that took eleven weeks to come home.
