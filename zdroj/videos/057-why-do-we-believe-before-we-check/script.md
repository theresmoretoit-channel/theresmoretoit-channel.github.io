<!-- S01 -->
You see a headline.
You get angry.
You share it.

<!-- S02 -->
And only later do you ask yourself
whether it was actually true.
Why?
Why do we believe before we check?

<!-- S03 -->
It would be comforting to blame
the internet for that.
It isn't the internet. It starts
with something your brain does
before the headline even lands.

<!-- S04 -->
Imagine hearing a noise in the bushes.
You could stop and work out
the probability that it wants to eat you.

<!-- S05 -->
Or you could run.
Evolution never waited for the results.
If there was a predator,
the one who ran survived.

<!-- S06 -->
If there wasn't, almost nothing was lost.
Fear was cheap.
Being wrong about fear was expensive.

<!-- S07 -->
So we ended up with a brain
that reacts first and checks afterwards.
Only the bushes have changed.

<!-- S08 -->
Instead of a tiger, a headline.
Instead of thirty people in a valley,
a few billion, all within reach.

<!-- S09 -->
And that last change
does something the first one doesn't.
One person becomes frightened.
Their neighbour sees it.

<!-- S10 -->
"What's going on?"
Now the neighbour is nervous too.
And somebody else
sees the nervous people.

<!-- S11 -->
Within a few minutes,
a street full of people is afraid.
Notice what nobody in that street got.
New information.

<!-- S12 -->
They saw other people being afraid,
and that was enough.
It works the other way just as well.
Excitement spreads. So does certainty.

<!-- S13 -->
A crowd doesn't only panic.
It can also decide together
that something is going to be wonderful.
And the more people believe it,
the more convincing it looks.

<!-- S14 -->
Now hold that thought,
and think about an election.
We like to picture it
as a rational process.

<!-- S15 -->
Several parties, each with a programme.
The voter studies the proposals,
weighs up the consequences,
and picks the best one.

<!-- S16 -->
How many voters
have ever finished a whole programme?
How many check the arithmetic
behind the promises?

<!-- S17 -->
And how many simply think:
"I like her." "I trust him."
"That one seems confident."
"I can't stand that man."

<!-- S18 -->
In 2005, a group of researchers
tested how far that goes.
They showed people photographs
of two candidates
they had never seen before.

<!-- S19 -->
No names. No parties. No policies.
One second of looking,
and one question:
which of these two faces
looks more competent?

<!-- S20 -->
Those snap judgements picked the winner
in roughly seven out of ten
American Senate races.

<!-- S21 -->
One second. A face. Nothing else.
We don't even need to hear someone speak
before we start judging them.

<!-- S22 -->
Which means the same sentence,
delivered calmly and steadily...
...or delivered nervously,
is not really the same sentence.

<!-- S23 -->
The information can be identical.
Our reaction isn't.
And now put that machinery
in front of something built to use it.

<!-- S24 -->
We could say that people
believe nonsense because they're stupid.
That explanation is far too easy.
A false story doesn't have to win
by having better arguments.

<!-- S25 -->
It can win by reaching
the right feeling first.
Somebody is frightened?
They are coming for what you have.

<!-- S26 -->
Somebody is angry?
Here is exactly who did it to you.
Somebody feels ignored?
Everyone lies to you.
We are the only ones who don't.

<!-- S27 -->
And a story that lands on a feeling
gets clicked, and shared,
and put in front of more people.
Which pays whoever published it.
So more of it gets made.

<!-- S28 -->
So that's our answer, isn't it.
Emotions are faster than reason,
and the modern world knows it.

<!-- S28b -->
But wait.

<!-- S29 -->
If our feelings push us around
this easily,
what are we supposed to do about it?
Stop trusting our own judgement?

<!-- S30 -->
Check everything ourselves?
That sounds right
until you try it exactly once.

<!-- S31 -->
Pick one claim about the economy.
Just one.
Find the original data.
Find out who collected it, and how.

<!-- S32 -->
Understand the statistics well enough
to know what that number cannot say.
Find the studies that came out
the other way.

<!-- S33 -->
And then work out
whether the people who study this
actually disagree with each other,
or only sound as if they do.

<!-- S34 -->
That is one claim.
Now do medicine. Energy. Law.
Climate. History. Finance.
Nobody has that many lifetimes.

<!-- S35 -->
So the real question
was never how to check everything.
It's how to decide who to trust
when we can't.

<!-- S36 -->
And needing to trust somebody
is not a weakness.
When your car stops working,
you don't become a mechanic first.

<!-- S37 -->
When the power goes out,
you don't sit down
and study the national grid.
You rely on somebody
who has spent years on it.

<!-- S38 -->
Which is why the sentence
"I don't trust scientists"
deserves a proper answer,
and not an eye roll.

<!-- S39 -->
Because the worry behind it is fair.
Scientists are not infallible.
Studies get overturned.

<!-- S40 -->
Whole fields have been wrong
for decades at a time.
And when better evidence arrives,
the answer changes.

<!-- S41 -->
That isn't science failing.
That is the only thing
that makes it worth anything.

<!-- S42 -->
It also tells you
what we are really trusting.
Not a person who cannot be wrong.
A process that is built
to catch the people who are.

<!-- S43 -->
But watch what usually happens next.
Somebody says
they don't trust the experts...
...and then believes a video instead.

<!-- S44 -->
No evidence was examined.
No field was studied.
No better source was found.
One authority was simply swapped
for a different one.

<!-- S45 -->
Usually for the one
that says what we hoped to hear.
That isn't critical thinking.

<!-- S46 -->
Critical thinking never meant
"I believe nothing."
It means
"I want a good reason to believe it."

<!-- S47 -->
And here is the useful part.
You usually can't judge the claim.
But you can nearly always
judge the way it is being made.

<!-- S48 -->
Does the source show you
where the information came from?
Does it correct itself
when it turns out to be wrong?

<!-- S49 -->
Does it tell you
what would change its mind?
Does it ever say
"this part we don't know yet"?

<!-- S50 -->
Try it on the next thing
that crosses your screen.
A new study proves something.
You can't evaluate the study.

<!-- S51 -->
But you can ask whether anybody
has shown you the study at all,
or only a screenshot of a number
with nobody's name on it.

<!-- S52 -->
And a source that has never once
told you it got something wrong
isn't more reliable than the others.
It just isn't checking.

<!-- S53 -->
None of those questions
require you to be an expert.
They only require you to look.

<!-- S54 -->
It isn't proof.
It is a far better bet
than how somebody made you feel.

<!-- S54b -->
By the way, if you'd like to see
where the next question takes us,
consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S55 -->
And now the uncomfortable part.
You might expect that being clever
protects you from all of this.

<!-- S55b -->
It doesn't.

<!-- S56 -->
Researchers gave people a genuinely
difficult maths problem about some data.
For half of them,
the data was about a skin cream.

<!-- S57 -->
For the other half,
exactly the same numbers
were about a law on guns.

<!-- S58 -->
On the skin cream,
the people who were best with numbers
did best. Obviously.
On the gun law, they didn't.

<!-- S59 -->
They split along political lines
harder than everybody else did.

<!-- S60 -->
Being good at reasoning
had not made them better
at finding the truth.
It made them better at defending
what they already thought.

<!-- S61 -->
That is the trap.
Not stupidity.
Skill, pointed the wrong way.

<!-- S62 -->
It is also why "do your own research"
can leave you worse off
than when you started.
You go looking...
and you find what you hoped to find.

<!-- S63 -->
And notice how easy it is
to enjoy that sentence.
Those people.
The ones who fall for propaganda.
The ones who share nonsense.

<!-- S63b -->
Same brain.

<!-- S64 -->
We also have opinions
we would prefer to be true.
We also have people
we believe automatically.

<!-- S65 -->
We can also be furious at a headline
before we have reached the end of it.

<!-- S66 -->
So perhaps the question isn't
whether people are rational
or emotional. We are obviously both.

<!-- S67 -->
We built mathematics and rockets.
And a single moment of fear
can still move us in under a second.

<!-- S68 -->
Which leaves one small habit
that is worth the effort.
When something makes you feel
strongly and immediately...

<!-- S69 -->
...notice that the feeling arrived first.
Then ask who is telling you this.
And how they could possibly know.

<!-- S70 -->
And then the hardest one of all.
Do I have a reason to believe this,
or do I just want to?

<!-- S71 -->
It costs about ten seconds.
And sometimes,
ten seconds is enough.

<!-- S72 -->
Because "I don't know"
is a complete answer.
It is a much better answer than
"I know, because I believe it."
