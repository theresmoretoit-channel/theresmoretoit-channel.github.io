# Why Is It So Hard to Quit Some Games?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Maybe you know this feeling.

You come home from work
in the evening.

And you are tired.

You don't really feel like doing
anything complicated.

<!-- S02 -->
So you turn on your computer.

And you start a game.

Not because you desperately
want to play.

It's just the easiest thing to do.

<!-- S03 -->
You already know the game.

You know how everything works.

You don't have to learn anything new.

You don't have to figure out
what to play.

<!-- S04 -->
You just log in.

And then you start doing things.

Daily quests.

Collecting rewards.

Levelling your character.

Upgrading your equipment.

<!-- S05 -->
Farming resources.

Completing achievements.

And, most importantly,
moving progress bars.

<!-- S06 -->
And suddenly,
three hours have passed.

And you ask yourself:

Did I actually have fun?

<!-- S07 -->
Maybe.

Maybe not.

Because sometimes you realise
that you spent the entire evening
doing things you didn't
really want to do.

<!-- S08 -->
You did them because they were there.

Because they were unfinished.

Because they were part
of your routine.

Because if you didn't do them today,
you would lose something.

<!-- S09 -->
And this raises
an interesting question:

Why do we keep playing games
even when we're no longer enjoying
the actual playing?

<!-- S10 -->
Traditional games have
a fairly simple structure.

You start the game.

You play through it.

And eventually, you finish it.

<!-- S11 -->
There is an ending.

You can play it again if you want,

but there is still a point
where you can say:

I'm done.

<!-- S12 -->
Online games can work
very differently.

They don't really have an ending.

Instead, they give you
an enormous number
of smaller goals.

<!-- S13 -->
Levels.

Gear.

Collections.

Achievements.

Skills.

Reputation.

Ranks.

Quests.

Seasonal rewards.

<!-- S14 -->
And when you finish one thing,
there is another one waiting.

And another.

And another.

You never really reach the end.

<!-- S15 -->
And there's nothing
inherently wrong with that.

Progress is a fantastic
game mechanic.

Starting at zero
and gradually getting better
feels good.

<!-- S16 -->
Getting a new ability feels good.

Completing a collection feels good.

Finally defeating a boss
you couldn't defeat before
feels good.

<!-- S17 -->
The problem begins somewhere else.

It begins when you stop
doing something
because you enjoy doing it...

and start doing it
because it's unfinished.

<!-- S18 -->
Imagine a progress bar
at seventy-three per cent.

What does that number actually mean?

Nothing particularly important.

<!-- S19 -->
But your brain immediately sees:

Twenty-seven per cent is missing.

And suddenly there is
something to fix.

<!-- S20 -->
You play a little more.

Seventy-three becomes eighty-two.

Eighty-two becomes ninety-four.

And finally:

One hundred per cent.

Done.

<!-- S21 -->
And that feels good.

But if the game is designed
around permanent progression,
something else happens immediately.

<!-- S22 -->
Another progress bar appears.

Empty.

And behind it, more.

And the whole thing starts again.

<!-- S23 -->
We often talk about rewards.

Experience points.

Loot.

Virtual currency.

Cosmetics.

<!-- S24 -->
But a reward is only one
of the things pulling at you.

There is something quieter
sitting next to it:

unfinished things.

<!-- S25 -->
When you leave something half-done,
it often pulls you back.

An unfinished task at work.

An unread message.

A half-finished project.

<!-- S26 -->
On its own,
that pull is fairly weak.

You put down a half-read book
and the world carries on.

A game doesn't leave it on its own.

<!-- S27 -->
It puts the unfinished thing
next to a reward.

Next to a deadline.

Next to a routine
you already have.

And then it shows you
a number.

<!-- S28 -->
It doesn't have to tell you:

Keep playing.

It can simply show you:

Eighty-seven per cent.

And suddenly you want
to make it one hundred.

<!-- S29 -->
This creates a strange situation.

You stop asking:

What do I want to do tonight?

And start asking:

What am I supposed to do tonight?

<!-- S30 -->
That's a very important difference.

Because now you're not necessarily
playing the game.

You're maintaining it.

<!-- S31 -->
You're doing your dailies.

You're keeping up
with the current meta.

You're maintaining your character.

You're collecting resources.

<!-- S32 -->
You're making sure
you don't fall behind.

The game has become something
you have to take care of.

<!-- S33 -->
But I want to finish everything.

Of course you do.

That's the trap.

Some games are built around goals
that can never really be finished.

<!-- S34 -->
You can collect everything
that exists today.

And tomorrow there is a new update.

New items.

New achievements.

New levels.

New systems.

New rewards.

<!-- S35 -->
So if your goal is
one hundred per cent completion,
the game can give you
an impossible task:

Finish everything
in a world that keeps expanding.

And you keep trying.

<!-- S36 -->
So how do you actually stop?

The obvious answer is:

Just use more self-control.

I don't think
that's very good advice.

<!-- S37 -->
Because when you're tired after work,
you're already dealing with
the worst possible combination.

<!-- S38 -->
Low energy.

A familiar habit.

And a game that gives you
an immediate reward.

<!-- S39 -->
Telling yourself:

I'm not going to play tonight.

can be surprisingly difficult.

<!-- S40 -->
So instead of relying
entirely on willpower,
you can change the environment
around the habit.

<!-- S41 -->
The important moment isn't
necessarily two hours
into the gaming session.

It's the moment when you decide
what to do after work.

<!-- S42 -->
If your routine is:

come home,
sit at the computer,
launch the game,

then you are already playing
before you've really made a decision.

<!-- S43 -->
So make the first step
less automatic.

Remove the shortcut.

Turn off notifications.

Log out.

<!-- S44 -->
Don't let the game
constantly remind you
that something is waiting for you.

And, most importantly,
prepare alternatives.

Not one alternative.

Several.

<!-- S45 -->
A game you've already played.

A short game.

A book.

A movie.

Something you've been meaning to do.

<!-- S46 -->
The goal isn't to find
a new perfect hobby.

The goal is simply to make sure
that the familiar game
isn't the only effortless option.

<!-- S47 -->
If you really want to know
how much a game
has taken over your routine,
sometimes it helps
to stop completely for a while.

<!-- S48 -->
Not as a punishment.

Not as a permanent decision.

Just an experiment.

Try two weeks.

And pay attention to what happens.

<!-- S49 -->
The first few days may feel strange.

You may feel that
something is missing.

But eventually you may discover
something important:

<!-- S50 -->
You don't actually miss the game.

You miss the routine.

And those are not the same thing.

<!-- S51 -->
Live-service games
are very good at creating the feeling
that something is happening right now.

<!-- S52 -->
A reward expires.

An event ends.

A season is almost over.

A daily bonus is waiting.

If you don't log in today,
you'll miss something.

<!-- S53 -->
But ask yourself:

What exactly am I going to lose?

Some virtual currency?

An item?

A daily reward?

A position on a leaderboard?

<!-- S54 -->
And what do I get in exchange
for not logging in?

Time.

Time to do something else.

<!-- S55 -->
Because the real cost of these games
isn't necessarily
what you spend inside the game.

It may be what you don't
spend your time on outside it.

<!-- S56 -->
Learn to leave things unfinished.

This may be the hardest part.

Leave the progress bar
at seventy-three per cent.

<!-- S57 -->
Leave the achievement unfinished.

Leave the collection incomplete.

Leave the quest unfinished.

Log out.

And see what happens.

<!-- S58 -->
Probably nothing.

The world doesn't end.

Your character doesn't disappear.

The unfinished thing
simply remains unfinished.

And you are somewhere else.

<!-- S59 -->
If unfinished progress
makes you uncomfortable,
one way to weaken that feeling
is to sometimes let something
remain unfinished on purpose.

<!-- S60 -->
You don't have to win
against the game.

You just have to prove to yourself
that you can walk away.

<!-- S61 -->
Before you continue
doing something in the game,
ask yourself:

If there were no progress for this,
would I still want to do it?

<!-- S62 -->
No experience points.

No loot.

No achievement.

No increase in a percentage.

Would I still want to do it?

<!-- S63 -->
If the answer is yes:

Great.

You're playing.

If the answer is no:

Maybe you're not playing anymore.

Maybe you're just completing.

<!-- S64 -->
None of this means
that games are bad.

And it doesn't mean
that progress is bad.

Progress is one of the reasons
games are fun.

<!-- S65 -->
Collecting things can be fun.

Getting better can be fun.

Completing difficult goals
can be incredibly satisfying.

<!-- S66 -->
The problem isn't:

I want to finish something.

The problem is:

I can't stop even though
I don't want to continue.

<!-- S67 -->
That's the line
worth paying attention to.

And maybe the solution
isn't to stop playing games.

Maybe it's to learn
how to play them differently.

<!-- S68 -->
Sometimes you can play
towards a goal.

Sometimes you can chase
a difficult achievement.

And sometimes you can simply
wander around
and do something completely pointless.

<!-- S69 -->
No progress.

No optimisation.

No reward.

Just because it's fun.

<!-- S70 -->
Because perhaps that's something
we forget when games become
systems of permanent progression.

We forget what it feels like
to play a game
without having to accomplish anything.

<!-- S71 -->
You don't have to reach
one hundred per cent.

You don't have to collect everything.

You don't have to claim every reward.

<!-- S72 -->
You don't have to finish every quest.

You don't have to keep up
with every update.

And you don't have to play every day.

<!-- S73 -->
A game can offer you
something valuable.

But you don't have to give it
all of your free time in return.

<!-- S74 -->
Because in the end,
the most important question isn't:

What is left to complete?

It's:

What do I actually want to do?

<!-- S75 -->
And sometimes the best way
to win a game...

is simply to turn it off.
