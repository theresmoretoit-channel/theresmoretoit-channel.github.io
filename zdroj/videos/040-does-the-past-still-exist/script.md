# Does the Past Still Exist?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
What happened yesterday?

You woke up.

You had breakfast.

You talked to someone.

Maybe you went to work.

<!-- S02 -->
And now?

None of those things
are happening anymore.

But they happened.

You know they happened.

<!-- S03 -->
So here is a strange question.

Where is yesterday now?

Is it somewhere in the universe?

Did it simply disappear?

<!-- S04 -->
Or does the past still exist,
in some sense,

even though we can no longer
experience it?

<!-- S05 -->
Before we can answer that,
we need a much smaller question first.

What does it mean
for something to exist?

<!-- S06 -->
Suppose you have a photograph
of yourself from twenty years ago.

The photograph exists.

It is sitting on your computer
right now.

<!-- S07 -->
But the person in that photograph?

That particular moment of your life
is not happening anymore.

<!-- S08 -->
The photograph is not the past.

It is an object in the present,
carrying information about the past.

And once you notice that,
you start seeing it everywhere.

<!-- S09 -->
A fossil is not the dinosaur.

Ash is not the tree that burned.

A memory in your head
is not yesterday.

<!-- S10 -->
They are all traces.

Marks left behind
by something that happened.

<!-- S11 -->
And traces are thin.

Ash tells you that there was a tree.

It does not tell you
which tree,

or how tall it stood,
or what the light looked like
underneath it.

<!-- S12 -->
Most of what happens
leaves nothing at all.

<!-- S13 -->
What colour was the sky
at three o'clock last Tuesday?

How many steps did you take
the day before that?

<!-- S14 -->
Both of those were real events.

They had a definite answer.

And now there may be no trace
of it anywhere.

<!-- S15 -->
Which gives us
an uncomfortable possibility.

Maybe the past does not exist.

Maybe all that exists
is the small amount
it happened to leave behind.

<!-- S16 -->
Look at the Sun.

What you see is not the Sun
as it is right now.

You see the Sun
as it was about eight minutes ago.

<!-- S17 -->
That is simply how long
its light takes to reach us.

And it gets much more extreme
than that.

<!-- S18 -->
When you look at a galaxy
a billion light years away,

you are seeing it
as it was a billion years ago.

<!-- S19 -->
Not a picture of it.

Not a recording of it.

That is the actual light,
arriving today,

after a journey
of a billion years.

<!-- S20 -->
So astronomy is not really
the study of distant things.

It is the study of old things.

The farther away we look,
the deeper into the past we see.

<!-- S21 -->
The screen in front of you
is a few nanoseconds in the past.

The far wall of the room,
a little further back than that.

<!-- S22 -->
You have never once
seen anything in the present.

Everything you have ever seen
is a record of something
that already happened.

<!-- S23 -->
So the universe is full
of information about its own past.

<!-- S24 -->
But look carefully
at what that information is.

It is light arriving today.

A signal in the present.

<!-- S25 -->
And information about the past
is still not the past itself.

<!-- S26 -->
Now there is a much bigger problem.

If light takes time to travel,

what does right now even mean
for the whole universe?

<!-- S27 -->
What is happening right now
on a planet
a thousand light years away?

Relativity gives us
no single answer.

<!-- S28 -->
Two observers moving differently
can disagree about which distant events
happen at the same time.

Neither of them is wrong.

Neither of them
is measuring badly.

<!-- S29 -->
There is no single universal now
shared by the entire universe.

<!-- S30 -->
And that is awkward for us.

Because if there is no universal present,

what exactly separates
the past from it?

<!-- S31 -->
One possible answer
is called the block universe.

<!-- S32 -->
Imagine a film.

Watching it, you get the story
one frame at a time.

Beginning.

Middle.

End.

<!-- S33 -->
But the film itself
is a complete strip of frames.

The ending does not come into existence
at the moment you reach it.

It was there the whole time.

<!-- S34 -->
The block universe
is a little like that.

Instead of a universe
being built moment by moment,

picture the whole of its history
as one four-dimensional structure.

<!-- S35 -->
Past events are part of it.

Present events are part of it.

Future events are part of it.

Nothing has to move through it.

The structure simply is.

<!-- S36 -->
On that picture, your childhood
is not something that vanished.

It is a different part of spacetime.

<!-- S37 -->
Which is a strange kind of comfort.

And also a strange kind of threat.

Because it would mean
that tomorrow's version of you
is already there.

<!-- S38 -->
But be careful here.

This is one possibility.

Relativity makes it attractive.

Relativity does not prove it.

<!-- S39 -->
Because there are other answers,
and philosophers have names for them.

<!-- S40 -->
The first one is presentism.

Only the present exists.

The past is gone.

The future has not happened yet.

<!-- S41 -->
It matches how everything feels.

But it has to answer a hard question.

If observers cannot agree
on what is happening now,

what exactly is the present?

<!-- S42 -->
The second one is eternalism.

Past, present and future
all exist.

Our sense of now is just
where we happen to be standing.

<!-- S43 -->
That fits naturally
with relativity.

But it has to explain
why we only ever experience
one thin slice of it,

and always in the same direction.

<!-- S44 -->
The third one sits between them.

It is called the growing block.

<!-- S45 -->
The past exists.

The present exists.

The future does not.

Reality is a block
that keeps getting bigger.

<!-- S46 -->
Which sounds reasonable,
until you ask where its edge is.

And relativity gives us
no universal way to define it.

<!-- S47 -->
Notice what physics has done here.

It did not settle the argument.

It made the argument harder.

<!-- S48 -->
Relativity makes a single universal now
very difficult to defend.

But relativity does not tell us
what the word exist means.

That part is still philosophy.

<!-- S49 -->
And it is worth asking honestly.

Suppose we say
that yesterday's breakfast exists.

What are we actually claiming?

<!-- S50 -->
Is it somewhere in space?

Can we go there?

Can we measure it?

Can anything about it still change?

<!-- S51 -->
Not in any way
we can access.

The only thing we can point at
is information available today.

<!-- S52 -->
Which takes us straight back
to the photograph.

<!-- S53 -->
The photo is not the event.

The memory is not the event.

The light from a galaxy
is not the galaxy's past.

And yet every one of them
points at something
that really did happen.

<!-- S54 -->
So here is a question
that pulls apart two ideas

we normally keep glued together.

<!-- S55 -->
Imagine that you wake up tomorrow
and every record of yesterday is gone.

<!-- S56 -->
No photographs.

No documents.

No memory of it, in any head.

No physical trace anywhere,
down to the last atom.

<!-- S57 -->
Would yesterday
have stopped existing?

<!-- S58 -->
Or would it still have happened,

even though nothing in the universe
remembered that it did?

<!-- S59 -->
And yet we already assume this,
quietly, all the time.

<!-- S60 -->
Somebody in a Bronze Age village
had a conversation one evening
and then went to bed.

Nothing recorded a word of it.

<!-- S61 -->
We do not conclude
that the conversation never took place.

We conclude
that we cannot reach it.

<!-- S62 -->
And that is two different things.

<!-- S63 -->
Whether the past exists.

And whether there is any evidence
that it does.

<!-- S64 -->
Those are not the same question.

And almost everything
that feels obvious about time

quietly assumes that they are.

<!-- S65 -->
So.

Does the past still exist?

<!-- S66 -->
Maybe not.

Maybe only the present is real,
and the universe
keeps making new moments.

<!-- S67 -->
Maybe it does.

Maybe the whole history of everything
sits there in spacetime,

and today is simply
where we are standing.

<!-- S68 -->
Or maybe the difference
between gone and still there

does not work
the way we think it does.

<!-- S69 -->
We don't know.

<!-- S70 -->
But there is something
oddly reassuring
about one of those answers.

<!-- S71 -->
Maybe the things that happened to us
do not have to keep happening
in order to be real.

<!-- S72 -->
They happened.

<!-- S73 -->
And perhaps that is enough.
