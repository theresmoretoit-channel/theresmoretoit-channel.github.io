<!-- S01 -->
You press play.
A cassette starts to squeal,
and five minutes later,
a game appears on the television.

<!-- S02 -->
In an earlier video,
we took that sound apart, bit by bit.
And it ended with a strange claim:

<!-- S03 -->
that the world's data centres
still keep their archives on tape.

<!-- S04 -->
That sounds like nostalgia.
It isn't.

<!-- S05 -->
In February 2011,
around forty thousand Gmail users
opened their inbox
and found it empty.

<!-- S06 -->
A software update at Google
had a bug in it.

<!-- S07 -->
Google keeps several copies
of every mailbox,
in different data centres.
The bug reached those too.

<!-- S08 -->
Every message was recovered.
It took hours instead of milliseconds,
because it came back from tape.

<!-- S09 -->
And Google isn't unusual.
CERN, the particle physics lab,
has stored an exabyte of data
from the Large Hadron Collider.

<!-- S10 -->
That's a million terabytes.
Most of it sits
on about sixty thousand tapes.

<!-- S11 -->
So here's the question.
We have hard drives, SSDs and the cloud.

<!-- S12 -->
Why would the people with the most data
still trust a strip of magnetic tape?

<!-- S13 -->
Tape is actually older than the hard drive.
In 1951, the UNIVAC I computer
stored its data on reels of metal tape.
The first hard drive came five years later.

<!-- S14 -->
The tape they use today
isn't a music cassette.
It's called LTO,
short for Linear Tape-Open.

<!-- S15 -->
HP, IBM and Seagate
developed it in the late 1990s,
as an open format
that anyone could build drives for.

<!-- S16 -->
The first cartridge, in 2000,
held one hundred gigabytes.

<!-- S17 -->
The newest generation, LTO-10,
holds up to forty terabytes.
Four hundred times as much,
in a box of the same size.

<!-- S18 -->
The box often says one hundred.
That number assumes your data
can be squeezed two and a half times.
Photos and video are already compressed,
so forty is the honest figure.

<!-- S19 -->
You can hold it in one hand,
and it holds more than
eight hundred million games
for a forty-eight kilobyte Spectrum.

<!-- S20 -->
The drive writes four hundred megabytes
every second.
Even so, filling one cartridge
takes more than a day.

<!-- S21 -->
A ZX Spectrum loaded
about fourteen hundred bits a second.
This drive writes more than three billion.
Over two million times faster.

<!-- S22 -->
How does all of that fit on tape?

<!-- S23 -->
First, length.
Inside is a single reel
with more than a kilometre of tape.

<!-- S24 -->
It's half an inch wide,
and many times thinner
than a human hair.

<!-- S25 -->
Then, tracks.
The drive doesn't write
one stripe along the tape.
It writes more than fifteen thousand.

<!-- S26 -->
Each one is less than
a thousandth of a millimetre wide.

<!-- S27 -->
The head writes thirty-two at once,
runs to the end of the tape,
shifts over by a hair's breadth,
and comes back the other way.

<!-- S28 -->
Hundreds of passes,
back and forth,
over the same kilometre.

<!-- S29 -->
Now, the problem.
Tape is flexible.
It stretches,
and it wanders sideways as it moves.

<!-- S30 -->
At these widths,
drifting by a single track
means hitting the wrong data.

<!-- S31 -->
So the tape carries its own guide rails.
At the factory,
servo patterns are written onto it
before it ever holds a single file.

<!-- S32 -->
The drive follows those patterns
the whole time,
steering the head
to stay on its track.

<!-- S33 -->
In LTO-10,
the head even tilts slightly,
to make up for the tape
shrinking and swelling
with heat and humidity.

<!-- S34 -->
The coating changed too.
Instead of the iron oxide
of old cassettes,
modern tape uses tiny,
carefully shaped particles
of barium ferrite.

<!-- S35 -->
All of this sounds fragile.
A kilometre of plastic,
racing past a head.

<!-- S36 -->
But the drive checks itself as it writes.
Just behind the write head
sits a read head,
checking every block a moment later.
If it doesn't come back right,
the drive writes it again
further along.

<!-- S37 -->
On top of that,
every block carries error-correcting codes,
so the drive can rebuild the data
even when part of it is damaged.

<!-- S37b -->
Rebuilding data you can't read
sounds impossible.
It deserves a video of its own.

<!-- S38 -->
The LTO-9 specification allows
one unreadable bit
in every twelve exabytes.

<!-- S39 -->
That's about twelve times
everything CERN has stored,
for a single error.

<!-- S40 -->
But the real advantage
is something tape doesn't do.
It doesn't stay connected.

<!-- S41 -->
For real protection,
a written cartridge is taken out
of the library altogether
and put on a shelf.

<!-- S42 -->
No power. No network.
Nothing that a bug, or an attacker,
can reach through a cable.

<!-- S43 -->
That's what saved Gmail in 2011.
The bug reached the copies
that were online.
The tapes weren't.

<!-- S44 -->
Today, the same idea
protects against ransomware.
Malware can encrypt every disk
in a building.

<!-- S45 -->
It can't encrypt
a cartridge sitting on a shelf.

<!-- S46 -->
Some cartridges can be written only once.
After that, nobody can erase them,
not even the person
who runs the system.

<!-- S47 -->
And sitting on a shelf costs nothing.
Large disk archives often keep
thousands of drives spinning,
using power and making heat.
A cartridge on a shelf
uses no power at all.

<!-- S48 -->
Makers rate the cartridges
for around thirty years,
if they're stored properly.

<!-- S48b -->
Tape is also cheap.
A thirty-terabyte cartridge
costs around two hundred and sixty dollars.
That's under ten dollars a terabyte.

<!-- S48c -->
A large new hard drive
costs around forty dollars a terabyte,
more than four times as much.

<!-- S48d -->
The catch is the drive itself.
A new LTO-10 drive costs
more than twenty thousand dollars.
So tape pays off
when one drive serves
thousands of cartridges.

<!-- S49 -->
So why don't we use tape for everything?
Even if we ignore the price,
tape still has one big weakness.

<!-- S50 -->
It can't jump straight to your data.
A hard drive moves its head
to any file
in a few thousandths of a second.

<!-- S51 -->
Tape has to travel.
If your file is at the far end
of a kilometre of tape,
the drive has to spool all the way there.

<!-- S51b -->
It's just like rewinding a cassette
to find the right song,
or the right game.

<!-- S52 -->
Finding a file
can take around a minute.
Thousands of times slower
than a hard drive.

<!-- S53 -->
That rules tape out
for running a computer,
a database,
or millions of tiny files.

<!-- S54 -->
But it's perfect for the opposite job.
Huge amounts of data,
written once,
and rarely needed again.

<!-- S55 -->
The cartridges sit in tape libraries:
cabinets the length of a room,
with thousands of slots inside.

<!-- S56 -->
A robot runs along a rail,
picks out the right cartridge,
and slides it into a drive.

<!-- S57 -->
At Lawrence Livermore,
a laboratory in California,
one library has
nearly twenty thousand slots,
and room for about
three hundred petabytes.

<!-- S57b -->
That's three hundred million gigabytes.

<!-- S58 -->
To the software,
it all looks like one enormous,
rather slow disk.

<!-- S59 -->
For decades,
that was the whole story.
Tape was the quiet basement
of the data centre.

<!-- S60 -->
And then, something changed.
Artificial intelligence.

<!-- S61 -->
Training a large AI model
takes enormous collections
of text, images and video.

<!-- S62 -->
Once it's collected,
that data is worth keeping.
But most of it
isn't needed every day.

<!-- S63 -->
That's exactly the kind of data
tape was made for.

<!-- S64 -->
In October 2026, Fujifilm,
one of the biggest makers of tape,
announced it would spend
about ten billion yen

<!-- S65 -->
so it could make roughly five times
as much data tape
within three years.

<!-- S66 -->
The reason it gave?
The spread of generative AI.

<!-- S67 -->
The thing we used to wait for
in front of the television
is growing again,
because of the newest technology we have.

<!-- S68 -->
And tape still has room to grow.
Its bits are packed roughly
a hundred times less densely
than on the newest hard drives.

<!-- S69 -->
In 2020, IBM and Fujifilm
showed a test tape in the lab
that could fit five hundred and eighty
terabytes on a single cartridge.
It isn't a product yet.
But it shows how far tape can go.

<!-- S70 -->
There's one catch, though.
And it's a strange one.

<!-- S71 -->
The tape lasts for decades.
The drives don't.

<!-- S72 -->
For years, a new LTO drive
could still handle tapes
two generations old.
From LTO-8, only one.

<!-- S73 -->
And an LTO-10 drive
can't read any of them.
Not a single older cartridge.

<!-- S73b -->
Why break it?
Remember the guide rails
written at the factory?
LTO-10 changed their pattern,
so the head could follow
even thinner tracks.

<!-- S73c -->
Older tapes carry the old pattern,
and staying compatible
would have cost capacity.

<!-- S73d -->
So what if a drive breaks?
Not much.
LTO is an open format,
so any LTO-10 drive, from any maker,
reads the same cartridge.

<!-- S73e -->
And the drives are built for hard work.
An LTO-9 drive is rated
for eighty thousand cartridge loads.
In a library, a worn-out drive
is simply swapped for a new one.

<!-- S73f -->
Older drives haven't vanished either.
LTO-9 drives are still on sale,
and they read LTO-8.
For anything older,
there are used drives,
and companies that rescue
old tapes for a living.

<!-- S74 -->
So archives don't just keep their tapes.
Every few years,
they copy everything onto new ones.

<!-- S74b -->
So why rate them for thirty years?
Because the tape is never
the reason to move.
Archives copy on their own schedule,
and if a move runs late,
the data is still safe on the shelf.

<!-- S75 -->
The tape can outlive
every machine that knows how to play it.

<!-- S76 -->
Which brings us back to that cassette.
A strip of tape,
a head,
and patterns of magnetism.

<!-- S77 -->
More than forty years later,
the idea hasn't changed.
Only the numbers and the technology have.

<!-- S78 -->
Somewhere right now,
a robot is pulling a cartridge
off a shelf,
pushing it into a drive,
and pressing play.
