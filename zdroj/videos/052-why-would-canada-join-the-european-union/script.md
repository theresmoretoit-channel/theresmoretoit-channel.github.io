<!-- S01 -->
Have you heard?
Canada wants to join the European Union.

<!-- S02 -->
What a nonsense.
How is Canada supposed to move to Europe?

<!-- S03 -->
Except that in 2026,
the president of the European Commission
stood up in the European Parliament
and proposed more or less exactly that.

<!-- S04 -->
Not as a joke.
As policy.

<!-- S05 -->
Not full membership.
Something that does not exist yet.
Canada as the Union's first
associate member.

<!-- S06 -->
And here is the part
most people miss.
Canada is already halfway in.

<!-- S07 -->
In February 2026,
Canada joined something called SAFE.

<!-- S08 -->
That is the Union's joint scheme
for arming itself.
A hundred and fifty billion euros
of borrowed money,
spent on weapons together.

<!-- S09 -->
Canada is the first country
outside Europe ever allowed in.

<!-- S10 -->
That did not come out of nowhere.
The summer before,
Canada and the Union signed
a security and defence partnership.

<!-- S11 -->
In 2024, Canada joined Horizon Europe,
the Union's research programme.
Canadian labs now apply
for European money.

<!-- S12 -->
And since 2017, goods have crossed
the Atlantic under a joint
trade agreement.

<!-- S13 -->
So the question was never
whether Canada can work with Europe.
It already does.

<!-- S14 -->
The question is how far this goes.

<!-- S15 -->
And that turns out to be a question
about three words in a treaty.

<!-- S16 -->
The European Union has a rule
about who may join.
It is short.

<!-- S17 -->
Any European State...
may apply to become a member
of the Union.

<!-- S18 -->
Any European State.

<!-- S19 -->
Canada is a great many things.
A European state is not one of them.

<!-- S20 -->
Although notice something.

<!-- S21 -->
That sentence is about membership.

<!-- S22 -->
The research programme Canada joined
never said anything of the kind.
Israel has been in it for years.
So has New Zealand.

<!-- S23 -->
Geography guards the front door.
It never guarded the side entrances.

<!-- S24 -->
So what is actually being proposed?

<!-- S25 -->
Picture two doors.

<!-- S26 -->
Behind the first one:
full membership.
A seat at the table.
A vote. A veto.
And every obligation that comes with it.

<!-- S27 -->
Under the treaties as they stand,
that door is shut.

<!-- S28 -->
Behind the second one:
an ordinary partner.
Trade deals. Joint projects.
Everything Canada already has.

<!-- S29 -->
That door is wide open.
It just doesn't take you anywhere new.

<!-- S30 -->
So Europe is talking about
building a third door.
One that isn't in the building yet.

<!-- S31 -->
The list of what might go through it
is already circulating.

<!-- S32 -->
Defence. Energy.
Critical minerals.
Artificial intelligence.
Quantum technology.
Cybersecurity.
And the Arctic.

<!-- S33 -->
But the moment you draw a new door,
you have to say how wide it opens.

<!-- S34 -->
How much of the single market
would Canada get?

<!-- S35 -->
Which European rules
would Canada have to follow?

<!-- S36 -->
Would Canadian firms
sell into Europe
on the same terms as German ones?

<!-- S37 -->
Would Canada pay in?

<!-- S38 -->
And then the one
that decides everything.

<!-- S39 -->
Would Canada get a say
in the rules it has to obey?

<!-- S39b -->
And this is not hypothetical.

<!-- S39c -->
Europe has started charging
imported steel and cement
for the carbon burnt to make them.

<!-- S39d -->
A Canadian smelter that wants
to sell into Europe
now fills in its paperwork
under a rule written in Brussels.

<!-- S39e -->
It pays.
It does not vote.

<!-- S41 -->
Norway is not in the European Union.
Norwegians voted against joining.
Twice.

<!-- S42 -->
But since 1994, Norway has been
inside most of the single market.

<!-- S43 -->
Norwegian goods move freely.
Norwegian citizens move freely.
Norway pays into the budget.

<!-- S44 -->
And Norway takes on
European rules.
More than thirteen thousand of them
since 1994.

<!-- S45 -->
Written in Brussels.
Voted on in Brussels.
By people Norwegians do not elect.

<!-- S46 -->
Norwegians have a phrase for it.
Fax democracy.
The rules arrive.
You sign.

<!-- S49 -->
So Europe already comes
in more than two sizes.

<!-- S50 -->
There is in.
There is out.
And there is a whole staircase
in between.

<!-- S51 -->
But every step on that staircase
has been standing in Europe.

<!-- S52 -->
Canada would be the first one
standing somewhere else.

<!-- S52b -->
By the way, if you'd like to see
where the next question takes us,
consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S53 -->
Which raises the obvious question.
Why would Europe want that?

<!-- S54 -->
Start with the map,
and then keep zooming out.

<!-- S55 -->
Canada reaches far into the Arctic.

<!-- S56 -->
It has the longest coastline
of any country on Earth,
and most of it faces ice.

<!-- S57 -->
As that ice thins,
the ocean underneath turns into
shipping lanes, seabed and borders
that nobody has finished arguing about.

<!-- S58 -->
Europe has Arctic coastline too.
It would rather not argue alone.

<!-- S59 -->
Then there is what sits
under the Canadian ground.

<!-- S60 -->
Nickel. Cobalt. Copper.
Rare earth elements.
The unglamorous metals
inside every battery,
every turbine in the North Sea,
every guided missile.

<!-- S61 -->
Europe has the factories
and almost none of the ore.

<!-- S62 -->
And Europe has spent four years
learning what it costs
to buy something essential
from a single supplier.

<!-- S63 -->
Now turn the map around
and look at it from Ottawa.

<!-- S64 -->
Canada sells about three quarters
of everything it exports
to one country.

<!-- S65 -->
The United States.

<!-- S66 -->
That is not a trading relationship.
That is a dependency
with a very long border.

<!-- S67 -->
And Canada has spent recent years
discovering that the terms
of that relationship
can change without warning.

<!-- S67b -->
And Europe has been having
a version of the same conversation
with itself.

<!-- S67c -->
Spending more on defence.
Asking out loud how much
of its own security
it ought to be able to provide.

<!-- S67d -->
Neither of them says it loudly.
Both of them are doing the arithmetic.

<!-- S68 -->
Canada is not going to replace
the United States with Europe.
That is not the proposal.

<!-- S69 -->
It doesn't have to.
It just has to be less alone
in the room.

<!-- S70 -->
So both sides want this.
Which is exactly when
it is worth slowing down.

<!-- S71 -->
Because the awkward questions
have not gone anywhere.

<!-- S72 -->
Imagine a European law
that lands on a Canadian company.

<!-- S73 -->
Canada follows the rule.
Canada did not vote on the rule.
Is that acceptable?

<!-- S74 -->
Now flip it.

<!-- S75 -->
Imagine Canada getting real influence
over European decisions.

<!-- S76 -->
Would a voter in Portugal accept
that a country on another continent
helped write the law
that binds them?

<!-- S77 -->
And if the answer to both
is a carefully worded maybe,
how many different kinds
of membership can one union hold
before nobody can say
what membership means?

<!-- S79 -->
That is the whole argument.

<!-- S81 -->
Who is in?
And who decides?

<!-- S82 -->
Which brings us back to the name.

<!-- S83 -->
The European Union.

<!-- S84 -->
Those two words only ever run
in one direction.
European, therefore Europe,
therefore European countries.

<!-- S85 -->
But look at what
the second word is doing.

<!-- S86 -->
Union.

<!-- S87 -->
A union is not a place.
It is an agreement
about what its members do together.

<!-- S88 -->
Think about what
the European Union actually is.

<!-- S89 -->
Common rules.
A common market.
Shared courts.
Money moving without permission.
Standards written once
and obeyed in twenty-seven countries.

<!-- S90 -->
Not one of those things
is inherently European.

<!-- S91 -->
A country on the other side
of the Atlantic
can have compatible courts.
It can meet the same standards.
It can agree to the same rules.

<!-- S92 -->
The project began in 1951
with six neighbours
and an agreement about coal and steel.

<!-- S93 -->
Neighbours was the starting point.
The question is whether it is
also the ceiling.

<!-- S94 -->
Nothing has been agreed.
This is a proposal,
and proposals in Brussels
have a long and difficult life
ahead of them.

<!-- S94b -->
There is a summit in October
that is supposed to turn the idea
into something with edges.

<!-- S95 -->
Canada may end up with
a slightly better trade deal
and nothing more.

<!-- S96 -->
But the interesting part
already happened.

<!-- S97 -->
Europe said out loud
that membership might not have to mean
what it has always meant.

<!-- S98 -->
Canada does not have to move to Europe.
Europe does not have to move to Canada.

<!-- S99 -->
For seventy-five years,
European integration answered
one question.
How do we get European countries
to cooperate?

<!-- S100 -->
The next question is different.
How do we get countries to cooperate
when they are not European at all?

<!-- S101 -->
Maybe the E tells us
where the European Union came from.

<!-- S102 -->
And maybe the U tells us
what it could still become.
