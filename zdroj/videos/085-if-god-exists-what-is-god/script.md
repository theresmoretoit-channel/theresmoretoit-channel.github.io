# If God Exists, What Is God?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine that tomorrow, we somehow prove that God exists.

<!-- S02 -->
Not just find an unexplained event.
Not discover something strange in the universe.
Not find an ancient text.

<!-- S03 -->
Imagine we actually establish it.
God exists.

<!-- S04 -->
At first, that sounds like the end of the question.
But it isn't.
Because we have immediately created another question.

<!-- S05 -->
What exactly have we proved exists?

<!-- S06 -->
Suppose we discover that the universe has a cause.
That would be extraordinary.

<!-- S07 -->
But a cause of the universe is not necessarily the God most people imagine.
It could be something very different.

<!-- S08 -->
So let's grant that much.
Let's say there really is something behind the existence of our universe.

<!-- S09 -->
Something we might reasonably call God.
What can we actually say about it?

<!-- S10 -->
The first strange question is:
Where is God?

<!-- S11 -->
If God created the universe, it would be tempting to imagine God sitting somewhere outside it.
But what does "outside the universe" even mean?

<!-- S12 -->
The universe isn't necessarily a giant object floating inside a larger empty space.
Space itself is part of the universe.

<!-- S13 -->
So if God created space, God wouldn't simply be located somewhere beyond the edge of space.

<!-- S14 -->
There might not be an edge.
And asking where God is might not even make sense.

<!-- S15 -->
God wouldn't be somewhere over there.
God would have to exist in some sense that doesn't depend on the space we inhabit.

<!-- S16 -->
And then we encounter an even stranger question.
When did God exist?

<!-- S17 -->
If time itself began with the universe, asking what happened before the universe may not make sense.

<!-- S18 -->
It's a little like asking:
“What is north of the North Pole?”

<!-- S19 -->
The question sounds grammatical.
But the coordinate system itself may have reached its limit.

<!-- S20 -->
So if God created time, perhaps God isn't simply an extremely old being.
Perhaps the idea of being old doesn't apply at all.

<!-- S21 -->
God wouldn't have existed for a very long time before creating the universe.
There may have been no before.

<!-- S22 -->
Now ask something even more basic.
What is God made of?

<!-- S23 -->
Everything we normally encounter is made of something.
Atoms.
Particles.
Fields.
Radiation.

<!-- S24 -->
Even seemingly empty space has physical properties.

<!-- S25 -->
But if God is the creator of the physical universe, saying that God is made of ordinary matter would create a problem.
That matter would already have to exist.

<!-- S26 -->
So classical concepts of God often describe God as non-physical.
But now we have another problem.

<!-- S27 -->
We have spent our entire lives experiencing physical things.
We don't actually have an intuitive category for a non-physical object.

<!-- S28 -->
We can say the words.
But what would such a thing actually be?

<!-- S29 -->
And this is where the idea becomes particularly interesting.
Suppose a cosmological argument successfully establishes a first cause.

<!-- S30 -->
What have we learned?
Perhaps:
Something caused the universe to exist.

<!-- S31 -->
But how do we get from that to:
Someone caused the universe?

<!-- S32 -->
A cause doesn't necessarily have to be a person.
A rock can cause a window to break.

<!-- S33 -->
A star can cause a planet to orbit.
A physical process can cause another physical process.

<!-- S34 -->
So why would the ultimate cause have a mind?

<!-- S35 -->
One possibility is that a personal cause is required for some philosophical reason.
But that requires an additional argument.

<!-- S36 -->
The word God doesn't automatically provide the missing steps.

<!-- S37 -->
The major monotheistic religions describe God as conscious.
God knows.
God understands.
God chooses.
God can respond.

<!-- S38 -->
But consciousness, as far as we know, is associated with physical nervous systems.
So what would consciousness without a brain mean?

<!-- S39 -->
Maybe consciousness doesn't fundamentally require a brain.
Maybe our brains are simply one way consciousness can exist.

<!-- S40 -->
But at this point, we are trying to extend concepts developed from human experience into a completely different kind of existence.

<!-- S41 -->
And if God has thoughts, do those thoughts happen one after another?
Because if they do, that sounds like time.

<!-- S42 -->
One thought happens.
Then another.
Then another.

<!-- S43 -->
But if God exists outside time, perhaps divine thought doesn't work like ours.
Maybe there is no sequence of thoughts.

<!-- S44 -->
No waiting.
No changing from one thought to another.
Instead, God might somehow know things all at once.

<!-- S45 -->
That sounds strange.
But we shouldn't reject an idea merely because it's difficult to imagine.

<!-- S46 -->
Our inability to picture something isn't proof that it is impossible.
It may simply mean that our brains evolved to understand the world at human scale.

<!-- S47 -->
Now suppose we have established some kind of transcendent creator.
Does that tell us there is only one?

<!-- S48 -->
Not necessarily.
Our universe could, at least conceptually, have multiple creators.

<!-- S49 -->
If there are two or more of them, perhaps they cooperate.
Perhaps they disagree.
Perhaps one created part of reality and another created something else.

<!-- S50 -->
And if we want to call that being the God, rather than merely a god, we're already adding information.

<!-- S51 -->
What about the familiar idea that God is all-powerful?
This raises an old philosophical puzzle.

<!-- S52 -->
Can an omnipotent God create a rock so heavy that God cannot lift it?

<!-- S53 -->
If God can create it, then there is something God cannot do: lift it.

<!-- S54 -->
If God cannot create it, then there is something God cannot do: create it.

<!-- S55 -->
The puzzle sounds devastating.
But it depends on what we mean by “can do anything.”

<!-- S56 -->
Perhaps omnipotence means the ability to do anything that is logically possible.

<!-- S57 -->
Under that definition, creating a square circle isn't a task God fails to accomplish.
It's not a coherent task in the first place.

<!-- S58 -->
The same could apply to the rock.
The paradox may reveal something about the meaning of omnipotence rather than a contradiction in the existence of God.

<!-- S59 -->
Now consider knowledge.
If God knows everything, then God knows the future.

<!-- S60 -->
But what does that mean for human freedom?
Suppose God knows with absolute certainty what you will do tomorrow.

<!-- S61 -->
Could you still choose differently?

<!-- S62 -->
If you did choose differently, God's knowledge would have been wrong.
But if you cannot choose differently, in what sense is the choice free?

<!-- S63 -->
Philosophers have argued about this for centuries.
Some say divine foreknowledge is compatible with free will.

<!-- S64 -->
Others think there is a genuine conflict.
And some reject the idea that the future contains one fixed set of facts at all.

<!-- S65 -->
And then we reach perhaps the most important property of all.
Is God good?

<!-- S66 -->
If we discover that some creator exists, that alone wouldn't tell us that this creator is morally perfect.

<!-- S67 -->
A powerful creator could theoretically be indifferent.
Or morally strange.
Or hostile.
Or simply unlike us.

<!-- S68 -->
If we want to conclude that God is perfectly good, we need another reason.
As we saw in an earlier video, this raises the problem of suffering.

<!-- S69 -->
And now we reach a question that I find particularly strange.
If God is perfect...
does God actually need anything?

<!-- S70 -->
Suppose God is complete.
God lacks nothing.

<!-- S71 -->
God doesn't need food.
Doesn't need shelter.
Doesn't need money.
Doesn't need protection.
Doesn't even need company.

<!-- S72 -->
Then why would God want anything?
Why create a universe?
Why create conscious beings?

<!-- S73 -->
Was God lonely?
That seems strange for a being that needs nothing.
Was God bored?
Even stranger.

<!-- S74 -->
Why would God care whether humans worship?
Why answer prayers?
Why give commandments?
Why judge anyone?

<!-- S75 -->
Maybe creation wasn't something God needed to do.
Maybe it was something God chose to do.

<!-- S76 -->
But then we have another question.
Why?

<!-- S77 -->
And this may be the central point.
There is a huge difference between these two statements:

<!-- S78 -->
Something caused the universe.

<!-- S79 -->
And:
There is an all-powerful, all-knowing,
perfectly good, personal God
who created the universe,
cares about humans, answers prayers,
gives moral commands,
and wants to be known.

<!-- S80 -->
The second statement contains an enormous amount of additional information.
Where did all of it come from?

<!-- S81 -->
Not necessarily from the original argument for a first cause.
Each additional property requires its own justification.

<!-- S82 -->
Maybe those arguments exist.
Maybe some are strong.
Maybe some are weak.
But they are separate questions.

<!-- S83 -->
There's a broader lesson here.
Whenever we explain something, we have to be careful not to smuggle extra information into the explanation.

<!-- S84 -->
Imagine finding an ancient machine.
You determine that someone must have built it.

<!-- S85 -->
That tells you something.
But it doesn't tell you who built it.

<!-- S86 -->
What they looked like.
Why they built it.
Whether they were kind.
Or whether they even still exist.

<!-- S87 -->
The evidence for one property doesn't automatically establish all the others.
The same reasoning applies to God.

<!-- S88 -->
So what is God?
A person?
A mind?
A force?
The first cause of the universe?
Something outside space and time?

<!-- S89 -->
Perhaps the word “God” is doing too much work.
It may refer to very different ideas in different philosophical and religious traditions.

<!-- S90 -->
So asking:
“Does God exist?”
can sound like we're asking about one clearly defined object.

<!-- S91 -->
But perhaps we're actually asking about an entire family of very different concepts.

<!-- S92 -->
And there is one final possibility worth considering.
Maybe if we discovered the ultimate foundation of reality, we wouldn't immediately recognise it as God.

<!-- S93 -->
It might not be a person.
Its thinking might not resemble human thought at all.

<!-- S94 -->
It might not even be the kind of thing for which the words “what is it?” make sense.

<!-- S95 -->
We would have discovered something fundamental about reality.
But we might still be arguing about whether it deserves to be called God.

<!-- S96 -->
So perhaps the question isn't simply:
Does God exist?

<!-- S97 -->
The deeper question is:
What would we actually mean by “God exists”?

<!-- S98 -->
If we mean a first cause, that's one claim.
If we mean an intelligent creator, that's another.

<!-- S99 -->
If we mean a perfectly good being who cares about humans and answers prayers...
that's another claim again.

<!-- S100 -->
So if someone ever proves that God exists, don't stop there.
Ask the next question.

<!-- S101 -->
What exactly did they prove exists?

<!-- S102 -->
Because proving that there is something behind the universe...
may be only the beginning of the mystery.
