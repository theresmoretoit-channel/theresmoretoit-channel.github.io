# Why Are There Suddenly So Many Trans People?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Have you ever met a transgender person?

Maybe you have.

Maybe you think you haven't.

But how do you know?

<!-- S02 -->
A transgender person doesn't necessarily
look a certain way.

They don't have a particular personality,
voice, job, or sexual orientation.

<!-- S03 -->
Some are very obviously transgender.

Others aren't.

Some talk openly about it.

Others never tell anyone.

<!-- S04 -->
And some people may spend
their entire lives hiding it.

<!-- S05 -->
So perhaps the first question isn't:

"Why are there suddenly
so many transgender people?"

<!-- S06 -->
Perhaps the first question should be:

who are transgender people, anyway?

Let's take a closer look.

<!-- S07 -->
What does transgender actually mean?

<!-- S08 -->
When a baby is born, doctors usually
assign a sex based primarily
on the baby's physical characteristics.

<!-- S09 -->
For most people, the sex they were
assigned at birth and their
gender identity correspond.

These people are commonly
described as cisgender.

<!-- S10 -->
For some people, however, they don't.

Their gender identity differs from
the sex they were assigned at birth.

These people may describe themselves
as transgender, or simply trans.

<!-- S11 -->
And this distinction is important.

Being transgender is not the same thing
as being gay, lesbian,
bisexual, or heterosexual.

<!-- S12 -->
Gender identity and sexual orientation
are different things.

One is about who you are.

The other is about
who you're attracted to.

<!-- S12b -->
A trans woman is a woman
who was assigned male at birth.

In other words, she was born
with a typically male body.

A trans man is the other way round:

a man who was assigned female at birth.

<!-- S13 -->
A trans woman can be heterosexual,
lesbian, bisexual, or asexual.

A trans man can be heterosexual,
gay, bisexual, or asexual.

<!-- S14 -->
There is no particular sexual orientation
that comes with being transgender.

<!-- S14b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S15 -->
But what does it actually feel like?

<!-- S16 -->
This is where things become
difficult to explain.

Because if you've never experienced it
yourself, you may simply have no idea
what it feels like.

<!-- S17 -->
Some transgender people experience
something called gender dysphoria.

<!-- S18 -->
It can involve significant distress
related to a mismatch between
a person's experienced gender

and aspects of their body, or the way
they are perceived by other people.

<!-- S19 -->
And this is not simply:

"I don't like my body."

Many people don't like
parts of their bodies.

<!-- S20 -->
Someone can hate their nose,
their weight, their hair, or their height
without experiencing gender dysphoria.

Gender dysphoria is about
something more fundamental.

<!-- S21 -->
For some people, particular parts
of their body can feel profoundly wrong,

because they don't fit the way
they experience themselves.

<!-- S22 -->
Being called by the wrong name or pronoun
can also be deeply distressing
for some people.

Looking in the mirror can be difficult.

<!-- S23 -->
Puberty can be especially difficult,

because the body begins developing
in a direction that feels increasingly
incompatible with how the person
understands themselves.

<!-- S24 -->
And this experience can be difficult
for someone who has never had it
to imagine.

<!-- S25 -->
Imagine waking up every morning
and seeing something about your body

that doesn't merely look unpleasant
to you, but feels fundamentally
out of place.

<!-- S26 -->
That analogy will never perfectly
reproduce another person's experience.

But it may help explain why simply saying:

"Just accept your body."

doesn't necessarily solve the problem.

<!-- S27 -->
And importantly, not every transgender
person experiences gender dysphoria
in exactly the same way,
or to the same degree.

<!-- S28 -->
Some people experience severe distress.

Others describe a more persistent sense
that something about their gender
doesn't fit.

And some may identify as transgender
without seeking medical treatment at all.

<!-- S29 -->
There isn't one kind of transgender person.

<!-- S30 -->
This is another thing
that's easy to forget.

Transgender people aren't
a uniform group.

They can be young or old.

<!-- S31 -->
They can be religious or not.

They can be married, with children.

They can be politically
conservative or progressive.

<!-- S32 -->
Some want hormones.

Some don't.

Some want surgery.

Some don't.

Some change their name and appearance.

<!-- S32b -->

Some don't.

<!-- S33 -->
And some simply want to live their lives
without making their gender identity
a major part of their public identity.

There is no single transgender experience.

<!-- S34 -->
What is transition?

<!-- S35 -->
The word transition
can also be misleading.

People sometimes imagine that transition
means one particular operation.

It doesn't.

Transition can involve
several different things.

<!-- S36 -->
There can be social transition.

This can mean changing someone's name,
pronouns, clothing, or appearance.

<!-- S37 -->
There can be legal
or administrative transition.

This can mean changing the name
or gender marker on official documents,
where the law allows it.

<!-- S38 -->
And there can be medical transition.

This can include hormone therapy and,
for some people, surgical procedures.

<!-- S39 -->
But these are not three mandatory steps.

A transgender person does not have
to complete all of them.

<!-- S40 -->
There isn't a checklist that someone
has to complete before their identity
becomes "real."

<!-- S41 -->
And some people decide
not to transition at all.

They keep these feelings to themselves.

<!-- S42 -->
They may get married.

They may have children.

They may build a career.

They may appear to everyone around them
to be living a completely ordinary life.

<!-- S43 -->
So the person sitting next to you at work,
or someone you've known for twenty years,

may have a story you know nothing about.

<!-- S44 -->
And that's not necessarily
because they're deceiving you.

It may simply be because
their gender history is private.

<!-- S45 -->
Is being transgender a fashion?

<!-- S46 -->
It's certainly true that
young people influence each other.

It's also true that social media
can spread ideas very quickly.

And people can sometimes
misinterpret their own feelings.

<!-- S47 -->
But that's different from saying
that being transgender
is simply a fashion trend.

<!-- S48 -->
For some people, these feelings appear
to be persistent and deeply significant.

Some spend years trying
to suppress them.

Some risk rejection from their families.

<!-- S49 -->
Some risk losing relationships,
jobs, or social connections.

Some eventually decide that living openly
is worth those risks.

<!-- S50 -->
That doesn't mean that every person
who questions their gender
is necessarily transgender.

It doesn't mean that every decision
about transition is automatically correct.

And it doesn't mean that nobody
ever changes their mind.

<!-- S51 -->
It means that the simple explanation,

"They're just doing it
because it's fashionable,"

is probably not enough
to explain the whole phenomenon.

<!-- S51b -->
Human biology is not
a perfectly standardised system.

People are born with countless variations
in their bodies, brains, development,
and senses.

<!-- S51c -->
Some are obvious from birth.

Others aren't discovered until childhood,
puberty, or adulthood.

<!-- S51d -->
So why would we assume
that gender identity
must be an exception?

Why would every person's brain and body
have to fit together
in exactly the same way?

<!-- S51e -->
Perhaps the more surprising assumption
is not that some people
experience their gender differently.

Perhaps it's the assumption
that this could never happen at all.

<!-- S52 -->
Human beings are more
complicated than that.

<!-- S53 -->
Are there really more
transgender people now?

<!-- S54 -->
It certainly looks that way.

In the United States, researchers estimate
that about one in a hundred people
aged thirteen and over

describe themselves as transgender,
a group that also includes
many non-binary people.

<!-- S55 -->
But that number hides
an enormous difference between ages.

Among people aged sixty-five and over,
it's about one in four hundred.

Among young adults
aged eighteen to twenty-four,
it's nearly three in a hundred.

<!-- S56 -->
That's roughly ten times as many.

<!-- S57 -->
So are young people simply
being born differently?

These numbers don't show that.

<!-- S58 -->
But there is an important distinction
between how many people exist

and how many people are visible.

<!-- S59 -->
A young person today can search the internet,

find other people describing
almost exactly the same feelings,

and discover words for experiences
they couldn't previously explain.

<!-- S60 -->
Someone born in the 1950s
grew up without any of that.

<!-- S61 -->
The numbers alone can't tell us
how much of the difference
comes from visibility,

and how much from something else.

<!-- S62 -->
And this isn't the first time
people like this have become visible.

<!-- S63 -->
Were transgender people
around in the past?

<!-- S64 -->
In 1919, a doctor called
Magnus Hirschfeld opened an institute
in Berlin.

<!-- S65 -->
It studied human sexuality and gender,

including people who felt
that the sex they had been assigned
didn't fit them.

It built up a large library
on sex and gender.

<!-- S66 -->
In 1930, a Danish painter
called Lili Elbe came to the institute.

<!-- S67 -->
Over the following sixteen months,
she underwent four operations.

She became one of the best-known early cases
of what we would now call
gender-affirming surgery.

She died in 1931, most likely
from complications after the last one.

<!-- S68 -->
Two years later, in May 1933,
the institute was raided.

<!-- S69 -->
A few days later, much of its library
was burned on a public square.

<!-- S70 -->
Almost a hundred years ago,
there was already a place

where people like this
could be seen.

And then, quite suddenly,
there wasn't.

<!-- S71 -->
Berlin wasn't unique.

Many cultures have had their own ways
of understanding people who didn't fit
conventional gender roles.

<!-- S71b -->
In South Asia, there are the Hijra,
communities with centuries of history.

In North America, some Indigenous nations
have roles now often described
by the modern term Two-Spirit.

<!-- S72 -->
But people whose lives didn't fit
conventional gender expectations

are certainly not a new invention
of the internet.

<!-- S73 -->
So why do we notice them so much now?

<!-- S74 -->
Partly because, in some parts of the world,
people can now talk openly

about experiences
that used to stay hidden.

<!-- S75 -->
So when more people say,

"I am transgender,"

that doesn't automatically mean
that more people became transgender.

<!-- S76 -->
It can also mean
that more people are visible.

And visibility is not
the same thing as existence.

<!-- S77 -->
Maybe the real change
isn't in the people.

<!-- S78 -->
For centuries, many people who didn't fit
society's expectations hid it.

They lived in a role expected of them,
or simply never told anyone.

<!-- S79 -->
Today, in at least some parts
of the world, there are more possibilities.

But that doesn't mean
everyone takes them.

And it certainly doesn't mean
that being transgender is easy.

<!-- S80 -->
In fact, the next question
may be even more interesting.

How does a person actually figure out
that they are transgender?

<!-- S81 -->
Because some people seem
to know very early.

Others spend years wondering
what is wrong with them.

<!-- S82 -->
Some are convinced that they're simply
failing to be the kind of man or woman
society expects them to be.

And some only begin to understand
themselves decades later.

<!-- S83 -->
So if being transgender
isn't simply a fashion choice...

how does someone actually discover it?
