<!-- S01 -->
The universe is thirteen point eight
billion years old.
And humans appeared here
only a tiny fraction
of a second ago,
on the cosmic timescale.

<!-- S02 -->
So here's the famous question.

<!-- S03 -->
Where is everybody?

<!-- S04 -->
If intelligent life is common,
shouldn't somebody have colonised
the galaxy by now?
Shouldn't we have seen something?

<!-- S05 -->
A signal.
A spacecraft.
A megastructure.
Anything.

<!-- S06 -->
This is the basic idea
behind the Fermi paradox.
And it sounds like
a very simple argument.

<!-- S07 -->
So...

<!-- S08 -->
where are the aliens?

<!-- S09 -->
But there's a problem.
The Fermi paradox isn't really
a conflict between two observations.
We observe one thing.
We haven't detected
extraterrestrial civilisation.

<!-- S10 -->
And then we combine it
with a large collection
of assumptions.
They should be common.
They should become intelligent.
They should develop
advanced technology.

<!-- S11 -->
They should survive.
They should want to expand.
And they should leave traces
that we can recognise.

<!-- S12 -->
If even one of those
assumptions is wrong,
the paradox becomes
much less paradoxical.
So let's take them apart.

<!-- S13 -->
The simplest possibility is that
technological intelligence is rare.
Maybe life itself is common.

<!-- S14 -->
But perhaps getting
from a primitive cell
to a civilisation capable
of building radio telescopes
is extraordinarily unlikely.

<!-- S15 -->
And we have one
inconvenient problem here.
We have exactly one confirmed example
of a technological civilisation.

<!-- S16 -->
Us.
That's it.

<!-- S17 -->
We don't have ten planets
with intelligent life.
We don't have a thousand.
We have one.

<!-- S18 -->
So when someone says this.
"Intelligent life
probably evolves like this..."
there is a rather obvious question.

<!-- S19 -->
Based on what?

<!-- S20 -->
Earth could be typical.
Or Earth could be a freak accident.
We simply don't have
enough data to know.

<!-- S21 -->
But let's assume
civilisations are common.
Would that mean the galaxy
should be full of aliens?

<!-- S22 -->
Not at all.
Because intelligence isn't
the same thing as expansion.

<!-- S23 -->
Imagine a civilisation
with abundant energy,
advanced computers,
and virtual reality
indistinguishable
from the real thing.

<!-- S24 -->
And then somebody says this.
"We should send people
to another star."

<!-- S25 -->
The trip takes centuries.
It costs enormous amounts of energy.
The destination may contain
nothing useful.

<!-- S26 -->
And even if it does,
there is no obvious reason
why anyone has to go there.
Why?

<!-- S27 -->
We assume that
an advanced civilisation
will automatically
become expansionist.
But that's a very human assumption.

<!-- S28 -->
Humans have always expanded.
We spread across continents.
We crossed oceans.
So perhaps we assume
every civilisation will do the same
on a larger scale.

<!-- S29 -->
But maybe that's wrong.

<!-- S30 -->
If your civilisation lives
inside computers,
why travel hundreds of light-years?
Why build cities on another planet
when you can build
another universe at home?

<!-- S31 -->
The classic Fermi argument
needs something much stronger
than this.
"Aliens exist."

<!-- S32 -->
It needs this.
"Aliens exist,
and they want to expand."
Those are very different claims.

<!-- S33 -->
There's a darker possibility.
Perhaps some step on the way
from matter to a civilisation
is almost impossible to pass.
It's often called
the Great Filter.

<!-- S33b -->
Maybe technological civilisations
almost always destroy themselves.

<!-- S34 -->
But we have no evidence
that this is what happens.
The Great Filter
is a possible explanation.
It is not an observation.

<!-- S35 -->
There's another possibility
that's almost embarrassingly simple.
Maybe we're searching badly.

<!-- S36 -->
We talk about SETI as though
humanity had searched the universe
and found nothing.
But that's not what happened.

<!-- S37 -->
We've searched tiny fractions
of the possible parameter space.
We don't know what frequency
an alien civilisation would use,
or whether it would transmit at all.

<!-- S38 -->
We don't even know
what technology it would use.
For most of human history,
we didn't know radio existed.

<!-- S39 -->
So imagine giving a radio telescope
to an ancient Roman.
He points it at the sky.
It detects thousands of signals.

<!-- S40 -->
What does he conclude?
Probably nothing.

<!-- S41 -->
The signals aren't part
of his conceptual model
of the universe.

<!-- S42 -->
We could stare directly
at an alien civilisation's
technological activity
and have no idea
what we're looking at.

<!-- S43 -->
The absence of a signal
isn't the absence of a civilisation.
It's the absence of a signal
we know how to recognise.

<!-- S44 -->
And even if they are out there,
the galaxy is enormous.
The Milky Way is roughly
a hundred thousand
light-years across.

<!-- S45 -->
A civilisation could exist
somewhere in the galaxy right now...

<!-- S46 -->
and its light wouldn't reach us
for thousands of years.

<!-- S47 -->
There could be civilisations
that existed millions
of years ago,
and civilisations
that haven't even evolved yet.

<!-- S48 -->
The galaxy isn't one moment.
It's a huge
four-dimensional history.

<!-- S49 -->
We aren't looking
at the galaxy as it is.
We're looking at different parts
of it at different points
in their past.

<!-- S50 -->
And when we imagine aliens,
we imagine something
remarkably human.
They build machines.
They communicate.
They colonise.

<!-- S51 -->
But why?

<!-- S52 -->
Perhaps technological advancement
eventually makes a civilisation
less visible.
Perhaps the loudest phase
of a civilisation
is also its shortest.

<!-- S53 -->
Searching for noisy civilisations
could be like searching for teenagers
by looking only for abandoned toys.

<!-- S54 -->
Now we get to one
of the most famous ideas.

<!-- S55 -->
The Dark Forest.

<!-- S56 -->
Imagine the galaxy as a dark forest.
Every civilisation is a hunter
hiding among the trees.

<!-- S57 -->
You don't know who else is there.
You don't know their intentions.
And you don't know whether
they are stronger than you.

<!-- S58 -->
So the safest strategy is this.

<!-- S59 -->
Don't make noise.

<!-- S60 -->
If another civilisation discovers you,
it might decide to destroy you
before you become a threat.
Therefore everyone stays silent.

<!-- S61 -->
And that would neatly explain
the Fermi paradox.
It's an elegant idea.

<!-- S62 -->
But there's a problem.
A surprisingly big one.
How exactly do you hide a planet?

<!-- S63 -->
We don't broadcast
deliberate messages
to the galaxy all the time.
But Earth itself
is extremely interesting.

<!-- S64 -->
We have been modifying
our atmosphere,
producing artificial chemicals,
and generating enormous amounts
of radio emission.

<!-- S65 -->
And all of this is happening
on a planet that is already
naturally observable.

<!-- S66 -->
You don't have to detect
an alien radio message saying this.
"Hello. We are humans."

<!-- S67 -->
You can look at
the planet's atmosphere.
You can study its spectrum.
You can ask
what gases are present.

<!-- S68 -->
A sufficiently advanced telescope
observing Earth as an exoplanet
could detect the chemicals
in our atmosphere.

<!-- S69 -->
Water vapour.
Carbon dioxide.
Oxygen.
Ozone.

<!-- S70 -->
Of course, detecting oxygen
does not automatically mean this.
"There are humans here."

<!-- S71 -->
It could simply mean this.
"This planet is interesting."

<!-- S72 -->
But that's already enough
to create a problem
for the Dark Forest.
The argument doesn't require them
to know exactly what we are.

<!-- S73 -->
It only requires them to notice
that something unusual
is happening here.

<!-- S74 -->
And Earth is not merely a rock
with a little bit of life.
Its atmosphere has been
dramatically shaped by biology.

<!-- S75 -->
And increasingly...

<!-- S76 -->
by technology.

<!-- S77 -->
And atmospheric chemistry
isn't even the only problem.
Some of our strongest transmissions
are not intended
for extraterrestrials at all.

<!-- S78 -->
Powerful radar systems
can produce signals
that are vastly stronger
than ordinary radio leakage.

<!-- S79 -->
Researchers have investigated
how detectable
Earth's technological activity
could be from other stars.

<!-- S80 -->
Under favourable circumstances,
some of these signals could
be detected across
thousands of light-years.

<!-- S81 -->
That does not mean
somebody could casually look
across the galaxy and say this.
"There are humans on Earth."

<!-- S82 -->
That's not how astronomy works.
Detection depends on distance,
direction and sensitivity.

<!-- S83 -->
But it does mean something
much more modest...

<!-- S84 -->
and much more interesting.

<!-- S85 -->
Earth is not perfectly hidden.

<!-- S86 -->
We are already producing
technosignatures.
And we're not even trying
very hard to hide them.

<!-- S87 -->
So does that kill the Dark Forest?
Not completely.

<!-- S88 -->
A sufficiently advanced civilisation
could perhaps hide
much better than we can.
But now the Dark Forest
has a new problem.

<!-- S89 -->
It isn't enough
for civilisations to be afraid.
They also have to be
extremely good at hiding.
And every one of them
has to reach that conclusion
independently.

<!-- S90 -->
Because if even one
civilisation decides this.

<!-- S91 -->
"We don't care. Let's expand."

<!-- S92 -->
then the galaxy might eventually
become much less quiet.

<!-- S92b -->
The Dark Forest therefore
doesn't solve the Fermi paradox.
It adds another assumption.
Almost everyone must be paranoid.

<!-- S93 -->
We started with this.
"Where is everybody?"

<!-- S94 -->
But perhaps that
isn't the right question.
Maybe the correct question is this.

<!-- S95 -->
"What exactly should we expect
an alien civilisation to look like?"

<!-- S96 -->
Do we expect it to expand?
Why?
Do we expect it to remain
detectable for millions of years?
Why?

<!-- S97 -->
And most importantly.
Do we expect our instruments
to recognise it?

<!-- S98 -->
We have one example
of a technological civilisation.

<!-- S99 -->
One.

<!-- S100 -->
And we've existed
for an absurdly short time.
So perhaps we're making
an enormous extrapolation
from a sample size of one.

<!-- S101 -->
Perhaps somewhere in the galaxy,
somebody has already
noticed our planet.
Not because we sent them a message.

<!-- S102 -->
But because...

<!-- S103 -->
we are difficult to hide.

<!-- S104 -->
So where does that leave us?
Maybe intelligent life is rare.
Maybe it's common.
Maybe they don't want to expand.
Maybe they're hiding.

<!-- S105 -->
Every one of these possibilities
is conceivable.
And that's precisely the problem.

<!-- S106 -->
The Fermi paradox is presented
as if we knew one side
of the equation.
"The universe should be full
of civilisations."

<!-- S106b -->
But we don't.
So perhaps there is simply
a huge amount of uncertainty...

<!-- S107 -->
wrapped inside
a very catchy question.

<!-- S108 -->
Where is everybody?

<!-- S109 -->
Maybe someone is looking at Earth
right now and wondering
what the hell is happening
on that strange little blue planet.

<!-- S110 -->
And perhaps the most important
question isn't this.

<!-- S111 -->
"Why haven't we found aliens?"

<!-- S112 -->
It's this.
"If they exist...
would we even recognise them
when we see them?"
