<!-- S01 -->
A child is alone in a room
with a throwing game and a prize.
There are three rules,
and they make it almost impossible to win.

<!-- S02 -->
Before the game, the researcher points
to an empty chair in the corner.
It isn't really empty, he says.
A friendly invisible princess, Alice,
is sitting in it.

<!-- S03 -->
Then he leaves the room,
and reminds the child that Alice is watching.
Nobody else is there to check.
A hidden camera is recording,
but the child doesn't know that.

<!-- S04 -->
The children who believed in Alice
held out about as long
as children watched by a real adult.

<!-- S05 -->
And the sceptical ones?
Most of them did cheat...
but only after they'd checked
that Alice really wasn't there.
A hand waved across her empty chair.

<!-- S06 -->
An invisible mind,
invented only minutes earlier...
and for the children who believed in her,
it worked like a real one.

<!-- S07 -->
That's a small version
of something humans have done everywhere.
Almost every culture we know of
has spirits, ancestors or gods.

<!-- S08 -->
But here's the strange part.
For most of human history,
the greatest gods people believed in
probably weren't moral judges.
They didn't watch whether you cheated,
or punish you for it.

<!-- S09 -->
So there are really two questions.
Why do we imagine invisible minds
so easily?
And when did they start watching us?

<!-- S10 -->
Part of the first answer
came up in an earlier video.
Our brains are built to detect minds,
and they would rather see one too many
than one too few.

<!-- S11 -->
But there's a stranger ingredient.
We don't only detect minds.
We find it easy to imagine a mind
with no body attached.

<!-- S12 -->
In another experiment,
young children watched a puppet show.
A baby mouse goes for a walk in the woods...
and gets eaten by an alligator.

<!-- S13 -->
Then the researchers asked questions.
Does the mouse still need to eat?
Most of the youngest children said no.
Its body has stopped working.

<!-- S14 -->
Does it still want to go home?
Does it still love its mother?
Most of them said yes.

<!-- S15 -->
The body had stopped.
Much of the mind, they assumed, carried on.

<!-- S16 -->
Put those two things together.
A brain that sees minds everywhere,
and finds it natural for a mind
to exist without a body.
Invisible beings are almost the default.

<!-- S17 -->
But an invisible being
isn't the same thing as a god
who watches you
and cares whether you lie.

<!-- S18 -->
When anthropologists compared
hunter-gatherer societies,
more than half had no known high god,
no supreme creator or ruler of the world.

<!-- S19 -->
And most of the others had one
who stayed out of human affairs.
Out of every eight societies,
about seven had no high god
who took an interest in people.

<!-- S20 -->
Their world was full of spirits.
A spirit could send rain or sickness,
or punish a broken taboo.
But it usually wasn't an all-seeing judge
of how you treated your neighbour.

<!-- S21 -->
In a small community,
gossip was the surveillance system.

<!-- S22 -->
Then societies grew.
Towns, cities, kingdoms,
full of people you'd never meet again,
and promises nobody else had heard.

<!-- S23 -->
Gossip doesn't reach that far.
So how do you trust a stranger?

<!-- S24 -->
One answer, proposed by the psychologist
Ara Norenzayan, is gods.
Big gods.

<!-- S25 -->
A god who sees everything,
including what you do in private.
Who cares how you treat strangers.
And who punishes.

<!-- S26 -->
If everyone around you believes that,
you don't need to know the stranger.
You only need to know
that the stranger believes
they're being watched.

<!-- S27 -->
It's Princess Alice,
scaled up to a civilisation.

<!-- S28 -->
It's a neat story.
Can it be tested?

<!-- S29 -->
In a study published in 2016,
researchers played a simple game
with five hundred and ninety-one people
in eight very different communities,
from Fiji and Vanuatu
to Siberia, Brazil and Tanzania.

<!-- S30 -->
Each player shared out coins
between two cups, by rolling a die.
One cup for themselves,
one for a distant stranger
of the same religion.
Nobody checked the rolls,
so cheating was easy.

<!-- S31 -->
The more people believed their god
knew what humans do
and punished wrongdoing,
the more coins they gave
to the distant stranger.

<!-- S32 -->
And it was punishment that mattered.
Belief in a god who rewards
didn't explain the result.

<!-- S33 -->
Notice who the stranger was, though.
Someone of the same religion.
Big gods may widen the circle of trust.
They don't necessarily include everyone.

<!-- S34 -->
So did big gods make
big societies possible?
Or did growing societies
come up with big gods
because they needed them?

<!-- S35 -->
In 2019, a huge study tried to settle it,
using a database of hundreds of societies
across ten thousand years of history.

<!-- S36 -->
Its conclusion, published in Nature,
went against the big gods idea.
Moralising gods turned up
only after societies reached
around a million people.
The big societies came first.

<!-- S37 -->
Two years later,
the paper was retracted.

<!-- S38 -->
Another team had looked at the data.
For most of the early societies,
the database said "unknown"
about moralising gods.

<!-- S39 -->
And in the analysis,
"unknown" had been counted as "no".

<!-- S40 -->
In the critics' reanalysis,
the result flipped.
Moralising gods could have come first.

<!-- S41 -->
The original team says many of those
unknowns were really mislabelled absences.
They redid the analysis,
and they still find that big societies
came first,
and that big gods didn't drive their growth.
The argument isn't over.

<!-- S42 -->
So here's the honest state of things.
Belief in a watching god
can change how people treat strangers.
Whether it helped build
the first great societies,
or arrived once they were already there,
we don't know yet.

<!-- S43 -->
And there's one more twist.
Some of the most trusting societies today
are also among the least religious.
Denmark. Sweden. Norway.

<!-- S44 -->
Norenzayan's explanation is that
courts, contracts and police
took over much of the watching.
In his words, those societies
climbed religion's ladder...
and then kicked it away.

<!-- S45 -->
None of this tells us
whether any god exists.
Why we believe something
and whether it's true
are separate questions.

<!-- S46 -->
So why does almost every culture
have gods?
Part of the answer may be a mind
that easily imagines invisible minds,
even ones without a body.

<!-- S47 -->
But that doesn't explain
why some of them became moral judges...
or whether they helped
build large societies.

<!-- S48 -->
Those are different questions.
And we still don't have
a complete answer.

<!-- S49 -->
And an invisible watcher
can still work...
even on a child alone in a room,
next to an empty chair,
as long as the child believes.

<!-- S50 -->
So here's a question for you.
If you knew for certain
that nobody would ever find out,
would you behave any differently?
Let me know in the comments.
