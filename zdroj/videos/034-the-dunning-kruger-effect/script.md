<!-- S01 -->
You know that feeling
when you learn something new...
and suddenly, it seems incredibly simple?

<!-- S02 -->
You watch a couple of videos.
Read a few articles.
Maybe you try it yourself.

<!-- S03 -->
And then you think:
"I get it."

<!-- S04 -->
But here's the scary part.

<!-- S05 -->
What if the thing you're missing
is precisely the thing
that would tell you
that you're missing something?

<!-- S06 -->
This is the basic idea behind
the Dunning–Kruger effect.

<!-- S07 -->
And it's much more interesting —
and much more disturbing —
than the popular internet version of it.

<!-- S08 -->
Imagine you've never programmed before.
Someone shows you a few lines of code.

<!-- S09 -->
You learn what a variable is.
You learn what a loop does.

<!-- S10 -->
You write your first little program...
and suddenly programming
doesn't seem that difficult anymore.

<!-- S11 -->
You might even look at
professional programmers and wonder:
"Why do they make
such a big deal out of this?"

<!-- S12 -->
But then you keep learning.

<!-- S13 -->
You encounter data structures.
Concurrency.
Memory management.

<!-- S14 -->
And suddenly you realise
that programming isn't one problem.
It's an entire landscape of problems
you didn't even know existed.

<!-- S15 -->
And that's the key.

<!-- S16 -->
In nineteen ninety-nine, psychologists
David Dunning and Justin Kruger
published a study investigating
something very similar.

<!-- S17 -->
They were interested in people's ability
to judge their own performance.

<!-- S18 -->
They gave students tests
of logical reasoning, grammar,
and humour.
Then they asked them
how well they thought they had done.

<!-- S19 -->
What they reported was that
people who performed poorly
on certain tasks often had difficulty
recognising just how poorly
they had performed.

<!-- S20 -->
And the numbers were striking.

<!-- S21 -->
The people who performed
in the bottom quarter
estimated their performance
as if they were around
the sixty-second percentile.

<!-- S22 -->
In reality, they were around the twelfth.

<!-- S23 -->
They weren't just wrong.
They were wrong
about how wrong they were.

<!-- S24 -->
In other words, the problem wasn't only
that they made mistakes.
They also lacked some of the knowledge
necessary to recognise those mistakes.

<!-- S25 -->
And this creates a peculiar situation.

<!-- S26 -->
You need a certain amount of knowledge
to perform a task.
But you may also need
some of that same knowledge
to accurately judge your performance.

<!-- S27 -->
So when your knowledge is very limited...
your ability to evaluate
your own knowledge can be limited too.

<!-- S28 -->
And this is where the phenomenon
becomes genuinely interesting.

<!-- S29 -->
Suppose I give you
a complicated problem.
You produce an answer.
You are completely convinced
it's correct.

<!-- S30 -->
How can you tell whether you're right?

<!-- S31 -->
You could compare it with an expert.
You could test it against reality.

<!-- S32 -->
You could look for counterexamples.
You could measure the result.

<!-- S33 -->
But imagine that none of those things
are available.
Then you're relying largely
on your own judgement.

<!-- S34 -->
And if your judgement is based
on the same incomplete knowledge
that produced the original answer...
you have a problem.

<!-- S35 -->
Your brain is essentially
grading its own exam.
With an answer key it doesn't have.

<!-- S36 -->
This is also why expertise can look
surprisingly different from confidence.

<!-- S37 -->
A beginner might say:
"This is easy."

<!-- S38 -->
An experienced person might say:
"It depends."

<!-- S39 -->
Why?
Not necessarily because
the expert is less confident.

<!-- S40 -->
The expert simply sees more variables.

<!-- S41 -->
The beginner sees the problem.

<!-- S42 -->
The expert sees the problem...
and all the ways
the problem can go wrong.

<!-- S43 -->
Think about something as simple
as fixing a computer.

<!-- S44 -->
A beginner might say:
"Just reinstall the operating system."

<!-- S45 -->
Sometimes, that's exactly right.

<!-- S46 -->
But an experienced administrator
immediately starts asking questions.

<!-- S47 -->
Is the hardware healthy?
Is the filesystem corrupted?
Is the disk failing?

<!-- S48 -->
Is there a backup?
Is the problem actually software?
Is there a network issue?

<!-- S49 -->
Will reinstalling
destroy useful evidence?
What caused the problem
in the first place?

<!-- S50 -->
The beginner sees one solution.
The expert sees a decision tree.

<!-- S51 -->
And that difference can make
expertise look like uncertainty.

<!-- S52 -->
But there's an important catch.

<!-- S53 -->
The Dunning–Kruger effect
does not mean:
"The less intelligent you are,
the more confident you become."

<!-- S54 -->
And it doesn't mean
that experts are always insecure.
That's a caricature of the research.

<!-- S55 -->
But there's another problem.

<!-- S56 -->
What if even the famous
Dunning–Kruger graph
doesn't mean quite
what we think it means?

<!-- S57 -->
Several researchers have argued
that the characteristic pattern
can appear partly
because of statistical effects.

<!-- S58 -->
Things like regression to the mean.
And the fact that almost everyone
thinks they're above average.
And a correlation created
by the way the two measurements
are plotted against each other.

<!-- S59 -->
And here's the surprising part.

<!-- S60 -->
If you take simulated data
with no such effect built into it,
and plot it the same way...

<!-- S61 -->
you can still get a graph
that looks much like the original.

<!-- S62 -->
In other words...
the graph itself
needs to be interpreted carefully.

<!-- S63 -->
And the debate isn't completely settled.

<!-- S64 -->
There are responses to those criticisms,
and responses to those responses.

<!-- S65 -->
Which brings us back
to the uncomfortable question.
How do you know you're right?

<!-- S66 -->
Dunning and Kruger's own claim
was more subtle.
People can have difficulty
accurately evaluating
their own competence,
especially when they lack the skills
needed to evaluate the task itself.

<!-- S67 -->
And that can happen to almost anyone.

<!-- S68 -->
In fact, that's what makes
this effect so dangerous.

<!-- S69 -->
You don't have to be stupid.
You just have to enter a new field.

<!-- S70 -->
Every expert was once a beginner.
Every beginner initially has
a very incomplete mental model
of the subject.

<!-- S71 -->
And that means every one of us
has areas where we may be
confidently wrong...
without realising it.

<!-- S72 -->
And here's the really
uncomfortable question.

<!-- S73 -->
How do you know
you're not doing it right now?

<!-- S74 -->
You might be thinking:
"Fortunately, I understand
the Dunning–Kruger effect,
so I'm not one of those people."

<!-- S75 -->
But that doesn't prove anything.
Knowing that the effect exists
doesn't magically make you immune to it.

<!-- S76 -->
You can understand the concept
perfectly...
and still overestimate
your knowledge of economics.

<!-- S77 -->
Or medicine.
Or programming.
Or history.

<!-- S78 -->
Or physics.
Or politics.
Or literally anything else.

<!-- S79 -->
So what can we actually do about it?

<!-- S80 -->
The answer isn't simply:
"Be more humble."

<!-- S81 -->
Humility is useful.
But humility by itself
doesn't tell you whether you're right.

<!-- S82 -->
A person can be extremely humble...
and still be wrong.

<!-- S83 -->
What we need is something better:
feedback from reality.

<!-- S84 -->
Test your assumptions.
Look for counterexamples.
Measure things.

<!-- S85 -->
Ask people who know more than you.
Try to predict what will happen
before it happens.

<!-- S86 -->
And, perhaps most importantly...
make it possible for yourself
to discover that you're wrong.

<!-- S87 -->
A good programmer doesn't trust
their code because
they feel confident about it.
They run tests.

<!-- S88 -->
A scientist doesn't trust an idea
because it feels convincing.
They test it against evidence.

<!-- S89 -->
An engineer doesn't say:
"I'm pretty sure this bridge will hold."

<!-- S90 -->
They calculate it.
Measure it.
Test it.
And build in safety margins.

<!-- S91 -->
These systems work because
they don't rely entirely
on the human ability to judge itself.

<!-- S92 -->
They let reality provide the answer.

<!-- S93 -->
And perhaps that's the most useful
lesson of the Dunning–Kruger effect.

<!-- S94 -->
The goal isn't
to constantly doubt yourself.
It's not:
"Maybe I'm wrong about everything."

<!-- S95 -->
The goal is to know
how you would find out
if you were wrong.

<!-- S96 -->
That's a much stronger form
of intellectual humility.

<!-- S97 -->
Instead of saying:
"I'm probably right."

<!-- S98 -->
Ask:
"What evidence would convince me
I'm wrong?"

<!-- S99 -->
If you have a good answer...
you're already doing
something very valuable.

<!-- S100 -->
Because the most dangerous ignorance
isn't simply not knowing something.
It's not knowing that you don't know it.

<!-- S101 -->
And sometimes...
the first step
towards becoming an expert...
is discovering just how much
there is left to learn.
