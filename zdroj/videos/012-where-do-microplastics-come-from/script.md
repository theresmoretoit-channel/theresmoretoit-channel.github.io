# Where Do Microplastics Come From?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Have you ever wondered
where all those microplastics
actually come from?

When we hear the word microplastics,
most of us probably think
of plastic bottles.

<!-- S02 -->
Plastic bags.

Food packaging.

Synthetic clothes shedding tiny fibres
in the washing machine.

Maybe cosmetics.

Or simply plastic waste
slowly breaking down
into smaller and smaller pieces.

<!-- S03 -->
And all of these things
really do contribute to the problem.

But there is another major source
of tiny plastic particles
that most people probably
don't think about.

<!-- S04 -->
And you probably have some of it
very close to you right now.

<!-- S05 -->
The tyres on your car.

<!-- S06 -->
You probably know that tyres wear out.

The tread slowly disappears.

After tens of thousands of kilometres,
the tyres are worn enough
that you have to replace them.

<!-- S07 -->
But have you ever stopped to think
about where all that missing
material actually goes?

Because it doesn't simply disappear.

<!-- S08 -->
Every time you drive,
the tyres rub against the road.

Tiny amounts of material
are worn away.

<!-- S09 -->
Some of it stays on the road.

Some gets blown around
by traffic and moving air.

And when it rains,
some of it is washed away
into drains and towards rivers.

<!-- S10 -->
Some of these particles are so small
that you can't see them at all.

<!-- S11 -->
And a modern tyre isn't simply
a piece of rubber.

<!-- S12 -->
It is a complex mixture
of natural rubber, synthetic polymers,
carbon-based materials
and a variety of chemical additives.

<!-- S13 -->
As the tyre wears, it produces
what scientists call
tyre wear particles.

And a portion of these particles
falls into the category
of microplastics.

<!-- S14 -->
But that phrase deserves a footnote.

A tyre is not simply plastic.

The tread is a blend
of natural rubber, which grows on trees,
and synthetic rubbers, which do not.

<!-- S15 -->
And by the time a particle
has left the road,
it usually isn't pure tread at all.

It is tyre rubber
with road surface ground into it,
in roughly equal parts.

<!-- S16 -->
That is why scientists prefer to say
tyre and road wear particles.

Whether those count as microplastics
depends on the definition you use.

<!-- S17 -->
They are not quite
a fragment of a plastic bottle,
and that is what makes them
so hard to study.

<!-- S18 -->
And there is another reason
these particles are hard to study.

The problem may not be
the rubber particle itself.

<!-- S19 -->
A tyre contains hundreds
of different chemicals.

One of them is called 6PPD.

It is there to protect the rubber
from ozone,
so that the tyre lasts longer.

<!-- S20 -->
It does that by reacting with ozone
before the rubber can.

And one of the things
that reaction leaves behind
is a chemical called 6PPD-quinone.

<!-- S21 -->
And that one is extremely toxic
to some fish.

It has been linked to mass deaths
of coho salmon in streams
near busy roads, after heavy rain.

<!-- S22 -->
So sometimes the question isn't
what happens when a piece of tyre
enters the environment.

It is what that tyre
releases along the way.

<!-- S23 -->
So every time a car
drives down a road,
it is gradually leaving
a tiny part of its tyres behind.

<!-- S24 -->
Individually, the amount
produced by one car
doesn't sound very impressive.

But add it up
over the life of a tyre.

<!-- S25 -->
A car tyre is thought to lose
something like a tenth to a fifth
of its own weight while it is used.

For a typical tyre
that is about a kilogram.

<!-- S26 -->
Several kilograms for a full set.

Scattered, invisibly,
along every road you have ever driven.

<!-- S27 -->
And there are well over a billion
vehicles on this planet.

Each one has several tyres.

And all of them
are constantly wearing down.

<!-- S28 -->
That's why tyre wear is considered
one of the major sources
of microplastic pollution.

<!-- S29 -->
The exact numbers vary
considerably between studies,
because researchers use
different definitions,
measurements and assumptions.

<!-- S30 -->
But the basic idea
is not controversial.

A lot of material
is leaving our tyres
and entering the environment.

<!-- S31 -->
So where does it all end up?

The models disagree on the details,
but they agree on the shape of it.

<!-- S32 -->
Most of the material
stays close to the road,
trapped in the asphalt
or in the soil beside it.

A large part is carried away by rain.

<!-- S33 -->
And something like a tenth
simply goes into the air.

Which means some of it
is not travelling to a river at all.

It is travelling to your lungs.

<!-- S34 -->
And tyres aren't the only source
of particles from cars.

There are also brakes.

Every time you slow down,
the brake pads and other components
wear down too.

<!-- S35 -->
This produces brake dust,
a mixture containing metals
and other materials.

It isn't simply microplastic,
but it is another significant source
of tiny particles from road traffic.

<!-- S36 -->
And there is also the road itself.

Tyres wear.

Brakes wear.

Road surfaces wear.

<!-- S37 -->
And traffic can stir up particles
that have already settled
on the road.

So when we talk about
emissions from cars,
the exhaust pipe
is only part of the story.

<!-- S38 -->
These are sometimes called
non-exhaust emissions.

<!-- S39 -->
And this brings us to something
that may sound slightly
ridiculous at first.

You may have heard about
the European Union introducing
new requirements for vehicle emissions.

<!-- S40 -->
But Euro seven
isn't only about exhaust gases.

For the first time,
Europe puts a number
on how much dust
your brakes are allowed to shed.

<!-- S41 -->
Three milligrams per kilometre
for a battery electric car,
seven for everything else.

<!-- S42 -->
Tyres are in there too,
but in a much weaker form.

Those limits have not
actually been written yet.

<!-- S43 -->
The regulation prints the headings,
leaves every single limit blank,
and hands the job
to a United Nations working group.

<!-- S44 -->
And here is the part
that people get wrong.

The rules set a limit.

They do not say how to meet it.

<!-- S45 -->
And one of the routes they are trying
sounds almost like a joke.

A vacuum cleaner for your brakes.

<!-- S46 -->
Not a household vacuum cleaner
strapped to the wheel.

But one French system
cuts a groove in the brake pad
and sucks the dust out
through the caliper.

<!-- S47 -->
On test rigs it captures most of it.

Nobody is required to fit one.

It is simply one answer
to a limit that has to be met.

<!-- S48 -->
And suddenly, the idea
doesn't sound quite so crazy.

Because if you know that a brake
is constantly producing tiny particles...

why not try to catch them
before they spread everywhere?

<!-- S49 -->
And here's another interesting part.

Electric cars don't make
this problem disappear.

<!-- S50 -->
They don't have exhaust emissions
from an internal combustion engine.

And most of them
barely touch their brake pads.

<!-- S51 -->
They slow down with the motor,
recovering energy
instead of rubbing a pad
against a disc.

Measurements suggest this can cut
brake particles by well over half.

<!-- S52 -->
Europe already asks them
for three milligrams instead of seven.

<!-- S53 -->
But no motor can help with the tyres.

They still have tyres.

They still drive on roads.

<!-- S54 -->
In fact, because some electric vehicles
are heavier than comparable
combustion-engine cars,
tyre wear can be
an interesting issue of its own.

<!-- S55 -->
So even a car
with zero tailpipe emissions
can still produce particles
as it moves.

<!-- S56 -->
So what can we actually do about it?

There are obvious
technological approaches.

Tyres could be designed to last longer
and produce less wear.

Their materials could be improved.

<!-- S57 -->
Road runoff could potentially
be filtered before it reaches
rivers and other waterways.

<!-- S58 -->
But there is also
a much simpler solution.

Don't wear the tyre out
in the first place.

<!-- S59 -->
Driving style matters.

Hard acceleration,
aggressive cornering and heavy braking
all put additional stress on tyres.

<!-- S60 -->
But even the gentlest driver
cannot eliminate tyre wear.

Because that's what
a tyre is designed to do.

It grips the road.

And some of its material
is inevitably lost in the process.

<!-- S61 -->
And this brings us
to the most uncomfortable part
of the story.

What does all of this
actually do to us?

<!-- S62 -->
The honest answer is
that we don't fully know yet.

Microplastics have been detected
in water, food, air
and human tissues.

<!-- S63 -->
And nanoplastics are an even newer
and more difficult subject to study.

<!-- S64 -->
But finding particles
in the human body
doesn't automatically mean
that they are causing disease.

<!-- S65 -->
We still don't know
what levels of exposure are harmful,
or what happens over decades.

<!-- S66 -->
And this is one reason
why simply calling all of this
microplastics can be misleading.

The research is still
catching up with the problem.

<!-- S67 -->
And perhaps that's the strangest thing
about all of this.

We've been driving cars
for more than a century.

We've always known
that tyres wear out.

<!-- S68 -->
We can literally see
the tread disappearing.

<!-- S69 -->
But only relatively recently
have we started asking
a much more basic question.

Where did all that
missing material go?

<!-- S70 -->
It didn't disappear.

It was gradually scattered
into the world around us.

<!-- S71 -->
And some of it became particles
so small that we can no longer see them.

<!-- S72 -->
So the next time
you look at a worn-out tyre...

remember that it isn't just a tyre
that has become thinner.

A small part of that tyre
has become part of the environment.

<!-- S73 -->
Which leaves one open question.

Can we learn to catch those particles
before they get away...

or does the tyre itself
have to change?
