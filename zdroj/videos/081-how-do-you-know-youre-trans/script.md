# How Do You Know You're Trans?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine someone asking you:

"How do you know you're a man?"

Or:

"How do you know you're a woman?"

<!-- S02 -->
You might have to think about it
for a moment.

And then you might answer:

"I just know."

<!-- S03 -->
And that's actually
a rather interesting answer.

Because most people never have to
examine their gender identity.

<!-- S04 -->
Nobody asks them to prove it.

Nobody says:

"Explain why you consider yourself a man."

It's simply part of their life.

<!-- S05 -->
But what if that sense
of certainty isn't there?

What if you start wondering:

"Am I really who everyone thinks I am?"

<!-- S06 -->
And even more importantly:

how do you know if the answer is no?

<!-- S07 -->
There isn't always a single moment.

<!-- S08 -->
When we imagine someone discovering
that they're trans, it's easy
to picture a dramatic moment.

<!-- S09 -->
As if one day they suddenly realise:

"Oh. I've actually been
someone else all along."

<!-- S10 -->
Sometimes the realisation
really can be that powerful.

But not always.

For many people,
it's a much slower process.

<!-- S11 -->
It might start with nothing more
than a vague feeling:

"Something about this
doesn't feel right."

<!-- S12 -->
But what exactly?

Often, the person doesn't know.

<!-- S13 -->
Maybe they don't feel comfortable
in their body.

Maybe they don't like the way
other people address them.

<!-- S14 -->
Maybe being seen as a man or a woman
feels strangely uncomfortable.

Or maybe they can't explain it at all.

<!-- S14b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S15 -->
Childhood, puberty, adulthood.

<!-- S16 -->
Some trans people say they questioned
their gender from an early age.

Others didn't notice anything like that.

<!-- S17 -->
For some, puberty becomes
the turning point.

Their body suddenly starts changing.

<!-- S18 -->
And they may discover that changes
which are a completely normal part
of growing up for everyone else

feel deeply uncomfortable to them.

<!-- S19 -->
But even then,
they may not think:

"I'm trans."

They may not even know
the word transgender.

<!-- S20 -->
They may simply think:

"I hate what's happening to my body."

<!-- S21 -->
And some people don't start asking
these questions until much later.

They may be thirty, forty,
or sixty years old.

<!-- S22 -->
They may have a partner, children,
a job, and an entire life

that looks completely ordinary
from the outside.

<!-- S23 -->
And yet they may eventually
start wondering:

"Why have I felt this way for so long?"

<!-- S24 -->
"But why didn't you know earlier?"

<!-- S25 -->
That's actually a reasonable question.

If someone is trans, why didn't they
simply know from the beginning?

<!-- S26 -->
But in order to recognise
and describe something,
you need words for it.

<!-- S27 -->
Imagine someone who has never heard
the word left-handed.

They only know that writing
with their right hand
feels strangely difficult.

<!-- S28 -->
They might spend years thinking
they're simply bad at writing.

<!-- S29 -->
Then one day they discover that some
people naturally use their left hand.

And they think:

"Wait. Maybe that's me."

<!-- S30 -->
Gender, of course, is not
the same thing as handedness.

But the process of putting a name
to something you've experienced
for a long time can be similar.

<!-- S31 -->
"Maybe I'm not man enough."

<!-- S32 -->
There's another complication.

Our ideas about being a man or a woman
aren't just about sex.

Society adds a whole collection
of expectations.

<!-- S33 -->
Men are supposed to be strong.

Women are supposed to be feminine.

<!-- S34 -->
Men are supposed to like certain things.

Women are supposed to like others.

<!-- S35 -->
And when someone doesn't fit
these expectations,
they may start wondering:

"Maybe I'm just not masculine enough."

Or:

"Maybe I'm not feminine enough."

<!-- S36 -->
A trans person can have
an additional question:

"Maybe I'm not trans.

Maybe I'm just a man who doesn't like
traditional masculinity."

<!-- S37 -->
And that's an important question.

Because not fitting gender stereotypes
is not the same thing as being trans.

<!-- S38 -->
A man can love pink.

A woman can hate dresses.

A man can be gentle and sensitive.

<!-- S39 -->
A woman can be aggressive,
technically minded,

or completely uninterested
in her appearance.

<!-- S40 -->
Gender identity is not a list
of personality traits.

<!-- S41 -->
And what about sexuality?

<!-- S42 -->
Another source of confusion
can be sexual orientation.

Someone might realise they're attracted
to people of the same sex.

<!-- S43 -->
And they might ask:

"So maybe I'm not a man?"

But sexual orientation and gender identity
are two different things.

<!-- S44 -->
A man can be gay.

A man can be straight.

A man can be bisexual.

And a trans person can have
any sexual orientation.

<!-- S45 -->
So being attracted to certain people
doesn't, by itself,
tell you whether you're trans.

<!-- S46 -->
"What if I'm just imagining it?"

<!-- S47 -->
And now we get to something
that may be much less visible
from the outside.

Doubt.

<!-- S48 -->
Someone may start asking:

"Am I really trans?"

"Or am I just unhappy with my body?"

<!-- S49 -->
"Or do I simply hate
traditional gender roles?"

"Or am I trying to explain
something else entirely?"

"What if I'm wrong?"

<!-- S50 -->
And sometimes these questions
keep coming back.

<!-- S51 -->
There is no simple test
that gives you a result:

Trans: yes.

Or:

Trans: no.

<!-- S52 -->
Suppressing it.

<!-- S53 -->
Some people decide they simply
won't think about it.

<!-- S54 -->
Maybe they tell themselves:

"This is nonsense."

"I just need to be normal."

"It'll go away."

"I'm not going to deal with this."

<!-- S55 -->
And sometimes people continue
living their original lives.

<!-- S56 -->
Not everyone who questions their gender
starts transitioning.

And not everyone who discovers
that they're trans
decides to make changes.

<!-- S57 -->
There can be many reasons.

Someone may not want
to change their life.

Someone may not want
to change their body.

Someone may not want
to tell other people.

<!-- S58 -->
And someone may simply
reach the conclusion:

"Yes, this is my identity.

But I don't want to transition."

<!-- S59 -->
That's not a contradiction.

Identity and what you decide
to do about it are not the same thing.

<!-- S60 -->
"So do you have to transition?"

<!-- S61 -->
No.

And that's an important point.

<!-- S62 -->
In the previous video, we distinguished
between social, legal,
and medical transition.

But none of them automatically follows
from discovering that you're trans.

<!-- S63 -->
Some people change the way they dress.

Some use a different name or pronouns.

<!-- S64 -->
Some take hormones.

Some undergo surgery.

Some do only some of these things.

And some do none of them.

<!-- S65 -->
Right now, we're interested
in something else:

how does someone actually come
to understand their own identity?

<!-- S66 -->
Telling someone else.

<!-- S67 -->
And then there may be another step.

Coming out.

<!-- S68 -->
Someone who has spent
months or years wondering:

"Am I trans?"

may eventually have to tell
someone else:

"I think I'm trans."

<!-- S69 -->
And that can be a strange moment.

Because inside that person's head,
something may have been happening
for years.

<!-- S70 -->
But for the other person,
this may be completely new information.

And they may ask:

"How do you know?"

<!-- S71 -->
Which brings us right back
to where we started.

<!-- S72 -->
How do you know?

<!-- S73 -->
Maybe the person doesn't have
a simple answer.

Maybe they can list dozens of memories.

<!-- S74 -->
Some from childhood.

Some from adolescence.

Some from the last few months.

But none of them necessarily
proves anything on its own.

<!-- S75 -->
And perhaps that's exactly why
the idea of a universal "trans test"
doesn't really work.

<!-- S76 -->
Because gender identity isn't something
most people discover
through a single measurement.

It's something people come to understand
through their own experience.

<!-- S77 -->
There's one more uncomfortable question
we should ask.

What if you're wrong?

<!-- S78 -->
Some people eventually stop
or change their transition.

This is often described
as detransition.

<!-- S79 -->
The reasons can be different.

Some people change
their gender identity.

Others discover that some parts
of transition aren't right for them.

<!-- S80 -->
And for some, the circumstances
in which they live
may play an important role.

<!-- S81 -->
The existence of detransition
doesn't prove either of the two
simple conclusions
people sometimes draw from it.

<!-- S82 -->
It doesn't mean that everyone
who transitions will eventually discover
they were "really cisgender" all along.

And it doesn't mean that nobody ever
changes their mind or stops transitioning.

<!-- S83 -->
People can make mistakes.

People can change their opinions.

And people can understand themselves
differently as they grow older.

That is true far beyond
questions of gender.

<!-- S84 -->
So we're back
to the original question.

How do you know you're trans?

<!-- S85 -->
Some people describe it as something
they knew from a very early age.

Others aren't sure for years.

<!-- S86 -->
Some first have to figure out
that their problem isn't simply
with gender stereotypes.

Others don't question their gender
until adulthood.

<!-- S87 -->
Some suppress those feelings.

Some don't transition.

And some eventually change their minds.

There isn't one universal story.

<!-- S88 -->
Perhaps the most honest answer
to the question:

"How do you know you're trans?"

<!-- S89 -->
is simply:

"Because I tried to understand
why I've felt the way
I've felt for so long."

<!-- S90 -->
For one person, the answer
may come very early.

For another, it may take years.

And someone else may never find
a completely certain answer.

<!-- S91 -->
But we already know one thing:

trans people don't have to look
a certain way.

They don't have to behave
a certain way.

<!-- S92 -->
They don't have to have a particular
sexual orientation.

And they don't have to undergo
any particular treatment.

<!-- S93 -->
But if someone comes to believe
that their body and their gender identity
don't fit together,
another question appears:

<!-- S94 -->
what can they actually do about it?

And that's the story
we'll explore next.
