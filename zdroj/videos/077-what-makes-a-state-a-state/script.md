# What Makes a State a State?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine waking up tomorrow
and discovering that the police
in your neighbourhood
no longer take orders from the government.

<!-- S02 -->
They take orders from someone else.

The government still exists.

The laws still exist.

The courts still exist.

But there is a new question.

Who is actually in charge?

<!-- S03 -->
We all know what a state looks like.

It has borders.

A flag.

A government.

A constitution.

But what actually makes it a state?

<!-- S04 -->
The sociologist Max Weber
had a famous answer.

He described the modern state
as a human community
that successfully claims
the monopoly of the legitimate use
of physical force
within a given territory.

<!-- S05 -->
That sounds extremely academic.

But the basic idea
is surprisingly simple.

A country can contain
millions of different organisations.

<!-- S06 -->
Companies.

Clubs.

Religious organisations.

Private security companies.

Even armed groups.

<!-- S07 -->
But the state says:

"When it comes
to the ultimate use of force,
that authority belongs to me."

<!-- S08 -->
If your neighbour steals your car,
you don't get to create
your own police department.

If someone attacks you,
you don't get to build your own prison.

<!-- S09 -->
And if two people disagree
over who is right,
they aren't supposed to bring
their own armies
and settle the argument.

The state is supposed to handle that.

<!-- S10 -->
Police.

Courts.

Prisons.

The military.

<!-- S11 -->
It's an enormous system
built around one basic function:

making rules enforceable.

<!-- S11b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S12 -->
Now comes the interesting part.

A state can have a constitution.

It can have laws.

It can have a parliament.

It can have impressive
government buildings.

<!-- S13 -->
But if it can't enforce its decisions,
something fundamental has changed.

A rule that nobody can enforce
is still written down.

It just no longer decides anything.

<!-- S14 -->
Imagine a government
passes a law saying:

"Starting tomorrow,
stealing is illegal."

<!-- S15 -->
But the police
don't have enough people.

The courts don't function.

The prisons are empty.

<!-- S16 -->
And armed groups
in different parts of the country
simply do whatever they want.

<!-- S17 -->
The law still exists.

The parliament still exists.

The flag is still flying.

But the state is gradually
losing control.

<!-- S18 -->
And now we have a problem.

Who is actually in charge?

<!-- S19 -->
Something like this can happen
when a central government
becomes too weak
to control its territory.

Other centres of power appear.

<!-- S20 -->
Militias.

Tribal groups.

Warlords.

Criminal organisations.

Religious movements.

<!-- S21 -->
And each one may have its own rules.

In one part of a city,
the government's laws apply.

A few streets away,
an armed group may impose its own.

<!-- S22 -->
And if that group has enough people
and enough weapons,
it can enforce them.

<!-- S23 -->
Suddenly, you have something
that looks surprisingly similar
to a state...

without actually being one.

<!-- S24 -->
Lebanon is an interesting example,
because the situation there
is not simply "there is no state."

<!-- S25 -->
There is a Lebanese government.

There is a national army.

There is a police force.

<!-- S26 -->
But for decades, Hezbollah has kept
its own armed forces,
alongside extensive political
and social structures.

<!-- S27 -->
This has led to Hezbollah
being described
as "a state within a state."

<!-- S28 -->
In March 2026,
that stopped being a theoretical problem.

Hezbollah fired on Israel.

The same day, the Lebanese government
banned its military activities.

<!-- S28b -->
"The decision of war and peace,"
said the prime minister,
"rests exclusively with the state."

The war went on for weeks anyway.

<!-- S29 -->
So if the government
has the legal authority to use force...

but another organisation
can start a war from its territory
without the government's consent...

who actually has the monopoly?

<!-- S30 -->
And then there is
an even more extreme example.

The Islamic State.

<!-- S31 -->
When ISIS controlled
large parts of Iraq and Syria,
it wasn't simply a gang
hiding weapons somewhere.

<!-- S32 -->
It had an army.

Police.

Courts.

Taxation.

Administration.

Its own rules.

<!-- S33 -->
And, most importantly,
the ability to enforce those rules.

<!-- S34 -->
That did not make ISIS
an internationally recognised state.

It wasn't.

<!-- S35 -->
But for a period of time,
in the territory it controlled,
it performed many functions
normally associated with a state.

<!-- S36 -->
And that reveals something important.

A state isn't just a piece of paper.

It also has to actually
control its territory.

<!-- S37 -->
Now imagine something even stranger.

What if the government
disappeared tomorrow?

<!-- S38 -->
The people wouldn't disappear.

Neither would the buildings.

The roads would still be there.

The money would still exist.

<!-- S39 -->
And on the first day,
perhaps almost nothing
would look different.

<!-- S40 -->
People would wake up.

Go to work.

Buy groceries.

Send their children to school.

<!-- S41 -->
And then someone would ask:

"Wait...

who is arresting people today?"

<!-- S42 -->
Because that's the moment
when you discover
what a state actually is.

<!-- S43 -->
It isn't just the president.

It isn't just parliament.

It isn't the flag.

<!-- S44 -->
It's an enormous network
of institutions and people
capable of saying:

"This is the rule."

<!-- S45 -->
And when someone answers:

"I don't care."

there has to be someone
capable of saying:

"Then you're coming with me."

<!-- S46 -->
And that creates a strange paradox.

The more successful a state is,
the less we actually notice
its monopoly on force.

<!-- S47 -->
Most of the time,
nobody is forcing us to do anything.

We follow the rules voluntarily.

<!-- S48 -->
We pay our taxes.

We stop at red lights.

We don't simply take
other people's property.

<!-- S49 -->
Not because there is a police officer
standing behind every person.

<!-- S50 -->
But because everyone knows
that if we cross certain lines...

the state can intervene.

<!-- S51 -->
And that's why a successful state
can appear to use very little force.

Its ability to use force
is part of the reason
it usually doesn't have to.

<!-- S52 -->
So...

what makes a state a state?

<!-- S52b -->
A flag?

<!-- S52c -->
A constitution?

<!-- S52d -->
Borders?

<!-- S52e -->
Elections?

<!-- S52f -->
Perhaps.

<!-- S53 -->
But if a state can no longer
enforce its decisions
across the territory
it claims to control...

something else begins to emerge.

<!-- S54 -->
Parallel power.

Militias.

Warlords.

Criminal organisations.

Competing authorities.

<!-- S55 -->
And eventually, perhaps,
an entirely new state.

<!-- S56 -->
Because history keeps showing us
something uncomfortable.

When two different powers
claim authority over the same territory,
the crucial question is not simply
who has the legal right to rule.

<!-- S57 -->
It's:

who can actually enforce the rules?

<!-- S58 -->
So let's go back to the beginning.

The police in your neighbourhood
take orders from someone else.

Who is in charge?

<!-- S59 -->
The obvious answer is:

the government.

But the deeper answer is:

whoever has enough power
to enforce its authority over you.

<!-- S60 -->
And perhaps that is
one of the most fundamental questions
we can ask about any state.

Who has the final say?

<!-- S61 -->
Inside a country,
the state is supposed to have it.

But who has the final say
over the states themselves?
