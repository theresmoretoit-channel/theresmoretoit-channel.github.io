# Why Do Drones Fly on a Wire?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine a small drone
flying low over a field.

<!-- S02 -->
It looks like any other drone.
But behind it, something is unwinding.

<!-- S03 -->
A thread about as thick
as the thinnest string on a guitar.

<!-- S04 -->
And that thread can be
twenty kilometres long.
Some are now forty.

<!-- S05 -->
The drone isn't talking
to its pilot by radio.
It's connected by a strand of glass.

<!-- S06 -->
In the age of satellites
and wireless everything,
why would anyone fly a drone on a wire?

<!-- S07 -->
The answer starts with a number.

<!-- S08 -->
In 2023, a British defence think tank
estimated that Ukraine was losing
around ten thousand drones a month.

<!-- S09 -->
And a big part of the reason
wasn't guns.
It was jamming.

<!-- S10 -->
A radio drone runs on two signals.
Video goes back to the pilot.
Commands come forward to the drone.

<!-- S11 -->
And a jammer doesn't need
to hack either of them.
It just shouts louder
on the same frequencies.

<!-- S12 -->
The drone can no longer hear its pilot.
It drifts, or it lands,
or it falls out of the sky.

<!-- S13 -->
Designers moved to new frequencies.
The jammers followed.
Then they moved again.

<!-- S14 -->
So someone asked a much simpler question.
What if the drone
didn't use the air at all?

<!-- S15 -->
An optical fibre carries light, not radio.
Pulses of light,
bouncing along inside a strand of glass.

<!-- S16 -->
Commands go out through the fibre.
Video comes back through the same fibre.

<!-- S17 -->
A jammer can fill the sky with noise.
But this drone isn't listening to the sky.

<!-- S18 -->
There's just one part
that sounds impossible.

<!-- S19 -->
Twenty kilometres of cable,
dragged behind a drone
that weighs a few kilograms?

<!-- S20 -->
It isn't dragged.

<!-- S21 -->
The spool rides on the drone,
and the fibre unwinds from it as it flies.
Whatever is already laid out
just lies still on the ground.

<!-- S22 -->
The drone never pulls its tail.
It only lays it down,
the way a spider leaves a thread.

<!-- S23 -->
That's how something this fragile
survives the flight at all.

<!-- S23b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S24 -->
And the idea isn't new.

<!-- S25 -->
For more than half a century,
anti-tank missiles have unspooled
a thin wire behind them.
One of the best known, TOW,
literally ends in "wire-guided".

<!-- S26 -->
In the 1980s, the US Army
replaced that wire with fibre
and put a camera in the nose.

<!-- S27 -->
The missile sent its picture
back down the fibre,
and the operator could steer it
from behind a hill,
seeing the target only on a screen.

<!-- S28 -->
It was cancelled.
Then revived.
Then cancelled again.
Mostly, it was too expensive.

<!-- S29 -->
What changed
is the price of everything else.

<!-- S30 -->
The telecom industry now makes
hundreds of millions of kilometres
of optical fibre every year.

<!-- S31 -->
And a small racing drone
costs a few hundred dollars.

<!-- S32 -->
Put the two together,
and an idea that was too expensive
for a missile programme
becomes cheap enough
to throw away after one flight.

<!-- S33 -->
By 2025, both sides of the war
were using fibre drones in large numbers.

<!-- S34 -->
And it turned out that beating the jammer
was only the first advantage.

<!-- S35 -->
A radio drone isn't just listening.
It's also transmitting.
And so is its pilot.

<!-- S36 -->
Every signal can be picked up,
and traced back to where it came from.

<!-- S37 -->
A pilot on a fibre is silent.
There's nothing in the air to trace.

<!-- S38 -->
And radio struggles
when there's something in the way.
A hill. A forest. A concrete wall.

<!-- S39 -->
Light inside a fibre doesn't care.
Wherever the drone can go,
the signal goes with it.

<!-- S40 -->
Unjammable.
Silent.
Works behind hills and inside buildings.

<!-- S41 -->
So why isn't every drone on a wire?

<!-- S42 -->
Start with the weight.

<!-- S43 -->
The fibre carries information,
not electricity.
The drone still runs on its own battery.

<!-- S44 -->
And the spool has to come along
for the whole flight.
A ten-kilometre spool
weighs about a kilogram.

<!-- S45 -->
That's weight the drone can't carry
as anything else.
They're described as sluggish,
and easily pushed around by wind.

<!-- S46 -->
Then there's the length.

<!-- S47 -->
When the spool is empty,
the drone has reached
the end of its leash.

<!-- S48 -->
And it's not a leash
you can wind back in.
Each fibre is laid once.

<!-- S49 -->
And then there's the tail.

<!-- S50 -->
Imagine flying through a forest
while laying a thread behind you.

<!-- S51 -->
It can catch on branches.
It can snag on wires and buildings.

<!-- S52 -->
So the pilot has two questions,
not one.
Can the drone get there?
And can the fibre?

<!-- S53 -->
But the strangest problem
is that the thread points.

<!-- S54 -->
It lies on the ground,
from the drone
all the way back to the pilot.

<!-- S55 -->
Where it hangs in grass and branches,
it glints in low sunlight.
And from the air,
a line like that can be followed
back to the pilot.

<!-- S56 -->
So the defence changed too.

<!-- S57 -->
If you can't jam a drone,
you have to stop it physically.

<!-- S58 -->
Along some supply roads,
both sides now stretch nets
over the road itself,
for kilometres.

<!-- S59 -->
A tunnel of netting,
because the airwaves
no longer protect you.

<!-- S60 -->
That's the pattern here.
The fibre didn't solve the drone problem.
It moved it.

<!-- S61 -->
An invisible link
that could be jammed
became a physical one
that can be tangled, cut and followed.

<!-- S62 -->
And there's one more place
the problem moved to.
The ground itself.

<!-- S63 -->
One flight leaves kilometres of fibre
lying across the landscape.

<!-- S64 -->
Now multiply that
by every fibre drone
flown on this front.

<!-- S65 -->
In some places, fields and tree lines
shimmer with threads,
like a spider's web
the size of a valley.

<!-- S66 -->
Glass, in a plastic coating.
It doesn't rot.
At most, it crumbles into microplastic.

<!-- S67 -->
And in June 2026,
a photo from eastern Ukraine
spread around the world.

<!-- S68 -->
A bird's nest,
knocked out of a tree by a bomb.
Woven out of grass...
and drone fibre.

<!-- S69 -->
That same month,
the army sent two nests like it
to a war museum in Kyiv.

<!-- S70 -->
Nobody knows yet
how much harm the fibre does to birds.
But long thin threads can wrap
around legs and wings.
Owls have already been found
hanging in them.

<!-- S71 -->
For a century, we've been cutting wires.
Phones, networks, aircraft.
Wireless everything.

<!-- S72 -->
And in the most crowded
radio sky on Earth,
the answer turned out to be
a wire again.

<!-- S73 -->
When the war ends,
the jammers will be switched off,
and the noise in the air
will stop that same day.

<!-- S74 -->
The threads will still be there.
