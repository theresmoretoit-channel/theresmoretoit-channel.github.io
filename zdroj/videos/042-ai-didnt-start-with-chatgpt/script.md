# AI Didn't Start With ChatGPT

<!-- S01 -->
At the end of November 2022,
a web page appeared
with a small box on it.

You typed a question.

And a computer answered.

<!-- S02 -->
Not with a pre-written reply.

With a sentence.

It could write an email.

Explain mathematics.

Translate a page.

Write code.

<!-- S03 -->
And it could hold something
that felt uncomfortably
like a conversation.

It was called ChatGPT.

And for millions of people,
this was the moment
artificial intelligence began.

<!-- S04 -->
Except a computer had done
something like this before.

<!-- S04b -->
Fifty-six years earlier.

<!-- S05 -->
In 1966, a researcher at MIT
called Joseph Weizenbaum
published a program called ELIZA.

<!-- S06 -->
You could type:

"My boyfriend says I'm
depressed much of the time."

And ELIZA would answer:

"I am sorry to hear
you are depressed."

And you could carry on.

And so would the program.

<!-- S07 -->
ELIZA was not intelligent.

It was not a neural network.

It understood nothing at all.

<!-- S08 -->
It matched keywords in what you typed
and handed the rest back
as a question.

The whole script fitted
in the appendix of a journal paper.

<!-- S08b -->
People talked to it anyway.

<!-- S09 -->
Weizenbaum's own secretary
had watched him build it.

She knew exactly what it was.

<!-- S09b -->
After a few exchanges with it,
she asked him to leave the room.

She wanted to be alone with it.

<!-- S10 -->
Which leaves a question
that the next half century
kept walking into.

<!-- S10b -->
How little does a machine
actually need

to seem intelligent?

<!-- S11 -->
Somebody had already asked
a version of that.

Sixteen years earlier.

In 1950, the British mathematician
Alan Turing opened a paper
with a question.

<!-- S11b -->
"Can machines think?"

<!-- S12 -->
And then he did something clever.

He decided the question
was too vague to answer,

and swapped it for a different one.

<!-- S13 -->
Put a person and a machine
at the far end of a conversation.

If you cannot tell which is which,

does it still matter
whether the machine
is really thinking?

<!-- S14 -->
It is an elegant argument.

And ELIZA is what happens
when you take it literally.

<!-- S15 -->
Because ELIZA convinced people
in 1966,

and there was nothing
behind it at all.

<!-- S16 -->
Six years after Turing's paper,
a group of scientists spent
the summer at Dartmouth College.

Their proposal used a phrase
that nobody had written down before.

Artificial intelligence.

<!-- S17 -->
And their ambition was enormous.

If we could describe
human intelligence
precisely enough,

we could program a machine
to reproduce it.

<!-- S18 -->
A second idea had been around
even longer, and it pointed
the opposite way.

<!-- S18b -->
In 1943, a neurophysiologist
and a logician wrote down a simple
model of a biological neuron.

<!-- S19 -->
It took a few numbers in.

Gave each one a weight.

Added them up.

And decided whether to fire.

<!-- S19b -->
On its own it can do
almost nothing.

<!-- S20 -->
Its weights were fixed by hand.

Two years after Dartmouth,
a psychologist built one
that could work them out for itself.

<!-- S20b -->
And the idea underneath that was this.

Maybe we do not program
intelligence at all.

Maybe we build enough simple units,
wire them together,

and let the system learn.

<!-- S21 -->
None of it worked.

Not because the ideas were wrong.

Because the machines
were nowhere near ready.

<!-- S22 -->
Computers were slow.

Memory was tiny.

And there was almost nothing
to learn from.

Training a large network
was not difficult.

It was impossible.

<!-- S23 -->
And there was a second problem,
which was worse,
because nobody had predicted it.

A computer could do arithmetic
faster than every human
who had ever lived.

<!-- S24 -->
But ask it to do something
a five-year-old does
without thinking about it,

and it fell apart.

<!-- S25 -->
Recognise a dog in a photograph.

For a person, instant.

For a computer, hopeless.

<!-- S26 -->
Eventually the money noticed.

Funding was cut.

The excitement faded.

The field went through long stretches
that later earned a name.

AI winters.

<!-- S27 -->
Artificial intelligence
did not disappear.

It just stopped being interesting.

<!-- S28 -->
What changed was not
a brilliant new idea.

From the 1990s onwards,
computers got dramatically faster
and storage got cheap.

<!-- S29 -->
And the world began producing
enormous amounts of digital data.

Web pages. Photographs.
Scanned books. Video. Text.

<!-- S30 -->
For the first time
there was something
for a machine to learn from.

And the question quietly turned over.

<!-- S31 -->
Not:

"How do we program
all of the rules into the computer?"

But:

"What if we hand it the data
and let it work out the rules?"

<!-- S32 -->
In 1997, a machine called Deep Blue
beat Garry Kasparov,
the world chess champion.

<!-- S33 -->
It was enormous news.

And it is worth being precise
about what it proved.

Deep Blue could not hold
a conversation.

It could not recognise a dog.

It could not learn anything.

<!-- S34 -->
It was extraordinarily good
at exactly one thing.

ELIZA looked intelligent
and was not.

Deep Blue was superhuman
and still was not.

<!-- S35 -->
Both of them broke
the same assumption.

That if something looks like thinking,

there must be thinking behind it.

<!-- S36 -->
Now, the dog.

In 2012, a neural network
called AlexNet was pointed
at more than a million photographs.

<!-- S37 -->
It was not a new idea.

Neural networks were older
than most of the people
working on them.

<!-- S38 -->
What was new
was everything around it.

Enough data.

Enough computing power.

Better ways to train it.

<!-- S39 -->
It did not just win.

It won by a margin that made
the older approaches look finished.

The thing a five-year-old could do
and a computer could not,

a computer could suddenly do.

<!-- S40 -->
And the question changed again.

Not "can neural networks work?"

But "how far do they go?"

<!-- S41 -->
In 2017, a paper came out
of Google with an odd title.

"Attention Is All You Need."

It described a new architecture
for neural networks,

called the transformer.

<!-- S41b -->
It let a model read a whole sentence
at once, and work out
which words belonged with which.

<!-- S42 -->
Almost everything you have heard of
since then is built on it.

GPT. BERT. Claude. Gemini.

And still, outside the field,
nobody had noticed anything.

<!-- S43 -->
OpenAI began building a family
of models called GPT.

<!-- S44 -->
Each one was larger than the last

and better at the same trick.

Given some text,
guess what comes next.

<!-- S45 -->
By GPT-3 that trick had turned
into something
nobody quite expected.

It could answer a question.

Finish an argument.

Write an essay.

Generate code.

<!-- S45b -->
And almost nobody used it.

<!-- S46 -->
It was a research tool.

You needed an account,
some documentation,
and a reason.

The technology was there.

The door was not.

<!-- S47 -->
On the 30th of November 2022,
OpenAI put a text box
on a web page.

<!-- S47b -->
That is close to the whole story.

<!-- S48 -->
It was not a new kind
of artificial intelligence.

It was not the first neural network.

And it certainly was not
the first chatbot.

<!-- S48b -->
ELIZA was doing that job
fifty-six years earlier.

<!-- S49 -->
What changed was that you
no longer had to understand any of it.

You typed. It answered.

<!-- S49b -->
Five days later,
a million people had signed up.

<!-- S50 -->
When people say
AI started with ChatGPT,

it is a little like saying
the internet started with Google.

It did not.

Google just made it
enormously easier to use.

<!-- S51 -->
And underneath ChatGPT
there is a list,

and every item on it
took decades.

<!-- S52 -->
Computing power.

Huge amounts of data.

Neural networks.

Better ways of training them.

Transformers.

And finally, a simple interface.

<!-- S53 -->
So when did artificial intelligence
actually begin?

<!-- S53b -->
2022?

No.

<!-- S53c -->
2017?

Not really.

<!-- S53d -->
1956?

Closer.

<!-- S54 -->
But even in 1956
nobody was starting from nothing.

The models of neurons
were already written down.

Turing had already asked
whether the question made sense.

<!-- S55 -->
And people had been telling stories
about artificial minds
for a very long time before that.

<!-- S55b -->
There is probably no first day.

<!-- S58 -->
Think about that secretary
at MIT in 1966,

asking to be left alone
with a few pages of pattern matching.

<!-- S59 -->
The machines have become
unimaginably better.

We have not become
much harder to convince.

<!-- S60 -->
So perhaps ChatGPT was not
the beginning of the age of AI.

Perhaps it was simply the moment
everybody finally noticed.

<!-- S61 -->
And perhaps the interesting question
was never when we invented this.

It is how long it takes us
to work out what we have built.
