<!-- S01 -->
Why do so many people hate Windows?

<!-- S02 -->
Windows runs on more than
a billion computers.
For most of those people
it is simply the thing that starts
when they press the button.

<!-- S03 -->
They open a browser.
Watch YouTube.
Write a document.
Play a game.

<!-- S04 -->
And they never think about
what Windows is doing inside.
Or about the fact
that anything else exists.

<!-- S05 -->
But some people can talk for an hour
about why Windows is terrible.

<!-- S06 -->
Some of them run Linux.
Some of them have a Mac.
Some simply don't
like Microsoft.

<!-- S07 -->
So what exactly bothers them?
And why might none of it
make any sense to you?

<!-- S08 -->
Let's start with something concrete.

<!-- S09 -->
Some programs written in the 1990s
still run on Windows today.
Thirty years later,
on hardware that didn't exist
when they were written.

<!-- S10 -->
Almost nothing else you own
can say that.

<!-- S11 -->
And here is the strange part.
That is also one of the reasons
people call Windows a mess.

<!-- S12 -->
The thing they complain about
and the thing that keeps it working
are the same thing.

<!-- S13 -->
But first, let's start with you.

<!-- S14 -->
Imagine you buy a washing machine.
You plug it in.
You select a programme.
It washes your clothes.

<!-- S15 -->
You don't want to read its source code.
You don't want to write
your own spin cycle.
You want clean clothes.

<!-- S16 -->
And that is how most people
see their computer.
The computer works.
Done.
And there is nothing wrong with that.

<!-- S17 -->
But some people want something more.
And to see why,
we have to look at how Windows
was actually built.

<!-- S18 -->
Today's Windows was never designed
as one system.
It is the result of more than
forty years of building.

<!-- S19 -->
It started as a graphical shell
running on top of MS-DOS.
Almost everything since then
has been added on top.

<!-- S20 -->
And Microsoft had one enormous
constraint.
Compatibility.

<!-- S21 -->
Imagine a building
that has been repaired and extended
for thirty years.

<!-- S22 -->
The original wiring is outdated.
Some pipes run in
completely illogical places.
Today you would design it
differently.

<!-- S23 -->
But people still live there.
Businesses still operate there.
So you can't demolish it
and start again.

<!-- S24 -->
You add a floor.
You fit a lift.
And you keep the old pipes,
because nobody may touch them.

<!-- S25 -->
Windows can be a bit like that.

<!-- S26 -->
A program written twenty years ago
may still expect
a particular interface,
a particular behaviour,
a particular file format.

<!-- S27 -->
And Microsoft can't simply delete
the old parts.
Because somewhere in the world
a company still depends on them.

<!-- S28 -->
That is the paradox.
Backward compatibility is
one of Windows' greatest strengths.
And one of the reasons it feels like
a technological patchwork.

<!-- S29 -->
And some versions of Windows
really didn't help.

<!-- S30 -->
Windows 95.
Windows 98.
Windows ME.

<!-- S31 -->
Drivers.
Conflicts.
Crashes.
Restarts.
Blue screens.

<!-- S32 -->
Installing a new piece of hardware
could feel like
a small engineering project.

<!-- S33 -->
And Windows earned a reputation.
Windows is buggy.
Windows crashes.
Windows needs reinstalling.

<!-- S34 -->
But underneath, Microsoft had
a second, sturdier line.
Windows NT.
And in 2001, Windows XP
finally brought it
to ordinary home computers.

<!-- S35 -->
Windows became a far more
stable system.
But the reputation stayed.

<!-- S36 -->
If a restaurant serves you
one genuinely terrible meal,
you can come back ten years later
and find it excellent.

<!-- S37 -->
And you will still think:
I once had the worst goulash
of my life in here.

<!-- S38 -->
And then there is Microsoft itself.

<!-- S39 -->
Windows became the dominant
operating system
for personal computers.
And with dominance comes power.

<!-- S40 -->
Microsoft ended up in a long series
of fights with its competitors.
Most famously the antitrust case
around Internet Explorer
and Netscape.

<!-- S41 -->
So there is a second reason
people dislike Windows.
Not because it crashed on them.
But because of how the company
behaved when it was winning.

<!-- S42 -->
So you can dislike Windows
without having any problem
with its technology.

<!-- S43 -->
But now we get to something deeper.

<!-- S44 -->
Imagine you buy a car.
It is yours.

<!-- S45 -->
And then the manufacturer says:
some of its functions
can be changed by an update.

<!-- S46 -->
Some parts have to be paired
with the car's electronics.

<!-- S47 -->
And some things simply aren't possible,
because we decided
not to allow them.

<!-- S48 -->
Most people won't care.
They will never open the bonnet.

<!-- S49 -->
But some people will say:
wait.
I bought this.
Why are you telling me
what I am allowed to do with it?

<!-- S50 -->
And that feeling gets sharper
the day the manufacturer announces
it is dropping a feature
you were using.

<!-- S51 -->
You can complain.
You can buy a different car.
But you cannot take their software
and keep the feature working.

<!-- S52 -->
And this is where
the philosophy of free software begins.

<!-- S53 -->
Windows is proprietary software.
Closed source.

<!-- S54 -->
Microsoft gives you
the finished program.
You don't get
its complete source code.

<!-- S55 -->
It is a car where the manufacturer says:
this is how we built it.
Trust us that it works properly.

<!-- S56 -->
And most people will say: fine.
Just as most people never open up
their car's control unit.

<!-- S57 -->
But some people will say:
I would like to look.

<!-- S58 -->
With open source you can read the code.
Study it.
Change it.
Fix it.
And if the licence allows it,
build your own version.

<!-- S59 -->
Closed source doesn't mean
that software cannot be checked at all.

<!-- S60 -->
You can watch how a program behaves.
You can watch its network traffic.
You can take the binary apart,
all the way down to
processor instructions.

<!-- S61 -->
But that is dismantling
the finished car
instead of reading
the service manual.

<!-- S62 -->
In theory, you can do it.
In practice, it is much harder.

<!-- S63 -->
So closed software asks you
for a certain amount of trust
in the company that made it.

<!-- S64 -->
And that, far more than any crash,
is what some people
are actually objecting to.

<!-- S65 -->
Because the argument
was never really about bugs.
It was about who decides.

<!-- S65b -->
By the way, if you'd like to see
where the next question takes us,
consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S66 -->
Which is why Linux
isn't simply Windows for free.
It is also a different philosophy.

<!-- S67 -->
Windows tells you:
this is how we designed it.

<!-- S68 -->
Linux tends to say:
here is the toolbox.

<!-- S69 -->
You choose a distribution.
A desktop environment.
Your programs.
And if you want to,
the system itself.

<!-- S69b -->
But you don't have to.
Install a mainstream distribution today
and you may never open
a terminal at all.

<!-- S70 -->
And if you are feeling brave,
you can still break something
you didn't understand
five minutes ago...
and then spend three hours
finding out why.

<!-- S71 -->
That is Linux too.
But it is an option,
not the price of entry.

<!-- S72 -->
For some people that freedom
is the whole point.
For everyone else
it simply sits there
until they need it.

<!-- S73 -->
And now an interesting paradox.

<!-- S74 -->
If closed source is your problem,
you can move to Linux.
But if convenience is what you want,
you can buy an Apple product.

<!-- S75 -->
Apple builds the hardware
and the software together,
so it has far more control
over the whole ecosystem.

<!-- S76 -->
And that has real advantages.
Things work together.
You don't have to think
about the technical details.

<!-- S77 -->
And that is exactly
what some people want.
They don't want to be
the administrator of their computer.
They want to be its user.

<!-- S78 -->
To someone else
the same sentence is the problem.
The more decisions
the manufacturer makes for you,
the fewer are left to you.

<!-- S79 -->
And there is one more thing
people hold against Apple.
Every year,
something is revolutionary again.

<!-- S80 -->
For some people that is part of the fun.
For others it is exactly why
they can't stand it.
They don't want a computer
to become a lifestyle.

<!-- S81 -->
Windows says:
you want compatibility?
Here is a system that has to work
with an enormous number of things.

<!-- S82 -->
Apple says:
you want it all to fit together?
We will decide how.

<!-- S83 -->
Linux says:
you want control?
Here you go.
As much or as little as you like.

<!-- S84 -->
And none of those
is automatically the right one.

<!-- S85 -->
There is one more reason
people actively dislike Windows.
And it has nothing to do
with the 1990s.

<!-- S86 -->
The feeling that the system
is constantly offering you something.

<!-- S87 -->
Updates.
OneDrive.
A Microsoft account.
Edge.
Notifications.
Features you never asked for.

<!-- S88 -->
And you might think:
I bought this computer.
Why does it keep trying
to sell me something?

<!-- S89 -->
There are obviously reasons
behind those decisions.
Cloud services.
Security.
One ecosystem.
Money.

<!-- S90 -->
But from where the user is standing,
the result is very simple.
Just let me use my computer.

<!-- S91 -->
So who is actually right?

<!-- S92 -->
Maybe everyone.
Because people arguing
about operating systems
are usually not
answering the same question.

<!-- S93 -->
One of them says:
I just want it to work.
The other says:
I want to know how it works.

<!-- S94 -->
One wants somebody else
to take care of it.
The other wants nobody
telling them what they can do.
Convenience, against control.

<!-- S95 -->
And most people want neither.
They want to turn the computer on,
open a browser,
and have it work.
Which is a perfectly
legitimate thing to want.

<!-- S96 -->
So maybe the better question
isn't which operating system is best.

<!-- S97 -->
Maybe it is:
what do you actually want
from your computer?

<!-- S98 -->
Convenience?
Compatibility?
Freedom?
Control?

<!-- S99 -->
Or do you just want it
to launch your game
when you turn it on?

<!-- S100 -->
Because in the end,
it is just a computer.

<!-- S101 -->
Well...
at least for most people.
