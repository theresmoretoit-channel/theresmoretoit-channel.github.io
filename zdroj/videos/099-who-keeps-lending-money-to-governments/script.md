# Who Keeps Lending Money to Governments?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine a country
that spends more than it collects.

<!-- S02 -->
Not once.

<!-- S02b -->
Every year.

<!-- S03 -->
Hospitals, schools, pensions, roads.
And every year, the money runs out
before the bills do.

<!-- S04 -->
So the government borrows.
The next year, it borrows again.
And again the year after that.

<!-- S05 -->
Meanwhile, the old loans
don't go away.
They come due, one after another.

<!-- S06 -->
So who keeps lending it the money?
And if the debt never seems to shrink,
how does any of this keep working?

<!-- S07 -->
Let's start with a country
where it stopped working.

<!-- S08 -->
In 2009, Greece owed
nearly one hundred and thirty percent
of what its economy produced in a year.

<!-- S09 -->
Lenders panicked.
Within two years, they were asking
for interest no government could pay.

<!-- S10 -->
In 2012, Greece went through
the biggest government debt
restructuring in history.

<!-- S11 -->
Today, Greece owes even more.
About one hundred and forty-six percent.

<!-- S12 -->
And for most of the past year,
it has borrowed more cheaply than France.
On ten-year bonds.

<!-- S13 -->
Same country.

<!-- S13b -->
More debt.

<!-- S13c -->
Cheaper money.

<!-- S14 -->
So the size of the debt
isn't the whole story.
What matters just as much
is who's lending, and why.

<!-- S15 -->
So let's follow the money.

<!-- S15b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S16 -->
First, two words
that get mixed up all the time.
Deficit and debt.

<!-- S17 -->
Imagine a government that collects
one hundred billion in taxes,
but spends one hundred and ten.

<!-- S18 -->
That gap of ten billion
is the deficit.
It's what happens in one year.

<!-- S19 -->
The debt is everything
it still owes
from all the years before.

<!-- S20 -->
A deficit is like water
running into a bathtub.
The debt is the water already in it.

<!-- S21 -->
So a country can have a lot of debt
and no deficit at all.
Or very little debt,
and a deficit every year.

<!-- S22 -->
Let's see what that looks like
in two real countries.

<!-- S23 -->
To compare them, we don't count debt
in kroner or dollars.
We measure it against the size
of the whole economy: its GDP.

<!-- S24 -->
A debt of a hundred billion dollars
would crush a small country,
and barely register in a huge one.

<!-- S25 -->
Denmark first.
At the end of 2025, its government owed
twenty-seven point nine percent of GDP.

<!-- S26 -->
And in 2025, Denmark did
what governments supposedly never do.
It collected more than it spent.

<!-- S27 -->
A surplus
of two point nine percent.
And not for the first time.

<!-- S28 -->
Since 2022, its debt has fallen
from thirty-three point three percent
to under twenty-eight.

<!-- S29 -->
A surplus can help
reduce government debt.
But it doesn't mean
the debt disappears.

<!-- S30 -->
Now let's look at the United States.

<!-- S31 -->
In 2025, its total government debt
stood at roughly
one hundred and twenty-four percent
of GDP.

<!-- S32 -->
And it ran a deficit
of about six point eight percent.
That's a very different picture
from Denmark.

<!-- S32b -->
The debt-to-GDP ratio can fall
even while the amount owed
is still growing,
if the economy grows faster.

<!-- S32c -->
But the percentage alone
doesn't tell us
whether a debt is sustainable.

<!-- S32d -->
For that, we need to look at
who owns it, what it costs to service,
and how the economy evolves.

<!-- S33 -->
Now let's widen the picture
across Europe.

<!-- S34 -->
Estonia: twenty-four percent.
Denmark: twenty-eight.
Czech Republic: forty-four.
Germany: sixty-three and a half.

<!-- S35 -->
France: one hundred and sixteen.
Italy: one hundred and thirty-seven.
Greece: one hundred and forty-six.

<!-- S36 -->
Six times as much,
from one end to the other.
And every one of them
still owes money to someone.

<!-- S37 -->
So who on earth
is lending them the money?

<!-- S38 -->
Governments don't usually
walk into a bank and ask for a loan.
They sell bonds.

<!-- S39 -->
A bond is a promise on paper.
You give the government money today.
It pays you interest,
and gives the money back on a fixed date.

<!-- S40 -->
Say you buy a ten-year bond
for one thousand dollars.
The government takes your thousand
and spends it.

<!-- S41 -->
You get interest payments every year,
and your thousand dollars back
in ten years' time.

<!-- S42 -->
So who buys them?

<!-- S42b -->
Banks. Pension funds.
Insurance companies. Investment funds.
Foreign governments. Central banks.

<!-- S43 -->
And here's the part
that's easy to miss.

<!-- S44 -->
A pension fund doesn't hold bonds
for its own sake.
It holds them for the people
who'll one day draw a pension.

<!-- S45 -->
If you pay into a pension,
or keep savings in a fund,
there's a good chance
you're one of the lenders.

<!-- S46 -->
The government's debt
is somebody's savings.
Very often, it's yours.

<!-- S47 -->
And that brings us to Japan.

<!-- S48 -->
Japan's government owes
more than twice its entire economy.
Far more than Greece.

<!-- S49 -->
But about nine-tenths of it
is held at home.
By its banks, its insurers,
its pension funds.

<!-- S50 -->
And the Bank of Japan,
which holds nearly half of its bonds.

<!-- S51 -->
That changes the risks.
But it doesn't make them disappear.

<!-- S52 -->
Greece in 2009 was the opposite.
Most of its debt was held
by foreign investors, many of them banks,
who could sell up and leave.

<!-- S53 -->
And Greece used the euro.
It couldn't create the money
its debts were written in.

<!-- S54 -->
Today, more than half of Greek debt
is owed to other eurozone governments,
through rescue funds,
on very long terms and at low rates.

<!-- S55 -->
Same country. Different lenders.
That's a big part of why it now borrows
more cheaply than France.

<!-- S56 -->
But that leaves the other question.
If governments keep borrowing,
when do they ever pay it back?

<!-- S57 -->
Imagine a government
with one billion dollars of bonds
coming due this year.

<!-- S58 -->
It has to pay those lenders back.
But it doesn't need to find
a billion dollars in taxes.

<!-- S59 -->
It sells one billion dollars
of new bonds,
and uses that money
to repay the old ones.

<!-- S60 -->
The old bonds are gone.
The lenders are paid in full.
And the debt is exactly the same size.

<!-- S61 -->
This is called refinancing.
And governments do it all the time.

<!-- S62 -->
Most governments refinance
a large part of their maturing debt.
Individual bonds come due,
but the total debt
doesn't have to reach zero.

<!-- S63 -->
In 2015, Britain redeemed
its last four undated government bonds.

<!-- S64 -->
Some of the historic liabilities
behind them
could be traced back to 1720.

<!-- S66 -->
But refinancing has a catch.
It only works if somebody
is willing to buy the new bonds.

<!-- S67 -->
And at an interest rate
the government can afford.

<!-- S68 -->
When lenders get nervous,
they ask for more interest.

<!-- S69 -->
Every cheap old bond that comes due
is replaced by an expensive new one,
and the interest bill starts to climb.

<!-- S70 -->
A bigger interest bill
makes the deficit worse.
A worse deficit makes lenders
more nervous.

<!-- S71 -->
And nervous lenders
ask for even more.

<!-- S72 -->
That's the spiral Greece fell into.
By early 2012, lenders wanted
around thirty percent a year.
At times, far more.

<!-- S73 -->
It ended the only way it could.
Private lenders lost
more than half of what they were owed.

<!-- S74 -->
Japan has sustained a much higher
debt ratio than Greece had
before its crisis.

<!-- S74b -->
But debt ratios alone
don't tell us when a country
will get into trouble.

<!-- S75 -->
What matters is whether lenders believe
they'll keep getting paid.

<!-- S76 -->
So why don't governments
just pay everything off?

<!-- S77 -->
Some have come close.
In the early 2000s, Australia
was paying off its debt so fast
it expected to be clear
within a few years.

<!-- S78 -->
So it asked whether it would
still need to sell bonds at all.

<!-- S79 -->
It decided to keep selling them.
Not to pay for the budget,
but because financial markets
relied on them.

<!-- S80 -->
Futures contracts were tied to them,
and traders used them
to manage interest rate risk.

<!-- S80b -->
By 2007, what the government owned
outweighed what it owed.
And it still kept selling bonds.

<!-- S81 -->
So a government doesn't sell bonds
only because it's short of money.
A working bond market
can be worth keeping for its own sake.

<!-- S82 -->
So, who keeps lending money
to governments?

<!-- S83 -->
Banks. Pension funds. Insurers.
Other governments.
And, quite often, you.

<!-- S84 -->
They can keep replacing
maturing debt with new borrowing.
But that only works while they can
find lenders on terms they can afford.

<!-- S85 -->
So next time you hear
that a country owes trillions,
don't just ask how it will ever pay.

<!-- S86 -->
Ask who's lending it the money.
And whether they'll still be there
next year.

<!-- S87 -->
Because government debt
is a chain of promises.

<!-- S88 -->
It holds for as long
as people believe it.
Until they don't.
