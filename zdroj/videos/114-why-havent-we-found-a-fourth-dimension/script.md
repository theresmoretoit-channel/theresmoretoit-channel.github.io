# Why Haven't We Found a Fourth Spatial Dimension?
**Script v0.6**

<!-- S01 -->
Close the door of your room.
Shut the windows.
The air inside stays inside.
It doesn't seep away
in a direction you can't point to.

<!-- S02 -->
That sounds too obvious to say.
But last time, we imagined
a fourth direction of space,
one that would let you
reach into a locked safe
without opening it.

<!-- S03 -->
So if that direction is real,
why isn't everything
leaking into it?
The air from your room.
The heat from your coffee.
You.

<!-- S04 -->
Part of the answer,
we already have.
A big, open fourth dimension
would wreck the solar system.
Planets couldn't hold their orbits.

<!-- S05 -->
So if there's one at all,
it has to be hidden.
Curled up too small to notice,
or out of reach,
because we're stuck to our three.

<!-- S06 -->
And for the second idea,
there may be a clue
hanging on your fridge.

<!-- S07 -->
A small magnet
holds up a sheet of paper.
Pulling down on that paper
is the gravity of the entire Earth.
And the magnet wins.

<!-- S08 -->
Take two protons.
The electric push between them
is stronger than their gravity
by about a trillion
trillion trillion times.

<!-- S09 -->
Physicists call this
the hierarchy problem.
Why is gravity so absurdly weak?
Nobody knows.

<!-- S10 -->
In 1998, three physicists
offered a startling answer.
Maybe gravity isn't weak at all.
Maybe it only looks weak to us.

<!-- S11 -->
Picture our universe
as ink on a page,
like last time.
Atoms, light and people
are stuck to the page.
Gravity isn't.

<!-- S12 -->
It spreads into the space
around the page as well.
So by the time it reaches us,
it's diluted.
It feels feeble,
because it's shared with directions
we can't see.

<!-- S12b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S13 -->
It's only an idea.
But it's a useful one,
because it makes predictions.
And predictions can be checked.

<!-- S14 -->
And that gives us a test.
Far apart, gravity follows
the old rule.
Double the distance,
and it gets four times weaker.

<!-- S15 -->
But bring two objects closer
than the size of those hidden dimensions,
and gravity would suddenly
grow stronger than it should.

<!-- S16 -->
In 1998, nobody could rule it out,
because nobody had measured gravity
at distances that small.

<!-- S17 -->
It's brutally hard to do.
At that scale, gravity is drowned out
by almost everything else.
Stray electric charges.
Vibration.
A change in temperature.

<!-- S18 -->
In Seattle, a team
at the University of Washington
hangs a thin disc from a wire,
and turns another disc beneath it.
If gravity changed at short range,
the hanging disc would twist
in a telltale rhythm.

<!-- S19 -->
In 2020, they reported
that gravity still follows the old rule
down to fifty-two micrometres.
That's thinner than a human hair.

<!-- S20 -->
So any hidden dimensions
that gravity leaks into
must be smaller than that.
And the version from 1998
that needed them a millimetre wide
is ruled out.

<!-- S21 -->
If gravity really is strong
at tiny distances,
then smashing particles together
hard enough
could let gravity take over.
Theory said the result
could be a microscopic black hole.

<!-- S22 -->
At the Large Hadron Collider,
physicists went looking
for those black holes on purpose.
Finding one would have meant
gravity leaks into hidden dimensions.

<!-- S23 -->
They also looked for something subtler.
A collision where some energy
seems to vanish,
as if something had slipped
off the page and carried it away.

<!-- S24 -->
But missing energy
isn't a photograph
of another dimension.
Ghostly particles called neutrinos
slip out of the detector too.
So every event like that
has to be checked
against everything we already know.

<!-- S25 -->
So far, the books balance.
No black holes.
No missing energy
beyond what known physics explains.

<!-- S26 -->
The third test came from
a hundred and thirty million
light-years away.
In August 2017,
detectors on Earth picked up
a ripple in space itself.

<!-- S27 -->
Two neutron stars,
the collapsed cores of dead stars,
had spiralled into each other
and merged.
Less than two seconds later,
a space telescope caught
a burst of gamma rays
from the same patch of sky.

<!-- S28 -->
For the first time,
the same event had been seen
in gravitational waves and in light.
And that let physicists ask
a very simple question.

<!-- S29 -->
If gravity leaked into
extra dimensions along the way,
the ripple would arrive
fainter than it should.
The galaxy would seem further away
in gravitational waves than in light.

<!-- S30 -->
It didn't.

<!-- S30b -->
The two distances agreed.

<!-- S31 -->
Across a hundred and thirty
million light-years,
the ripple faded
just as it would
if gravity had only our three
dimensions to spread through.
If any of it leaked,
it was too little to see.

<!-- S32 -->
So here's the scorecard.
On a lab bench, no leak.
In the collider, no leak.
Across the universe, no leak.

<!-- S33 -->
That doesn't mean
there's no fourth dimension.
It means the hiding places
we've checked are empty,
and the ones left are smaller,
or stranger.

<!-- S34 -->
The oldest hiding place
is still open.
A dimension curled up
far smaller than any experiment
can reach.

<!-- S35 -->
Even then,
it might leave a trace.
In some theories,
a particle moving around
that tiny circle
would show up to us
as a heavier copy
of a familiar particle.

<!-- S36 -->
That doesn't mean an electron
is secretly a tiny
four-dimensional ball.
It's a specific prediction:
new particles, at particular masses.
None has been found.

<!-- S37 -->
String theory offers one reason
physicists still take this seriously.
Its consistent versions
require extra dimensions.
But a mathematical requirement
is not evidence that they're real.

<!-- S38 -->
Remember the square in Flatland.
He learned about the third dimension
because a sphere came to visit.

<!-- S39 -->
We're not waiting for a visitor.
We're looking for footprints.
A pull that's slightly too strong
at the width of a hair.
A collision that doesn't add up.
A ripple that arrives too faint.

<!-- S40 -->
Meanwhile, the magnet on your fridge
is still beating the whole planet.
And nobody knows why.

<!-- S41 -->
So the question has changed.
It's no longer
what another dimension
would look like.
It's what it could do
that would finally give it away.
