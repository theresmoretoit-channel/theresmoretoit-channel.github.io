# It Still Works. So Why Upgrade It?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine a company
that has been building software
for twenty years.

Its programmers know
what technical debt is.

<!-- S02 -->
They know what outdated
technology means.

They know that support eventually ends.

They know that one day
the system will have to be upgraded.

And yet...

<!-- S03 -->
...every so often,
someone still has to explain
why we should upgrade.

Welcome to IT.

<!-- S04 -->
The most popular argument is:

"But it works."

And yes.

It does.

<!-- S05 -->
Windows XP worked.

A phone from 2008 can still work.

A twenty-year-old car can still work.

<!-- S06 -->
That doesn't necessarily mean
you should build
the next ten years of your business
around it.

<!-- S07 -->
Because "it works"
is not a property of your software.

It's a property of your software
and everything around it.

Today.

<!-- S08 -->
Software doesn't exist in isolation.

Every application depends on
languages, frameworks, libraries,
operating systems, databases and servers.

And all of those things keep changing.

<!-- S09 -->
Here's a real example.

Imagine a device
that uses a 2G mobile network.

It works perfectly.

Then the operator announces:

"2G is going away."

<!-- S10 -->
And you say:

"But my device still works!"

Yes.

But it needs the world around it.

And the world has moved on.

<!-- S11 -->
Now let's make this ridiculous.

Imagine your water company announces:

"Starting next year,
we're switching to Water 2.0."

<!-- S12 -->
Your pipes still work.

Your sink still works.

Your toilet still works.

Everything is fine.

<!-- S13 -->
Except your plumbing
only supports Water 1.0.

So you have two choices.

Replace your plumbing...

or pay extra
to keep receiving the old water.

Forever.

<!-- S14 -->
Sounds ridiculous.

And yet that is roughly what happens
with legacy technology.

<!-- S15 -->
Old versions stop being supported.

New software stops working with them.

And eventually you pay extra
just to keep something obsolete alive.

<!-- S16 -->
Let's make it even more ridiculous.

Imagine a company
that makes office chairs.

They release Chair 8.

Your company has Chair 6.

<!-- S17 -->
It works perfectly.

It has four legs.

It has a backrest.

Everything is fine.

<!-- S18 -->
Then the manufacturer announces:

"Chair 6 is now out of support."

No more replacement parts.

No more safety fixes.

<!-- S19 -->
You say:

"Why should we buy new chairs?

These ones work!"

Fair enough.

You can keep using Chair 6.

<!-- S20 -->
But eventually you discover
that the new desks don't fit.

The replacement legs
aren't available.

And the only person
who knew how to repair Chair 6
retired five years ago.

<!-- S21 -->
Fortunately, there is a solution.

Enterprise Extended Chair Support.

<!-- S22 -->
For several million a year,
the manufacturer will keep making
replacement legs
for your obsolete chairs.

<!-- S23 -->
Completely absurd.

But replace "chair" with "software"...

and suddenly nobody
finds it strange.

<!-- S24 -->
Except this part is not a joke.

When Windows XP ran out
of support in 2014,
Microsoft sold exactly that deal.

<!-- S25 -->
Custom support.

Around two hundred dollars
per machine for the first year.

Double that for the second.

<!-- S26 -->
The British public sector signed it.

Five and a half million pounds
for one more year
of security updates.

<!-- S27 -->
A year later
they decided not to renew.

And the old machines
were still there.

<!-- S28 -->
That is Enterprise Extended Chair Support.

It just stops being funny
when the invoice is real.

<!-- S29 -->
This is also why the question:

"What will the upgrade actually give us?"

can be misleading.

A new feature?
Users see it.

A new button?

<!-- S29b -->
Users see it.

A technology upgrade?

<!-- S29c -->
Nothing.

And that is exactly the point.

<!-- S30 -->
A successful upgrade
has one very boring result:

Nothing happens.

The application keeps working,
only now on supported technology.

<!-- S31 -->
Although that is not
quite the whole truth.

Old technology
rarely fails by crashing.

<!-- S32 -->
It fails when the security audit
comes back with a finding.

When a partner's new interface
needs something
your platform cannot speak.

<!-- S33 -->
When the hosting provider
retires the image you run on.

Or when you try to hire someone
and nobody wants the job.

<!-- S34 -->
None of that is a bug.

There is nothing to fix,
because nothing is broken.

The world simply
stopped waiting for you.

<!-- S35 -->
It's like changing the oil in your car.

Nobody gets excited about new oil.

But skip the maintenance
for long enough...

and eventually you get
a much more exciting problem.

<!-- S36 -->
Technical debt works the same way.

A small upgrade today might mean:

Update a few dependencies.

Fix a few incompatibilities.

Run the tests.

Done.

<!-- S37 -->
Wait five years.

Now it is:

Update the platform.

Replace half the libraries.

Upgrade the framework.

Rewrite that ancient component.

And whatever you do...

don't touch that file.

<!-- S38 -->
At that point
we are no longer doing an upgrade.

We are doing archaeology.

<!-- S39 -->
"Look! I found a dependency from 2014!"

"Don't touch it."

"Why?"

"We don't know what it does."

<!-- S40 -->
Because technical debt
isn't only old software.

It's also fear.

Fear of changing something
nobody fully understands any more.

<!-- S41 -->
And this is where
an old saying comes in.

The blacksmith's horse goes barefoot.

<!-- S42 -->
A blacksmith spends his life
making shoes
for everyone else's horses...

while his own horse has none.

<!-- S43 -->
English usually tells it
about the cobbler's children,
who have nothing on their feet.

Different trade.

Same blind spot.

<!-- S44 -->
Software companies
are remarkably good at this.

We spend decades explaining to customers
why their old systems
need to be upgraded...

while our own systems keep getting older.

<!-- S45 -->
Because:

"We don't have time right now."

Next year:

"Still not a priority."

<!-- S46 -->
A few years later:

"That's going to be
a pretty big upgrade."

And eventually:

"It's too big to upgrade."

<!-- S47 -->
That is the trap.

The longer you postpone,
the further your system drifts
from the technology around it.

And closing that distance
gets more expensive every year.

<!-- S48 -->
And none of this
is a misunderstanding.

They know it is old.

They know support is ending.

They know the upgrade
will eventually be necessary.

It is a business decision.

<!-- S49 -->
The cost of upgrading is visible today.

The cost of not upgrading
is somewhere in the future.

<!-- S50 -->
And those two costs
don't land on the same person.

The upgrade comes out of
this year's budget,
approved by someone
who gets nothing visible in return.

<!-- S51 -->
The outage comes out of a budget
that doesn't exist yet,
in a year when somebody else
will be sitting in that chair.

<!-- S52 -->
Humans are remarkably good
at sending problems into the future.

Unfortunately...

the future eventually arrives.

<!-- S53 -->
Technical debt doesn't disappear
when we ignore it.

It grows.

<!-- S54 -->
To be fair,
not everything needs upgrading.

A system that gets switched off next year
can stay exactly as it is.

<!-- S55 -->
And "there is a newer version"
is not an argument on its own.

<!-- S56 -->
The question was never
"is this the latest?"

The question is:

"when this breaks,
will anyone still be able to fix it?"

<!-- S57 -->
Which is why the healthy version of this
looks disappointingly boring.

A few dependencies every release.

One framework version at a time.

<!-- S58 -->
Not a project.

Not a migration.

Not a two-year programme
with a steering committee.

Just maintenance.

The same way nobody
runs a project to change the oil.

<!-- S59 -->
So the next time someone asks:

"Why should we upgrade?

It still works."

<!-- S60 -->
Remember 2G.

Remember Water 2.0.

And remember Chair 6.

<!-- S61 -->
Because "working today"
is not the same
as "still working in five years".

Technology moves on.

<!-- S62 -->
And if we don't move with it,
eventually we are not
maintaining our software.

We are maintaining a museum.

<!-- S63 -->
Which is exactly what
the blacksmith's horse was doing.

Walking around barefoot...

while the blacksmith was busy
making shoes for everyone else.
