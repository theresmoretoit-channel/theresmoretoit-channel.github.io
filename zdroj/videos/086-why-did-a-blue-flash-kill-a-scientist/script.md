# Why Did a Blue Flash Kill a Scientist Nine Days Later?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you're standing in a laboratory.
In front of you is a small metallic sphere.

<!-- S02 -->
It doesn't look particularly dangerous.
There is no fire.
No explosion.
No warning siren.
Then, suddenly—
a flash of blue light fills the room.

<!-- S03 -->
For a fraction of a second, something has happened that you cannot see.

<!-- S04 -->
The scientist standing closest to it immediately knows what went wrong.
He throws the equipment away.

<!-- S05 -->
And everyone in the room runs for the door.
Nine days later, he is dead.
His name was Louis Slotin.

<!-- S06 -->
And the object in front of him would later become known as the Demon Core.

<!-- S07 -->
But what actually happened in that laboratory?
And where did that strange blue light come from?

<!-- S08 -->
Slotin knew this work was dangerous.
He had been warned.

<!-- S09 -->
Enrico Fermi reportedly told him that if he kept doing it this way, he would be dead within a year.

<!-- S10 -->
Richard Feynman once compared this kind of work to tickling the tail of a sleeping dragon.

<!-- S11 -->
And this wasn't experimental plutonium made for a laboratory.
This was a bomb core.

<!-- S12 -->
It had been made for the third atomic bomb.

<!-- S13 -->
On the day of the accident, Slotin was teaching the man who was about to take over his job.

<!-- S13b -->
The experiment wasn't even scheduled.
Slotin decided to do it as a demonstration.

<!-- S14 -->
The approved method used spacers, so the assembly could never close completely.
Slotin took them out.

<!-- S14b -->
His way of doing it depended on a screwdriver.

<!-- S15 -->
So why was a screwdriver anywhere near a nearly critical plutonium core?

<!-- S16 -->
One fission releases neutrons.
Those neutrons can trigger more fissions.

<!-- S17 -->
If enough of them keep the process going, the reaction becomes self-sustaining.
The system is called critical.

<!-- S18 -->
If too few neutrons remain available, the reaction dies out.
But if the number grows—
the reaction can increase extremely rapidly.

<!-- S19 -->
The scientists at Los Alamos were studying precisely how close they could bring a piece of plutonium to that dangerous boundary.

<!-- S19b -->
Was the core dangerous even without a screwdriver?

<!-- S19c -->
Far less than you might think.
On its own, it was below critical mass, so most of its neutrons simply escaped.

<!-- S19d -->
Its radiation was mostly alpha particles, and a thin nickel coating stopped them.
The sphere just felt warm to the touch.

<!-- S19e -->
The danger began only when something sent those escaping neutrons back.

<!-- S20 -->
Around it, scientists could place materials that reflected neutrons back toward the plutonium.
One such material was beryllium.

<!-- S21 -->
Move the reflector closer—
and the system becomes more reactive.
Move it away—
and the system becomes less reactive.

<!-- S22 -->
And with beryllium wrapped almost all the way around the core, even a tiny gap made all the difference.

<!-- S22b -->
And there was no reliable way to do this by remote control yet.
So it was done by hand.

<!-- S23 -->
On May 21, 1946, Louis Slotin was performing exactly this kind of experiment.

<!-- S24 -->
Slotin was a Canadian physicist working at Los Alamos.
He had performed similar experiments before.

<!-- S25 -->
The core sat inside a beryllium half-shell.
Slotin slowly lowered a second shell over it, holding it with his thumb.

<!-- S26 -->
The shells could almost completely surround it.
But he needed to keep a tiny gap between them.

<!-- S27 -->
So he used a screwdriver.

<!-- S28 -->
The screwdriver wasn't some sophisticated safety mechanism.
It was literally a screwdriver in Slotin's hand.

<!-- S29 -->
Then—
the screwdriver slipped.

<!-- S30 -->
The upper shell dropped into place.

<!-- S31 -->
For an instant, the plutonium became supercritical.
And something extraordinary happened.

<!-- S32 -->
A burst of radiation passed through the laboratory.
Witnesses reported seeing a blue flash.

<!-- S33 -->
Slotin immediately flipped the upper shell off.
The reaction stopped.

<!-- S34 -->
The entire event had lasted only a fraction of a second.
But the damage had already been done.

<!-- S35 -->
There was no nuclear explosion.
The plutonium core did not explode like an atomic bomb.

<!-- S36 -->
Instead, it produced an extremely intense burst of radiation.

<!-- S37 -->
For a fraction of a second, the chain reaction ran away on its own, faster than any human could react.

<!-- S38 -->
But for the people standing nearby, a fraction of a second was enough.

<!-- S39 -->
Slotin had been standing closest to the core.
He received a massive dose of radiation.

<!-- S40 -->
The other scientists in the room also received radiation, but their exposure was considerably smaller.

<!-- S41 -->
Slotin knew immediately that he had been badly exposed.

<!-- S42 -->
One of the men in the room later said:
"He knew he had had it at that time."

<!-- S42b -->
Even so, he sketched where everyone had been standing, so the doctors could work out their doses.

<!-- S43 -->
He had survived the immediate accident.
But he would not survive its consequences.

<!-- S44 -->
The flash looked like Cherenkov radiation.

<!-- S45 -->
That is the blue glow you can see around nuclear reactor fuel under water.

<!-- S46 -->
It happens when charged particles move through a material faster than light can travel through that material.

<!-- S47 -->
But there was a problem.
This happened in air.

<!-- S48 -->
In air, light travels almost as fast as it does in a vacuum.
Hardly anything can outrun it there.

<!-- S49 -->
The burst of radiation ionised the surrounding air.
And excited air molecules give off light of their own.

<!-- S50 -->
So the blue flash wasn't the thing that killed Slotin.

<!-- S51 -->
It was the warning that the invisible thing had already happened.

<!-- S52 -->
He was conscious when he reached the hospital.

<!-- S53 -->
But the radiation had already fatally damaged cells throughout his body.

<!-- S54 -->
Over the following days, his condition deteriorated.

<!-- S55 -->
Nine days after the accident, Louis Slotin died.
He was only thirty-five years old.

<!-- S56 -->
And this wasn't even the first time.

<!-- S57 -->
The same plutonium core had already caused a fatal accident.

<!-- S58 -->
Less than a year earlier, another scientist named Harry Daghlian had been performing a criticality experiment with the same core.

<!-- S59 -->
He was using tungsten-carbide bricks to reflect neutrons toward it.

<!-- S60 -->
One of the bricks slipped from his hand and fell onto the assembly.
The core went supercritical.

<!-- S60b -->
A guard sat a few metres away, reading a newspaper with his back to the table.
The blue flash lit up the page in his hands.

<!-- S61 -->
Daghlian immediately removed the brick.

<!-- S62 -->
But he had received a lethal dose of radiation.
He died twenty-five days later.

<!-- S63 -->
Both fatal accidents happened on the twenty-first day of a month.

<!-- S64 -->
One in August 1945.
The other in May 1946.

<!-- S65 -->
The coincidence meant nothing scientifically.
But it made the story almost impossible to forget.

<!-- S66 -->
It wasn't called the Demon Core when Slotin died.

<!-- S67 -->
The name came later, after the same piece of plutonium had been involved in two fatal accidents.

<!-- S68 -->
One core.
Two scientists.
Two criticality accidents.

<!-- S69 -->
And both times, the warning came too late.

<!-- S70 -->
The accidents changed the way scientists approached criticality experiments.

<!-- S71 -->
Hands-on experiments like this were banned.

<!-- S71b -->
Eventually, they were run from about four hundred metres away, watched on television.

<!-- S72 -->
Scientists became much more cautious about bringing fissile material close to criticality.

<!-- S73 -->
The core had been made for the third atomic bomb.

<!-- S74 -->
After the second accident, the core was too radioactive to use straight away.

<!-- S75 -->
It was moved to a third test at Bikini —
and that test was cancelled.

<!-- S76 -->
The age of scientists manually holding pieces of equipment around a nearly critical plutonium core was coming to an end.

<!-- S77 -->
The Demon Core itself was eventually melted down and its plutonium reused.

<!-- S78 -->
So the infamous sphere no longer exists.
But the story survived.

<!-- S79 -->
Remember the blue flash?

<!-- S80 -->
It wasn't an explosion.
It wasn't the plutonium burning.

<!-- S81 -->
It wasn't even the thing that killed Louis Slotin.

<!-- S82 -->
It was the moment the invisible danger became visible.

<!-- S83 -->
And all it took was a screwdriver slipping a few millimetres.
