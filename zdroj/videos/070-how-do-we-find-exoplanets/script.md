# How Do We Find Exoplanets?

<!-- S01 -->
There is a strange problem
with finding planets
outside our Solar System.
They are everywhere.
But we can barely see them.

<!-- S02 -->
Imagine pointing the most powerful
telescope ever built
at another star system.
You might expect to see
something like this:
A star.
And next to it, a planet.
Simple.
Except that almost never works.

<!-- S03 -->
A planet is incredibly faint
compared with the star it orbits.
And from dozens or hundreds
of light-years away,
the star and the planet appear
almost like a single point of light.

<!-- S04 -->
So how do astronomers find planets
they usually cannot see?
The answer is surprisingly clever.

<!-- S05 -->
They don't necessarily look
for the planet.
They look for what the planet does
to everything around it.
And there are several ways to do that.

<!-- S06 -->
Let's start with the most obvious one.
Direct imaging.
If we can somehow block
the overwhelming light of a star,
a sufficiently bright planet
can appear next to it.
This is not science fiction.
Astronomers have directly imaged
some exoplanets.

<!-- S07 -->
But there is a catch.
The planets we can image directly
tend to be large, young,
and still relatively hot.

<!-- S08 -->
A young planet can glow strongly
in infrared light
because it is still radiating heat
left over from its formation.
Even then, separating the planet
from its star is extremely difficult.

<!-- S09 -->
A telescope may need a special instrument
called a coronagraph.
A coronagraph blocks or suppresses
the light from the star
while allowing light from nearby objects
to reach the detector.

<!-- S10 -->
And even after all that,
we are usually not looking
at a detailed picture of a planet.
We are looking at a tiny point of light.
But that tiny point can contain
an astonishing amount of information.

<!-- S11 -->
If we split its light into a spectrum,
we can look for the chemical fingerprints
of molecules in the planet's atmosphere.

<!-- S12 -->
So direct imaging can potentially
tell us not just:
"There is something there."
But:
"There is a planet there —
and this is what its atmosphere
may contain."

<!-- S13 -->
Still, direct imaging is difficult.
Fortunately, there is a much easier trick.
We can wait for the planet
to get in the way.

<!-- S14 -->
Imagine looking at a star from far away.
Now imagine a planet crossing directly
between the star and us.

<!-- S15 -->
The planet blocks a tiny fraction
of the star's light.
The star gets slightly dimmer.
And then, when the planet moves away,
the star becomes bright again.
That's a transit.

<!-- S16 -->
The change can be incredibly small.
For an Earth-sized planet
crossing a Sun-like star,
the drop in brightness is only about
zero point zero zero eight per cent.

<!-- S17 -->
That's about one part
in twelve thousand.
A tiny change in a very bright signal.
But modern telescopes can measure
tiny changes in brightness.

<!-- S18 -->
And if the same dip happens
again and again at regular intervals,
we have something very interesting.
A planet candidate.
Other causes,
such as an eclipsing pair of stars,
have to be ruled out first.

<!-- S19 -->
The time between the dips
tells us the planet's orbital period.
The depth of the dip tells us
approximately how large the planet is
compared with its star.

<!-- S20 -->
And the shape and duration of the transit
can reveal additional information
about the orbit
and the geometry of the system.

<!-- S21 -->
This is one of the reasons missions
such as Kepler and TESS
have been so successful.
They don't need to photograph
thousands of planets individually.
They simply watch enormous numbers
of stars and ask:
"Did any of them get slightly darker?"

<!-- S22 -->
But there is an important limitation.
A transit only happens
if the planet's orbit happens
to be aligned so that the planet
crosses the star from our point of view.
A planet can be there
and never transit at all.

<!-- S23 -->
So what if we could detect a planet
without waiting for it
to pass in front of its star?
We can.
And this next method
is particularly clever.

<!-- S24 -->
It is tempting to imagine a planet
orbiting a stationary star.
But that's not quite what happens.

<!-- S25 -->
A planet and its star both orbit
their common centre of mass.
Usually the star is much more massive,
so the centre of mass
is very close to the star.

<!-- S26 -->
But the star still moves.
Tiny amounts.
And that movement can be detected.
How?
With a spectrograph.

<!-- S27 -->
A spectrograph spreads starlight
into its component wavelengths.
Stellar spectra contain
dark absorption lines
produced by atoms and molecules
in stellar atmospheres.

<!-- S28 -->
If the star moves slightly towards us,
those spectral lines shift
towards shorter wavelengths.
If it moves away from us,
they shift towards longer wavelengths.
This is the Doppler effect.

<!-- S29 -->
So instead of seeing the planet,
we watch the star's spectrum.
If the spectral lines repeatedly
move back and forth,
something may be pulling the star around.
And one possible explanation is a planet.

<!-- S30 -->
This technique is usually called
the radial velocity method.
It can tell us about
the planet's orbital period
and the size of the star's motion.

<!-- S31 -->
And from that,
with an estimate of the star's mass,
astronomers can estimate
the planet's mass —
more precisely, its minimum mass
unless the orbital inclination is known.

<!-- S32 -->
Now something really interesting happens
when we combine the two methods.
Suppose a planet passes
in front of its star.
The transit tells us the planet's size
relative to its star.
If we know the star's size,
we can work out the planet's size too.

<!-- S33 -->
The radial velocity method tells us
how strongly the planet pulls
on its star.
That gives us information about its mass.
And if we know both mass and size,
we can calculate density.

<!-- S34 -->
Suddenly, the planet stops being
just a mysterious dot.
We can start asking
what it might actually be made of.
A planet with a relatively high density
could be rocky.

<!-- S35 -->
A much lower density could indicate
a large atmosphere
surrounding a smaller solid core,
or a planet rich in water or gas.
But density alone cannot tell us
exactly what a planet is made of.

<!-- S36 -->
And this is one of the most powerful
ideas in exoplanet astronomy:
Sometimes different methods
don't just find the same planet.
They answer different parts of the puzzle.

<!-- S37 -->
But there is an even stranger method.
One where we don't need the planet
to cross its star.
And we don't even need
to watch the star wobble.

<!-- S38 -->
Imagine a distant star
behind another star.
As the foreground star passes
in front of it,
its gravity bends the light
from the background star.

<!-- S39 -->
The foreground star acts
like a gravitational lens.
The background star
temporarily appears brighter.

<!-- S40 -->
Now add a planet
orbiting the foreground star.
The planet's gravity can produce
an additional distortion.
A brief anomaly in the brightening curve.
That anomaly can reveal the planet.

<!-- S41 -->
This is called gravitational microlensing.
And it is particularly useful
for finding planets that can be
difficult to detect by other methods.

<!-- S42 -->
But microlensing has
a strange disadvantage.
The event may happen only once.
After the stars move apart,
the alignment is gone.

<!-- S43 -->
So unlike a transit,
where we can watch the same planet
pass in front of its star
again and again,
a microlensing event can be more like
catching a rare cosmic coincidence.
One moment, something happens.
Then it's gone.

<!-- S44 -->
Finding a planet is only the beginning.
Astronomers can sometimes study
its atmosphere by looking at starlight
that passes through it during a transit.

<!-- S45 -->
Imagine the planet moving across the star.
Most of the starlight
travels directly to us.
But a tiny fraction passes through
the planet's atmosphere first.

<!-- S46 -->
Different atoms and molecules
absorb different wavelengths of light.
So when astronomers compare
the star's spectrum during a transit
with its spectrum outside the transit,
they may see tiny changes.

<!-- S47 -->
Those changes can reveal the fingerprints
of atoms and molecules in the atmosphere.
Water vapour.
Carbon dioxide.
Methane.
Sodium.
And other substances,
depending on the planet
and the observations.

<!-- S48 -->
The signals can be incredibly faint.
But modern instruments can measure them.
So a planet that is completely invisible
in an ordinary image
can leave a fingerprint
in the light of its star.

<!-- S49 -->
And that brings us back
to the original question.
How do we find exoplanets?
Sometimes we actually image them.
But much more often,
we detect their effects.

<!-- S50 -->
A planet can make its star
slightly dimmer.
It can make its star move.
It can distort the light
from a distant background star.
Its atmosphere can alter
the spectrum of starlight.

<!-- S51 -->
In other words, astronomers have developed
several ways of detecting something
without directly seeing it.
And that is perhaps
the most fascinating part.

<!-- S52 -->
When you hear that astronomers
have discovered an exoplanet
hundreds of light-years away,
it does not necessarily mean
that a telescope took a photograph
of a little blue or green world.
Often, the evidence is much more subtle.

<!-- S53 -->
A tiny dip in brightness.
A microscopic shift in a spectral line.
A brief gravitational distortion.
A few missing wavelengths in a spectrum.

<!-- S54 -->
And from these tiny signals,
astronomers reconstruct an entire world.
Its orbit.
Its size.
Its mass.
Its density.
And sometimes even clues
about its atmosphere.

<!-- S55 -->
All from light that has travelled
across the galaxy to reach us.
The planet itself may be
almost impossible to see.
But it cannot completely hide
the consequences of being there.
And that's how we find worlds
we cannot see.
