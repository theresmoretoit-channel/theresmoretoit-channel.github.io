<!-- S01 -->
In 1977, you could fly
from London to New York
in about three and a half hours.

<!-- S02 -->
Today, the same trip
takes around seven or eight.

<!-- S03 -->
And that isn't just the story
of one unusual plane.
The ordinary jets got stuck too.

<!-- S04 -->
The Boeing 747 entered service in 1970.
It cruised at about eighty-five percent
of the speed of sound.

<!-- S05 -->
The newest airliners flying today
cruise at... about eighty-five percent
of the speed of sound.

<!-- S06 -->
In those fifty-odd years,
computers went from filling rooms
to fitting in pockets.
The speed of a passenger jet
barely moved.

<!-- S07 -->
So what happened?
Did we forget how to build fast planes?

<!-- S08 -->
No.
We hit a wall.
And the wall is made of air.

<!-- S09 -->
A wing works partly
by speeding up the air
that flows over its top.
That air moves faster
than the plane itself.

<!-- S10 -->
So when the plane flies
at about eighty percent
of the speed of sound,
parts of the air on top of the wing
are already going faster than sound.

<!-- S11 -->
And where that supersonic air
has to slow down again,
it forms a shock wave.

<!-- S12 -->
Shock waves are expensive.
They add a whole new kind of drag,
and it doesn't creep up gently.
Past a certain speed, it shoots up.

<!-- S13 -->
For many airliners,
that steep rise begins somewhere
between eighty and eighty-five percent
of the speed of sound.

<!-- S14 -->
Just where the 747 flew in 1970.
And just where airliners fly now.

<!-- S15 -->
And drag isn't just a number
in a wind tunnel.
It's paid for in fuel.

<!-- S16 -->
More drag means the engines
have to push harder.
Pushing harder means burning more fuel
for every kilometre.

<!-- S17 -->
And fuel is one of the biggest bills
an airline pays.
Often a quarter to a third
of everything it spends.

<!-- S18 -->
So airlines don't simply fly
as fast as their planes can go.
They choose a speed that balances
the cost of fuel
against the cost of time.

<!-- S19 -->
Too slow, and the crew is paid
for longer,
and the plane fits fewer trips
into a day.

<!-- S20 -->
Too fast, and fuel costs
climb sharply.
Airlines look for the balance.

<!-- S21 -->
Even some versions of the Boeing 707,
back in the 1960s,
could fly at up to ninety percent
of the speed of sound.
Airlines usually flew it
at about eighty-two.

<!-- S22 -->
Engineers did find ways
to push the wall back a little.
Swept-back wings.
And in the 1960s, at NASA,
Richard Whitcomb's supercritical wing.

<!-- S23 -->
It could have been used to fly faster.
Mostly, airliners used it
to fly at the same speed
on less fuel.

<!-- S24 -->
Remember that choice.
It's going to keep coming back.

<!-- S25 -->
Because somebody did try
to fly faster, right up at the wall.
In 1962, the Convair 990
entered service as the fastest airliner
in the world.

<!-- S26 -->
But it never quite reached the speed
it had been promised.
And it burned more fuel
than the Boeing 707.

<!-- S27 -->
For the airlines, a little extra speed
wasn't worth the fuel.
Only thirty-seven were ever built.

<!-- S28 -->
So flying just a little faster
at the wall doesn't pay.
But what about going
straight through it?

<!-- S29 -->
That's Concorde.
It cruised at twice the speed of sound,
over two thousand kilometres an hour.

<!-- S30 -->
Getting through the wall
didn't make the wall go away.
It just meant paying for it.

<!-- S31 -->
Concorde carried about a hundred people.
Per passenger, it burned roughly
three times as much fuel as a 747.

<!-- S32 -->
And it brought something
no ordinary airliner makes.
A sonic boom.

<!-- S33 -->
A sonic boom isn't a single bang
at the moment you pass the speed of sound.
It follows the plane the whole way,
a carpet of noise on the ground
dozens of kilometres wide.

<!-- S34 -->
In 1973, the United States banned
civilian supersonic flight over its land.
And Concorde was kept
mostly to routes over the sea.

<!-- S35 -->
That left it a handful of routes,
mostly London and Paris
to New York.
Only twenty were ever built.

<!-- S36 -->
And its timing was terrible.
Concorde was designed
in the 1960s, when fuel was cheap.

<!-- S37 -->
In early 1973, the big American airlines
that had held options on it
pulled out.
Then, that October,
the oil crisis sent fuel prices soaring.
Two separate blows,
a few months apart.

<!-- S38 -->
In 1997, a return ticket
from New York to London
cost over eight thousand dollars.

<!-- S39 -->
Some people paid it.
Not enough of them.
Concorde had always been expensive
to run and to maintain.

<!-- S40 -->
After a crash in 2000,
and a slump in air travel
after September 2001,
it flew its last passengers in 2003.

<!-- S41 -->
So now we know where the wall is,
and what it costs to get past it.

<!-- S42 -->
But the clearest answer
came in the early 2000s.
Because then, the airlines got something
very close to a direct choice.

<!-- S43 -->
In 2001, Boeing proposed
the Sonic Cruiser.
It would fly just under
the speed of sound,
at up to ninety-eight percent of it.
Around fifteen to twenty percent
faster than a normal airliner.

<!-- S44 -->
By 2002, Boeing was also discussing
another idea with airlines.
A plane that flew at the usual speed,
but burned seventeen to twenty percent
less fuel than a 767.

<!-- S45 -->
Faster... or cheaper.

<!-- S46 -->
Cheaper won.
In 2002, Boeing dropped the Sonic Cruiser
and turned to efficiency instead.
That plane became the Boeing 787.

<!-- S47 -->
It's the same choice
Whitcomb's wing ran into.
The same trade-off that hurt the Convair,
and that Concorde never escaped.

<!-- S48 -->
Again and again,
airlines chose efficiency over speed.

<!-- S49 -->
Which makes it sound
like airliners stood still
for fifty years.
They didn't.

<!-- S50 -->
They just got better
at something else.

<!-- S51 -->
A new airliner today
uses about half as much fuel per seat,
for the same distance,
as a new one from the 1960s.

<!-- S52 -->
And over those years,
flying got much cheaper.

<!-- S53 -->
In the United States,
the median domestic return fare,
adjusted for inflation,
fell by almost forty percent
between 1980 and 2005.

<!-- S54 -->
And air travel
exploded.
In 1970, the world's airlines carried
a few hundred million passengers.
Before the pandemic,
it was four and a half billion a year.

<!-- S55 -->
So planes did get faster.
Just not between the airports.

<!-- S56 -->
In 1970, flying abroad was still
beyond the budget
of many ordinary families.
Over the decades, that changed.
Flying became affordable
to far more people.

<!-- S57 -->
We were watching the wrong number.
Speed is the one you can see
on the departures board.
Price is the one that decided
who got on the plane at all.

<!-- S58 -->
But that bargain
might be about to change.

<!-- S59 -->
NASA's X-59 is built to make
a much quieter sonic boom.
Its whole shape is designed
to spread the shock waves out,
so on the ground, it should sound
more like a distant thump.

<!-- S60 -->
In 2026, it flew faster than sound
for the first time.

<!-- S61 -->
And in 2026, the American regulator
proposed replacing the ban over land
with a limit on how loud the boom
can be on the ground.
It's a proposal, not yet a rule.

<!-- S62 -->
A start-up called Boom Supersonic
is designing an airliner for that world,
with a first flight planned
for the late 2020s.

<!-- S63 -->
But notice what hasn't changed.
Getting past the wall
still costs more fuel.

<!-- S64 -->
So the old question is still there.
Not whether we can build it.
We could in the 1970s.
But who pays for the hours it saves.

<!-- S65 -->
For fifty years,
most of us chose the cheaper seat.
And we got what we chose.

<!-- S66 -->
Planes no faster
than the ones from 1970...
carrying more than ten times
as many passengers a year.

<!-- S67 -->
So here's a question for you.
How much would you pay
to land three hours earlier?
