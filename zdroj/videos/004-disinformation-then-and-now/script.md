<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you live in the fourteenth century.
No internet. No television. No newspapers.
Most of the people around you cannot read.

<!-- S02 -->
One day a traveller arrives, and he says:
"In the north, a monster has appeared.
It has the body of a man, the head of a dog,
and it eats children."

<!-- S03 -->
How do you find out whether that is true?
You don't. There is no way.
Maybe you don't believe him.
But maybe, that evening,
you mention it to your neighbour.

<!-- S04 -->
He tells a few more people.
And within a week, half the country knows
there is a monster in the north.

<!-- S05 -->
Today we would laugh at that.
But were people in the past really
so much more stupid than we are?
And did more nonsense actually spread back then
than it does now?

<!-- S06 -->
People have always believed strange things.
A comet could be read as a warning
that something terrible was coming.

<!-- S07 -->
And when the plague arrived,
the best medical minds in Europe
concluded that the air itself had gone rotten.
That was not a fringe theory.
That was the official answer.

<!-- S08 -->
But most of those people were not stupid.
They simply did not have
the information we have.

<!-- S09 -->
And with no way to check anything,
a story changes every time it is retold,
until it has almost nothing to do
with whatever actually happened.

<!-- S10 -->
Of course, it was not only ignorance.
Deliberate lies are as old as we are.

<!-- S11 -->
Rulers needed propaganda.
Politicians needed to justify wars.
People spread rumours about their enemies.
And during an epidemic,
somebody always had to be blamed.

<!-- S12 -->
On the fourteenth of February,
thirteen forty-nine,
the city of Strasbourg burned
its entire Jewish community.
About two thousand people,
according to the city's own chronicler.

<!-- S13 -->
The accusation was that they had poisoned
the wells, and caused the plague.
It was a lie.
The Pope had written, months earlier,
that the plague was killing people
in places where no Jews lived at all.

<!-- S14 -->
It spread anyway.
City after city, for another two years.

<!-- S15 -->
So, no.
Disinformation is not an invention
of the internet.
The internet only changed
its speed, and its reach.

<!-- S16 -->
And then came new technology.
The printing press could make
hundreds of copies of the same text.

<!-- S17 -->
Historians estimate that around
twenty-eight thousand different editions
from the first decades of printing
still survive today.

<!-- S18 -->
Even at a conservative few hundred copies each,
printing had already produced
millions of books.

<!-- S19 -->
Newspapers could inform a whole region.
Radio and television could reach
millions of people at once.

<!-- S20 -->
And the pattern kept repeating.
The new technology made it faster
to spread knowledge.
And just as much faster
to spread nonsense and propaganda.

<!-- S21 -->
So the technology was not good,
and it was not bad.
It was a multiplier.
It multiplied truth and lies alike.

<!-- S22 -->
And here is my favourite example.
In nineteen thirty-eight, Orson Welles
put a radio play about an invasion
from Mars on the air.

<!-- S23 -->
And it supposedly sent
the whole of America into a panic.
You have probably heard the story.
Millions of terrified listeners.
People running out of their homes.

<!-- S24 -->
Except that it didn't happen.

<!-- S25 -->
The service that measured radio audiences
rang five thousand households that night
and asked what they were listening to.
Two per cent said the Welles programme.

<!-- S26 -->
And not one newspaper ever verified
a single death caused by the panic.

<!-- S27 -->
The panic was reported by newspapers,
which at that moment were losing
their advertising money to radio.

<!-- S28 -->
A story about the new medium
driving listeners out of their minds
was extremely convenient.

<!-- S29 -->
So the most famous example
of a medium spreading mass hysteria
is itself a piece of disinformation.
Spread by the older medium.
About the newer one.

<!-- S30 -->
And then came the internet,
and that changed the rules completely.

<!-- S31 -->
Somebody makes a claim? You can look it up.
Checking a fact has never in history
been as easy as it is right now.

<!-- S32 -->
But the internet also let everybody publish.
Reaching a million people used to need
a newspaper, a radio station,
or a television channel.

<!-- S33 -->
Now it needs a phone and an account.

<!-- S34 -->
So we got the largest library in history.
And also the largest pub in history,
where anybody can shout across the room.

<!-- S35 -->
And if it is shocking enough,
a million people hear it by the evening.

<!-- S36 -->
And it doesn't even have to be a lie.
Our brains love information that is
surprising, frightening or outrageous.
So sensational nonsense can travel
further than boring truth.

<!-- S37 -->
"Researchers analysed the data and found..."
Boring.
"SCIENTISTS DISCOVER THE SHOCKING TRUTH
THEY ARE HIDING FROM YOU!"
Click.

<!-- S38 -->
And that is not just a feeling.
It has been measured.
A study published in Science
followed a hundred and twenty-six thousand
stories spreading across Twitter.

<!-- S39 -->
A false story was seventy per cent
more likely to be passed on
than a true one.
The truth took about six times as long
to reach fifteen hundred people.

<!-- S40 -->
True stories almost never got past
about a thousand people.
The most successful false ones
reached up to a hundred thousand.

<!-- S41 -->
And then the researchers did something
that surprised everybody.
They took the bots out of the data.
The bots had been spreading true stories
and false stories at the same rate.

<!-- S42 -->
The difference was not the machines.
The difference was us.

<!-- S43 -->
So here is where it gets interesting.
The problem in the Middle Ages
was not having enough information.
Our problem is the opposite.
We have far too much.

<!-- S44 -->
And now another large change is arriving.
Artificial intelligence.
The internet let us search for information.
AI can produce it.

<!-- S45 -->
You can ask about a historical event,
a scientific problem,
or anything you don't understand.
And within a few seconds
you get a perfectly worded answer.

<!-- S46 -->
But a perfectly worded answer
does not have to be a correct one.
A model can produce a very convincing claim
that is simply wrong.

<!-- S47 -->
And that is not all.
AI can now also make images.
And video. And voices.
So a false claim no longer has to be
a sentence typed somewhere on the internet.

<!-- S48 -->
It can have a photograph.
It can have footage.
It can have the voice of somebody
who supposedly witnessed the whole thing.

<!-- S49 -->
It can even arrive with a very persuasive
assistant, ready to explain it all to you.
And that is a new kind of problem.
Not only: is this true?
But also: who, or what, made it?

<!-- S50 -->
So were people back then more stupid?
Probably not.
A medieval person had far less knowledge,
and far fewer ways to check anything.

<!-- S51 -->
We have the opposite problem.
An enormous amount of knowledge,
an enormous amount of nonsense,
and tools that manufacture nonsense at scale.

<!-- S52 -->
So perhaps we are asking the wrong question.
"Were there more lies in the past?"
A better question is:
"How hard was it to find out
that something was false?"

<!-- S53 -->
And there, the difference is enormous.
In the Middle Ages you heard a story
from a traveller,
and you had no way to check it.

<!-- S54 -->
Today you can find the sources,
compare the accounts,
and track down the original data.
In minutes.

<!-- S55 -->
So perhaps we are not living in an age
with more lies in it than ever before.

<!-- S56 -->
We are living in an age that holds
the best tools ever built for finding truth,
and the best tools ever built
for manufacturing nonsense.

<!-- S57 -->
Printing let us copy information.
Radio and television broadcast it.
The internet handed it to everybody.
And AI has started producing it.

<!-- S58 -->
Every one of those gave us something.
And every one of them created a new way
for information to mislead us.

<!-- S59 -->
So maybe the most important skill now
is not knowing everything.
That isn't possible anyway.

<!-- S60 -->
It is being able to say:
"I don't know. Let's check."

<!-- S61 -->
Because when somebody tells us today
that there is a monster in the north
with the head of a dog...
we don't have to believe it,
and we don't have to disbelieve it.

<!-- S62 -->
We can just open the internet.
And look.
