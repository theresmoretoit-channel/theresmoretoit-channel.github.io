# Why Is This 90-Year-Old Airplane Still Flying?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you are standing on a runway
somewhere in the Canadian Arctic.

<!-- S02 -->
It is freezing.

There is no major airport nearby.

No repair centre.

No endless supply of spare parts.

<!-- S03 -->
And then an airplane arrives.

<!-- S04 -->
It has two giant radial engines.

Its cockpit is full of
round mechanical instruments.

No screens.

No fly-by-wire.

No computers.

<!-- S05 -->
This is the Douglas DC-3.

It first flew in 1935.

<!-- S06 -->
During the Second World War,
more than ten thousand
military versions were built.

<!-- S07 -->
And more than ninety years
after that first flight,
some of them are still working.

<!-- S08 -->
Not in a museum.

Hauling cargo and supplies
to remote places.

<!-- S09 -->
Most airliners are retired
after about twenty-five years.

Some DC-3s still at work
are more than three times that age.

<!-- S10 -->
So what did Douglas get
so incredibly right?

<!-- S11 -->
To understand that, we need to go back
to the early days of passenger flying.

<!-- S12 -->
Airliners were noisy.

They were slow.

They were uncomfortable.

And many airlines couldn't make money
carrying passengers alone.

They depended on mail contracts
and subsidies.

<!-- S13 -->
Then Douglas built the DC-2.

All-metal construction.

Retractable landing gear.

A streamlined fuselage.

Twin engines.

<!-- S14 -->
And then Douglas took what worked...

and made it bigger, more comfortable
and more capable.

The result was the DC-3.

<!-- S15 -->
And suddenly,
passenger flying made economic sense.

It was fast enough.

It carried enough people.

It had enough range.

And it was reliable enough.

<!-- S16 -->
For the first time, an airline
could make money carrying passengers alone.

The DC-3 didn't just become
a successful airplane.

It helped turn air travel
into a real industry.

<!-- S16b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S17 -->
So what was the secret?

There wasn't one.

And that's the interesting part.

<!-- S18 -->
The DC-3 didn't have
one magical technology.

Almost everything about it was good
at the same time.

<!-- S18b -->
And above all, it was good
at the one thing an airliner has to do.

Make money.

Douglas made the cabin wider.

Twenty-one seats instead of fourteen.

Fifty percent more seats,
on every flight.

<!-- S23 -->
It wasn't the fastest airliner.

It wasn't the biggest.

It didn't have the longest range.

But it was good enough
at almost everything that mattered.

<!-- S24 -->
Its wings were relatively large
for its weight.

That let it fly slowly and safely,
and use short, rough runways.

Its structure was strong.

Its systems were simple.

<!-- S25 -->
And with two engines,
if one failed,
the other could keep it flying.

Not indefinitely.

But long enough
to give the pilots options.

<!-- S26 -->
That matters enormously
when the nearest airport
is hundreds of kilometres away.

<!-- S27 -->
And then there was the way it flew.

Pilots describe the DC-3
as stable and predictable.

<!-- S28 -->
If you were slightly too slow,
the airplane warned you
before things got really bad.

<!-- S29 -->
And a small movement of the controls
gave you a small, smooth response.

Never a sudden lurch.

<!-- S29b -->
It even talked to you.

Some veteran airline pilots claimed
they could feel a stewardess
walking up the aisle with coffee.

Her weight shifted the balance,
and they felt it in the controls.

<!-- S29c -->
Landing was gentle, too.

Those big wings settled softly
as the airplane neared the ground,
and it had little tendency to bounce.

Some pilots say it's easier to land
than many small private airplanes.

<!-- S30 -->
Pilots have a word for that.

Forgiving.

<!-- S30b -->
And think about where
the DC-3 spent its working life.

Bad weather.

Short gravel strips.

Long days.

Pilots who were tired,
or still new to the job.

<!-- S30c -->
An airplane that punishes
every small mistake
doesn't last long in that kind of life.

One that forgives them might.

<!-- S31 -->
It doesn't mean the DC-3 couldn't crash.

It absolutely could.

It means small mistakes
didn't immediately turn
into catastrophic ones.

And in aviation, that room to recover
can be priceless.

<!-- S31b -->
As one DC-3 pilot put it,
it's slow to get you into trouble...

but even slower to get you out of it.

<!-- S32 -->
But here's the really weird part.

Nobody in 1935 set out
to build an airplane
that would still be flying in 2026.

The goal was simply
a really good airliner.

<!-- S33 -->
But some of the designers' choices
aged remarkably well.

An aluminium structure.

Mechanical controls.

Mechanical engines.

<!-- S35 -->
A mechanic could understand
most of the machine
just by looking at it.

And that becomes extremely important
when your airplane
is more than eighty years old.

<!-- S36 -->
When something breaks on a modern aircraft,
you may need special diagnostic equipment,

a replacement electronic module,

software,

and someone certified to work on it.

<!-- S37 -->
Now imagine doing that in the Arctic.

<!-- S38 -->
That's where the DC-3's
old-fashioned design
becomes an advantage.

<!-- S38b -->
Picture one on a gravel strip,
hundreds of kilometres
from the nearest hangar.

Something isn't working.

<!-- S38c -->
A mechanic can often open a panel
and actually see the problem.

A cable.

A pulley.

A leaking line.

No error code.

Just the part itself.

<!-- S38d -->
And generations of mechanics
have learned how to keep
these airplanes alive.

<!-- S39 -->
That doesn't make it easy.

The problem is no longer
understanding the machine.

It's finding parts.

Finding specialists.

And satisfying modern aviation rules.

<!-- S40 -->
The airplane may be simple.

The world around it isn't.

<!-- S41 -->
The DC-3 also arrived
at exactly the right moment.

Aerodynamics, aluminium construction
and aircraft engines
had all matured at the same time.

And then history gave it
an enormous push.

<!-- S42 -->
During the war,
the military version was called the C-47.

That's where most of those
ten thousand came from.

<!-- S43 -->
They carried troops,
cargo, paratroopers
and medical supplies,

across enormous distances,
in brutal conditions.

<!-- S44 -->
And suddenly there were thousands
of people who knew how to fly them...

and thousands more
who knew how to fix them.

<!-- S45 -->
After the war,
many of those aircraft
were sold for civilian use.

And the DC-3 stopped being
just an airplane.

It became an ecosystem.

<!-- S46 -->
Parts.

Mechanics.

Pilots.

Training.

Experience.

And airplanes everywhere.

<!-- S47 -->
Sometimes a machine survives
not because it is perfect...

<!-- S47b -->
but because so many people
know how to keep it alive.

<!-- S48 -->
So why aren't we still
using them everywhere?

Because being durable
isn't the same as being efficient.

<!-- S49 -->
A modern turboprop carries more,
burns less fuel for every passenger,
and has far more capable avionics.

And fuel isn't free.

Neither is maintenance.

<!-- S49b -->
So why would anyone still use one?

<!-- S50 -->
Because efficiency isn't everything.

Remote areas.

Rough airfields.

Specialised cargo work.

<!-- S51 -->
Places where being able
to operate independently
matters more than squeezing out
the last percent of efficiency.

<!-- S52 -->
Some DC-3s have even been rebuilt.

The radial engines replaced
with turboprops.

The old cockpit
with modern avionics.

<!-- S53 -->
One famous example
is the Basler BT-67.

It starts with a DC-3 airframe,
and replaces much of what's inside.

<!-- S54 -->
It's like taking
an eighty-year-old classic car...

and fitting it with a modern engine
and modern electronics.

<!-- S55 -->
The body survives.

The concept survives.

The technology around it changes.

<!-- S56 -->
We often imagine technological progress
as a straight line.

New technology replaces the old,

and the old stuff disappears.

<!-- S57 -->
But sometimes, an old design
gets something fundamentally right.

And then newer technology
doesn't destroy it.

It gets added to it.

<!-- S58 -->
The DC-3 doesn't have to compete
with a modern airliner.

It just has to keep doing the job
it was exceptionally good at.

<!-- S59 -->
And apparently,
it is very difficult to convince
this particular airplane to retire.

<!-- S60 -->
So somewhere in the Arctic,
more than ninety years
after the first DC-3 took to the sky...

<!-- S61 -->
one of them may be starting its engines.

And flying to work,
like it has for generations.
