<!-- S02 -->
If you're a programmer,

<!-- S03 -->
Maybe you use Windows.
Maybe you have an iPhone.
Maybe you've never even seen Linux.

<!-- S04 -->
And if someone asked you

you might imagine something like this.

<!-- S05 -->
A black terminal.
Lots of text.
Someone typing commands.

<!-- S06 -->
Linux has a reputation for being
complicated, weird,
and mostly useful to programmers,
system administrators,
and people who apparently enjoy
making their computers harder to use.

<!-- S07 -->
But here's the interesting question.

<!-- S08 -->
Where does Linux actually run?

<!-- S09 -->
You might be surprised by the answer.

<!-- S10 -->
And to understand why,
we need to go back
to nineteen ninety-one.

<!-- S11 -->
In nineteen ninety-one,
a twenty-one-year-old Finnish student
named Linus Torvalds
started working on a small
operating-system kernel.

<!-- S12 -->
It wasn't supposed to become
a revolution.
It was basically a hobby project.

<!-- S13 -->
Linus wanted to create a Unix-like
system for his computer,
and he released the source code
so other people could experiment with it.

<!-- S14 -->
More and more programmers
started contributing.
People improved it, fixed bugs,
added features,
and adapted it to different hardware.

<!-- S15 -->
And something rather unusual happened.
A project started by one student
became a global collaboration.

<!-- S16 -->
Decades later, Linux is developed
by thousands of people,
and supported by companies,
universities, independent developers
and volunteers around the world.

<!-- S17 -->
The most recent release of the kernel
took about nine weeks,
and it brought in the work
of two thousand six hundred
and fifty-two developers.

<!-- S18 -->
It has become one of the largest
collaborative software projects
humanity has ever created.

<!-- S19 -->
And remember that question?

<!-- S20 -->
Where does Linux actually run?

<!-- S21 -->
Well, the answer starts with
understanding what Linux actually is.
And here's the really important part.

<!-- S22 -->
Linux isn't really
one operating system.

<!-- S23 -->
Linux is a kernel.

<!-- S24 -->
Think of the kernel as the part
of the system that sits
between software and hardware.
It manages the processors,
the memory and the devices.

<!-- S25 -->
Around that kernel, you can build
very different systems.
A desktop computer.
A server.
A tiny embedded device.
A supercomputer.

<!-- S26 -->
And things that don't look
like computers at all.

<!-- S27 -->
Which brings us back
to our original question.
Where is Linux actually running?

<!-- S28 -->
And now we can finally answer it.

<!-- S29 -->
Let's start with something
you probably have right now.

<!-- S30 -->
Your phone.

<!-- S31 -->
If you're using Android,
you're already using
the Linux kernel.

<!-- S32 -->
And there are more than
three billion active Android devices,
in over a hundred
and ninety countries.

<!-- S33 -->
Android isn't simply Linux
in the same sense
as Ubuntu or Fedora.
But underneath Android
is the Linux kernel.

<!-- S34 -->
So that little computer
in your pocket?

<!-- S35 -->
Linux.

<!-- S36 -->
And that's just the beginning.

<!-- S37 -->
Look around your home.
There's probably another computer
nearby that you don't think of
as a computer at all.

<!-- S38 -->
Your Wi-Fi router.

<!-- S39 -->
That little plastic box
connecting your devices to the Internet
can contain a surprisingly
powerful computer.
And many routers run Linux.

<!-- S40 -->
You don't see the terminal.
You don't even know it's there.
You just connect to Wi-Fi.
But underneath the interface...

<!-- S41 -->
Linux.

<!-- S42 -->
What about your television?
Modern smart televisions
aren't really televisions anymore.
They're computers
with a screen attached.

<!-- S43 -->
And Linux is used
in many smart televisions
and other consumer electronics.

<!-- S44 -->
So you can sit on your couch,
turn on Netflix,
and never realise that Linux
may be involved
in several different places
between your remote control
and the film you're watching.

<!-- S45 -->
But now let's make the jump

<!-- S46 -->
The Internet.

<!-- S47 -->
When you open a website,
you're usually not connecting
to some magical place
called the cloud.
You're talking to computers.
Lots of computers.
Servers in data centres.

<!-- S48 -->
And Linux has become
enormously important
in server infrastructure.
Web servers.
Databases.
Containers.

<!-- S49 -->
So when you browse the web,
there's a good chance
you're interacting with Linux
without ever seeing it.

<!-- S50 -->
And this is where things
start getting strange.

<!-- S51 -->
Linux isn't just sitting
inside a few devices.
It is part of the infrastructure
underneath much
of the modern Internet.

<!-- S52 -->
Now let's go to
the opposite end of the scale.

<!-- S53 -->
Supercomputers.

<!-- S54 -->
These are machines built to perform
enormous amounts of computation.
Weather simulations.
Astronomy.
Climate modelling.

<!-- S55 -->
Twice a year, someone publishes
a list of the five hundred

in the world.

<!-- S56 -->
All five hundred of them run Linux.

<!-- S57 -->
And that has been true

since two thousand and seventeen.

<!-- S58 -->
Think about that contrast.
The same kernel that can be running
inside a tiny embedded computer
can also be running on machines
containing enormous numbers
of processors.

<!-- S59 -->
A hobby project started
by a Finnish student
ended up powering some
of the most powerful computers
humanity has ever built.

<!-- S60 -->
And Linux doesn't only live
in buildings.
It can be found in things that move.

<!-- S61 -->
Cars run Linux in various systems,

<!-- S62 -->

Robotics.
Drones.
Cameras.
Storage devices.
Network equipment.

<!-- S63 -->
The important thing is that

<!-- S64 -->

There doesn't even have to be a screen.

<!-- S65 -->

<!-- S66 -->
