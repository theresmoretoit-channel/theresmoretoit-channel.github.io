<!-- S02 -->
Inside each one

<!-- S03 -->

A rock with tiny dark spots,

<!-- S03b -->

<!-- S04 -->

Not proof of life.
A pattern that could have
a biological origin.
The rover can tell what the spots
are made of.
Not how they formed.

<!-- S05 -->
To find out what it really is,
the rock has to come to Earth.

<!-- S06 -->
NASA had a plan to bring it here.
Less than half a kilogram of rock,

<!-- S07 -->
That's more than twenty million dollars

<!-- S08 -->
And in January 2026,
the original programme
lost its funding.

<!-- S09 -->

In 1969, Apollo 11 brought back

<!-- S09b -->
And not only with astronauts.
In 2024, a Chinese robot
brought back samples
from the far side of the Moon.

<!-- S10 -->
So why can we bring rocks
back from the Moon,
but not half a kilogram from Mars?

<!-- S11 -->
Start with the obvious difference.
Mars is bigger.
Its gravity is more than twice
as strong as the Moon's.

<!-- S12 -->
To escape the Moon,
you need about two point four kilometres
per second.
To escape Mars, about five.

<!-- S13 -->
That sounds like twice the effort.
It's much worse.

<!-- S14 -->
If you've seen our video

you know the rocket equation.

<!-- S15 -->
Fuel doesn't grow in step with speed.
It grows exponentially,
because extra fuel
has to lift more fuel, too.

<!-- S16 -->
In a simple example,
a small rocket leaving the Moon
can be about half fuel.
Leaving Mars,
with the same kind of engine,
about three quarters.

<!-- S17 -->
And that rocket has to get there first.
Everything that launches from Mars
has to land on Mars.

<!-- S17b -->
The Moon has no atmosphere at all.
Mars has a thin one,
just enough to burn you up,
and too little for parachutes
to stop you on their own.

<!-- S18 -->
NASA's planned lander would have weighed
about two thousand three hundred
kilograms.
More than twice Perseverance.
It would have been the heaviest thing
ever landed on Mars.

<!-- S19 -->
Then it has to wait.
Through dust storms,
and nights of minus eighty degrees,
for the samples to arrive.

<!-- S19b -->
The best launch windows
come roughly every twenty-six months.
Miss one,
and the next is more than two years away.

<!-- S20 -->
And then it has to launch,
with no one there.
A radio signal from Earth takes
between three and twenty-two minutes.

<!-- S21 -->
No rocket has ever launched
from the surface of another planet.
The Moon isn't a planet.
A rocket launched from Mars
would be the first.

<!-- S22 -->
So is it worth it?

<!-- S23 -->
After all,
we already have pieces of Mars on Earth.

<!-- S24 -->
In 2025, the largest one ever found

Twenty-four and a half kilograms
of Martian rock.

<!-- S25 -->
It sold for
five point three million dollars.
About two hundred dollars a gram.

<!-- S26 -->
A hundred thousand times cheaper

So why not study meteorites?

<!-- S27 -->
Because a meteorite has no address.

<!-- S28 -->
It was blasted off Mars by an impact,

for millions of years,
and burned through our atmosphere.

<!-- S29 -->
Then it lay in a desert.
We don't know where on Mars it came from,

<!-- S29b -->

<!-- S29c -->

<!-- S30 -->
The rocks in Perseverance's tubes

We know exactly which layer

<!-- S31 -->

<!-- S32 -->
On Earth, the samples could go to

<!-- S39 -->
Why not just fly straight home?

<!-- S40 -->
A direct return would need

<!-- S41 -->

<!-- S42 -->

<!-- S44 -->

<!-- S45 -->

<!-- S46 -->
A lander, a rover, a rocket,

<!-- S48 -->

<!-- S49 -->

<!-- S53 -->

<!-- S54 -->

<!-- S55 -->

<!-- S56 -->

<!-- S56b -->

<!-- S61 -->
