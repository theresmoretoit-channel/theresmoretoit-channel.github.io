<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you could send a message
faster than light.

<!-- S02 -->
You send it to a friend
on a distant spaceship.
They read it and send a reply,
just as fast.

<!-- S03 -->
And the reply reaches you
before you've sent the message.

<!-- S04 -->
That's not a trick of the clocks,
and it's not science fiction.
It's what the equations of relativity say
could happen.

<!-- S05 -->
In our last video, we asked
what it would take
to travel faster than light.
Warp drives. Wormholes. Exotic matter.

<!-- S06 -->
This time, the harder question.
Why do physicists think
the universe might not allow it at all?

<!-- S07 -->
First, the speed limit itself.
Because it isn't a rule of thumb.
It has been measured.

<!-- S08 -->
In 1964, at MIT,
William Bertozzi fired electrons
down a tube and timed them
over about eight metres.

<!-- S09 -->
Then he gave them more energy.
And more.
In the end, thirty times as much.

<!-- S10 -->
At the lowest energy,
the electrons moved
at about eighty-seven per cent
of the speed of light.

<!-- S11 -->
With thirty times the energy?
So close to the speed of light
that his timing couldn't tell the difference.

<!-- S11b -->
Newton's physics had a different answer.
By his rules, the fastest of them
should have been moving
several times faster than light.

<!-- S12 -->
And the energy hadn't gone missing.
He caught the electrons
in an aluminium disc
and measured the heat.
It was all there.

<!-- S13 -->
The extra energy went in.
The electrons crept closer to light speed,
and stopped short.
That's the wall.

<!-- S13b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S14 -->
But a wall isn't a reason.
Why should there be a limit at all?

<!-- S15 -->
The answer hides in something
we never think about:
the word "now".

<!-- S16 -->
Picture a long train
racing past a station.
Lightning strikes both ends of it.

<!-- S17 -->
A woman on the platform
stands halfway between the strikes.
Both flashes reach her together.
For her, the strikes were simultaneous.

<!-- S18 -->
A passenger sits
in the middle of the train.
But she's moving towards the front flash
and away from the back one.

<!-- S19 -->
So the front flash reaches her first.

<!-- S20 -->
And because light travels
at the same speed for everyone,
the passenger concludes, correctly,
that the front was struck first.

<!-- S21 -->
Neither of them is wrong.
This was Einstein's own example.
Whether two distant events
happen at the same time
depends on how you're moving.

<!-- S22 -->
That sounds dangerous.
Could someone see an effect
before its cause?

<!-- S23 -->
No. And this is the key.

<!-- S24 -->
The order can only flip
for events so far apart
that not even light could get
from one to the other in time.

<!-- S25 -->
If one event can affect the other,
everyone agrees which came first.

<!-- S26 -->
In relativity,
the light-speed limit
is what keeps cause before effect.

<!-- S27 -->
Now let's break it.

<!-- S28 -->
Alice is on Earth.
Bob is on a spaceship,
one light-year away,
flying further out at ninety per cent
of the speed of light.

<!-- S29 -->
Alice sends him a message
that travels at twice the speed of light.

<!-- S30 -->
For Alice, nothing strange happens.
The message leaves,
and almost eleven months later,
it reaches Bob.

<!-- S31 -->
But the two events are so far apart
that light couldn't connect them.
So Bob and Alice
don't have to agree on their order.

<!-- S32 -->
And in Bob's frame,
the message arrives
before Alice sent it.

<!-- S33 -->
That's unsettling.
But it isn't a paradox yet.

<!-- S34 -->
Now Bob replies.
Same kind of signal,
twice the speed of light,
measured from his ship.

<!-- S35 -->
Work it through,
and his reply reaches Earth
about five months
before Alice sent her message.

<!-- S36 -->
Alice receives an answer
to a question she hasn't asked yet.

<!-- S37 -->
Einstein himself noticed this in 1907.
In 1970, three physicists gave it a name:
the tachyonic antitelephone.

<!-- S38 -->
And here's the problem.
Suppose the reply says:
"Don't send the message."

<!-- S39 -->
If Alice obeys,
there's nothing for Bob to answer.
So where did the reply come from?

<!-- S40 -->
An effect has prevented its own cause.

<!-- S41 -->
Combine faster-than-light signals
with the usual rules of relativity,
and you can construct a message
that arrives before it was sent.

<!-- S41a -->
That is a serious problem
for cause and effect.
Not proof that every imaginable shortcut
through spacetime must fail.

<!-- S41b -->
And it doesn't matter
what the signal is made of.
Radio, particles, anything.
All that counts
is that it's faster than light,
and that the usual rules hold.

<!-- S42 -->
Now, physics never proves
that something is impossible.
A new theory could change the rules.

<!-- S43 -->
But it would have to explain
why relativity has passed
every test we've given it.

<!-- S44 -->
And the tests are very good.
Modern experiments have searched
for any difference in the speed of light
between different directions.

<!-- S45 -->
Down to about one part
in a billion billion,
they found none.

<!-- S46 -->
And once, it looked
as though something had broken through.

<!-- S47 -->
In 2011, the OPERA experiment
sent neutrinos from CERN, near Geneva,
to a lab under a mountain in Italy,
seven hundred and thirty kilometres away.

<!-- S48 -->
They arrived about sixty billionths
of a second early.
Faster than light.

<!-- S48b -->
And it wasn't a fluke of statistics.
The result was far too strong for that.
Whatever was wrong
was wrong every single time.

<!-- S49 -->
The team didn't claim a revolution.
They published the result
and asked others to find the mistake.

<!-- S50 -->
Within months,
the investigation found problems
in the timing system,
including a faulty fibre-optic connection.

<!-- S51 -->
Once the timing was corrected
and the measurement repeated,
the faster-than-light result disappeared.

<!-- S52 -->
The lesson isn't that relativity
can never be wrong.
It's that an extraordinary result
has to survive checking.
This one didn't.

<!-- S53 -->
So signals can't simply outrun light.
But in our last video,
we met a different idea.

<!-- S54 -->
A wormhole.
A shortcut between two places,
where nothing has to outrun light,
because the path is shorter.

<!-- S55 -->
And in 1988, Kip Thorne
and two colleagues showed
how a wormhole could be turned
into a time machine.

<!-- S56 -->
It takes one trick
we've already seen.

<!-- S57 -->
A clock that travels close to light speed
runs slow
compared with one that stays at home.

<!-- S58 -->
Start with a wormhole
whose two mouths sit side by side.
Through the tunnel,
their clocks agree.

<!-- S59 -->
Now take one mouth on a long trip,
at ninety-nine per cent
of the speed of light,
and bring it back.

<!-- S60 -->
On Earth, ten years have passed.
For the travelling mouth,
less than a year and a half.

<!-- S61 -->
But through the tunnel,
the clocks at the two mouths still agree.

<!-- S62 -->
So step into the mouth that travelled,
and you come out of the other one
more than eight years earlier.

<!-- S63 -->
A time shift between the mouths
isn't yet a time machine.

<!-- S64 -->
That happens when the shift
is longer than light needs
to get from one mouth to the other
the ordinary way.

<!-- S65 -->
Then you can step out in the past,
walk back to the first mouth,
and arrive before you left.

<!-- S66 -->
None of this tells us
how to build such a wormhole,
or keep it open.
It's a solution on paper.

<!-- S67 -->
But it shows that the paradox
isn't only about signals.
Any shortcut through spacetime
may lead to the same place.

<!-- S68 -->
So does the universe forbid it?
There are two famous answers.

<!-- S69 -->
In 1992, Stephen Hawking proposed
that the laws of physics themselves
prevent time machines from forming.

<!-- S70 -->
His idea was
that as a wormhole came close
to becoming a time machine,
the restless energy of empty space
would build up inside it without limit,
and stop the time machine from forming.

<!-- S71 -->
He called it
the chronology protection conjecture.

<!-- S72 -->
And he pointed out, half jokingly,
that we have not been invaded
by hordes of tourists from the future.

<!-- S73 -->
It was a joke, not a proof.
In 2009, he threw a party
for time travellers,
and sent out the invitations
only afterwards.

<!-- S74 -->
Nobody came.

<!-- S75 -->
The Russian physicist Igor Novikov
answered differently.

<!-- S76 -->
Maybe the past can be visited,
but it can't be changed.

<!-- S77 -->
The physicist Joseph Polchinski
challenged the idea with a billiard ball.
Send it into a wormhole,
so that it comes out in the past

<!-- S78 -->
and knocks its younger self off course.
Then it never enters the wormhole.
A paradox.

<!-- S79 -->
But when Thorne and two colleagues
worked through the equations,
they found that the collision
could also happen differently.

<!-- S80 -->
A glancing blow.
The younger ball is nudged,
not stopped,
and still enters the wormhole.

<!-- S81 -->
For every starting point they tried,
they found at least one
self-consistent history.
For some, there was more than one.

<!-- S82 -->
Neither answer is proven.
Hawking's is a conjecture.
Novikov's is a principle,
not an observation.

<!-- S83 -->
So for now, the speed of light
isn't just a limit on our engines.
It may be what keeps
causes before their effects.

<!-- S84 -->
Either the universe
never lets a time machine form...

<!-- S85 -->
or it allows only histories
that never contradict themselves.

<!-- S86 -->
We don't yet know which, if either.
