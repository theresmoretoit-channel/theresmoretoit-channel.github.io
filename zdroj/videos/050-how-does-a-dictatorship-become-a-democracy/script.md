<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you are a dictator.
You control the government.
You control the army.
You control the police.
You control the newspapers.

<!-- S02 -->
And one morning somebody walks in
with an interesting suggestion.
Why don't we hold free elections?

<!-- S03 -->
So you ask the obvious question.

<!-- S03b -->
And what happens if I lose?

<!-- S04 -->
That is the whole problem in one line.
If losing an election costs you
your position, your money, your freedom,
possibly your life,
why would you ever agree to one?

<!-- S05 -->
And yet it has happened.
More than once.
One of the strangest examples
is an island you have heard of
for completely different reasons.

<!-- S05b -->
Taiwan.

<!-- S06 -->
In 1949, the Chinese Communist Party
won the civil war on the mainland,
and the government of the Republic of China,
led by the Kuomintang,
retreated to Taiwan.

<!-- S07 -->
If you know the usual story,
you probably picture what came next.
A small democratic island,
safely out of reach of the mainland.

<!-- S07b -->
Not quite.

<!-- S08 -->
Taiwan was an authoritarian state.
Martial law was declared in 1949,
and it stayed in place for 38 years.
Opposition was heavily restricted.
The press was controlled.

<!-- S09 -->
People suspected of political opposition
could be imprisoned.
In the years known as the White Terror,
tens of thousands were imprisoned,
and thousands were executed.

<!-- S10 -->
This was not a democracy
waiting patiently for its turn.
It was a dictatorship.

<!-- S11 -->
And then it started to change.
Taiwan industrialised.
Factories, exports, wages.
People got richer.
Education expanded. Cities grew.

<!-- S12 -->
A large middle class appeared,
which had not really existed before.
And alongside the money,
something less visible was growing.

<!-- S13 -->
People formed associations.
Students protested.
Journalists pushed at the edges
of what the censors would allow.
Opposition politicians organised,
even though parties were banned.

<!-- S14 -->
The government could still arrest them.
It did.
But a society is easy to control
only while it stays simple,
and this one was getting complicated.

<!-- S15 -->
And then, in September 1986,
the opposition did something
that was straightforwardly illegal.
They announced a new political party.

<!-- S16 -->
Under martial law,
that was enough to be arrested for.
The government knew about the meeting.
It had the names.

<!-- S17 -->
And it did nothing.

<!-- S18 -->
The following year,
Taiwan lifted martial law.
The rules on parties and newspapers
came off gradually after that.

<!-- S19 -->
In 1996, Taiwan held
its first direct presidential election.
And then came the moment
that actually matters.

<!-- S19b -->
The year 2000.

<!-- S20 -->
The Kuomintang had run Taiwan
for half a century.
And it lost.
The opposition won the presidency.

<!-- S20b -->
And the tanks did not roll.

<!-- S21 -->
The army did not overturn the count.
Nobody announced that the result
had been misinterpreted.
The government simply changed hands.

<!-- S22 -->
The old ruling party carried on existing.
It became the opposition.
And eight years later it won
an election and came back.

<!-- S23 -->
Sit with that for a moment.
A party that had run
an authoritarian state for decades
discovered that it could lose power
and survive it.

<!-- S24 -->
So why did Taiwan do it?
Did the rulers wake up one morning
and become nice people?
That seems unlikely.

<!-- S25 -->
Does getting richer
automatically make a country democratic?
Hold that thought.
Because Taiwan was not the only place
where something like this happened.

<!-- S26 -->
Look at South Korea.
For decades it was run
by authoritarian governments.
And for decades it was also doing
the other thing.

<!-- S27 -->
Industry, exports, schools,
a huge urban middle class.
And by 1987, the protests were enormous.

<!-- S28 -->
And there was another pressure
in the room.
Seoul was hosting the Olympic Games
the following year.
The world would be watching.

<!-- S29 -->
The protests did not stop.
And the ruling establishment
accepted major reforms.
The June 29 Declaration promised
direct presidential elections.

<!-- S30 -->
Notice the shape of that.
It is not simply
a dictator being overthrown.

<!-- S31 -->
It is partly the people in power
deciding that keeping the old system
is becoming harder,
and more dangerous,
than changing it.

<!-- S32 -->
Chile is stranger still.
Augusto Pinochet's military government
had written a constitution
with a referendum built into it.

<!-- S33 -->
In 1988, the country was asked
whether Pinochet should continue
as president.
The regime expected the vote
to hand it some legitimacy.

<!-- S33b -->
The No side won.

<!-- S34 -->
And the result was accepted.
Elections followed in 1989,
and Pinochet left the presidency in 1990.
It was not clean,
and it was not simple.

<!-- S35 -->
Pinochet remained commander-in-chief
of the army for another eight years.
And the political system still contained
important remnants of the old regime.

<!-- S36 -->
But the central fact stands.
A dictator had put his continuation
in office to a vote.
And lost.

<!-- S37 -->
Spain did yet another version
of the same thing.
Francisco Franco died in 1975,
and Spain did not collapse
into revolution.

<!-- S38 -->
Instead, reformers inside the system
began taking the system apart.
Parties were legalised.
Elections were held in 1977.
A new constitution followed in 1978.

<!-- S39 -->
Not a revolution.
Not the destruction
of the old political class.
A transition.

<!-- S39b -->
By the way, if you'd like to see
where the next question takes us,
consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S40 -->
So there is a pattern here,
and it is not
that dictators become democrats.

<!-- S41 -->
It is that a regime can reach a point
where its own leaders start asking
a completely different question.
Not how do we hold on forever.

<!-- S42 -->
But how do we change this
without destroying ourselves.
Which brings us to the thing
this whole video is really about.

<!-- S42b -->
The exit problem.

<!-- S43 -->
Imagine two dictatorships.
In the first one, the ruling party
knows roughly what losing looks like.

<!-- S44 -->
We would still be allowed to exist.
We would keep our houses.
We would keep our businesses.
We could stand in the next election.
We might even win it.

<!-- S45 -->
Losing is unpleasant.
It is not the end.
Now the second dictatorship.

<!-- S46 -->
If we lose, they take everything.
Our families are prosecuted.
Our allies go to prison.
So do we.

<!-- S46b -->
Possibly worse.

<!-- S47 -->
Now ask which of those two groups
is more likely to agree
to a free election.
It is not a hard question.

<!-- S48 -->
The second group has every reason
to hold on, whatever that costs.
Even if the country
would be better off without them.

<!-- S49 -->
Even if they would privately prefer
a quiet way out.
Their survival is now welded
to the survival of the regime.

<!-- S50 -->
And at that point,
democracy stops being a reform.
It becomes a gamble
with their own lives on the table.

<!-- S51 -->
Which may explain one of the strangest
comparisons in modern politics.
After Mao Zedong died,
China changed enormously.

<!-- S52 -->
Deng Xiaoping and the leaders after him
opened up the economy.
Markets expanded.
The country became dramatically richer.

<!-- S53 -->
Education expanded.
An enormous middle class appeared.
China became deeply connected
to the world economy.

<!-- S54 -->
In other words, China went through
a transformation that, in other countries,
had sometimes gone alongside
political liberalisation.

<!-- S54b -->
But here, it didn't.

<!-- S55 -->
The Communist Party kept
its monopoly on national political power.
Economic reform did not
turn into political reform.

<!-- S56 -->
Which tells us something
worth being careful about.
Economic development can create
the conditions that make
democracy possible.

<!-- S56b -->
It does not make it inevitable.

<!-- S57 -->
Institutions matter.
Civil society matters.
It matters whether opposition groups
can organise, compete,
and eventually take power
without destroying the system
they are replacing.

<!-- S58 -->
And most of all,
the people currently holding power
need some reason to believe
that losing will not destroy them.

<!-- S59 -->
That is also why this is so hard
to predict from the outside.
Sometimes a regime is overthrown.
Sometimes it collapses.
Sometimes it loses a war.

<!-- S60 -->
And sometimes the people
running the dictatorship
quietly take parts of it apart themselves.
Not because they became saints.
Because the arithmetic changed.

<!-- S61 -->
Which makes Taiwan's story
more awkward than
good democracy beats bad dictatorship.
The dictatorship came first.

<!-- S62 -->
The economic transformation happened
under that dictatorship.
The civil society that eventually
demanded political freedom
grew up inside it.

<!-- S63 -->
And some of the people
who took the authoritarian system apart
were products of it.

<!-- S64 -->
That is the paradox.
A dictatorship can create
the conditions that make
dictatorship hard to keep.

<!-- S65 -->
And there is a deeper one underneath it.
A dictator does not have to love
democracy to allow it.
He only has to believe
that he can survive losing.

<!-- S66 -->
We usually imagine democracy beginning
when ordinary people demand
the right to choose.
That is half of it.

<!-- S67 -->
The other half is much quieter.
For any of it to hold,
the people who already have power
have to accept something
that sounds almost trivial.

<!-- S67b -->
They might lose.

<!-- S68 -->
And when they do,
nothing terrible has to happen.
They go home.
They form an opposition party.
They criticise the new government.

<!-- S69 -->
They campaign again.
And sometimes they win again.
The peaceful transfer of power works
because losing is survivable.

<!-- S69b -->
That is the trick.
That is the whole trick.

<!-- S70 -->
So a regime that once jailed
its political opponents
ended up building a system
in which those opponents could win.
And the old rulers could accept it.

<!-- S71 -->
No revolution. No civil war.
A ballot box.

<!-- S72 -->
Not a dictatorship that was defeated.
A dictatorship that turned into a place
where losing was safer
than refusing to change.
