<!-- S01 -->
You put a nozzle into your car.
You press a button.
A few seconds later,
you've got petrol.

<!-- S02 -->
Simple.

<!-- S03 -->
But where did that petrol come from?
Was it already hiding
inside the crude oil?
Did someone just boil the oil
until petrol came out?

<!-- S04 -->
And what about diesel?
What happens if the crude oil
is really heavy?

<!-- S05 -->
And perhaps
the most important question.

<!-- S06 -->
If I somehow got my hands
on a barrel of crude oil,
could I make my own petrol at home?

<!-- S07 -->
To answer that,
we first need to see what a refinery
actually does to crude oil.

<!-- S08 -->
So the crude oil arrives.
First, it has to be cleaned up.
Water, salts and other unwanted
material are removed.

<!-- S09 -->
Then it's heated.
And this is where
the classic refinery image appears.
The enormous distillation column.

<!-- S10 -->
It looks like a giant industrial tower.
And essentially, it is
a giant separation machine.

<!-- S11 -->
But before we climb into it,
one number.

<!-- S12 -->
A barrel of crude oil
is a hundred and fifty-nine litres.
American refineries pour in
a hundred and fifty-nine litres...
and get out about
a hundred and seventy.

<!-- S13 -->
More comes out than went in.

<!-- S14 -->
Nobody is cheating.
The products are simply less dense
than the crude they came from.

<!-- S15 -->
A refinery is not a machine
that takes petrol out of oil.
It's a machine that makes
new things out of it.

<!-- S16 -->
And to see how,
we have to look at what is actually
inside that barrel.

<!-- S17 -->
Crude oil is not
a liquid version of petrol.
And it isn't one chemical compound.

<!-- S18 -->
It's a mixture of thousands
of different hydrocarbons,
along with sulphur, nitrogen,
oxygen and metals.

<!-- S19 -->
And they come in wildly different sizes.
Some are small.
Some are so large and heavy
that they behave more like tar.

<!-- S20 -->
So when we say oil,
we're talking about
an enormous molecular soup.
And not all of that soup is the same.

<!-- S21 -->
Crude oil is described
using two characteristics.
One is light versus heavy.
Light crude contains more
small hydrocarbon molecules.
Heavy crude contains more
large, complex ones.

<!-- S22 -->
The second is sweet versus sour.
That has nothing to do with taste.
Sweet crude contains little sulphur.
Sour crude contains more.
So two barrels can look similar...
while being very different
raw materials.

<!-- S23 -->
Which is one reason why refineries
are not all built the same way.

<!-- S24 -->
Back to the column.
The refinery isn't separating
molecules one by one.

<!-- S25 -->
It's separating groups of hydrocarbons
according to their boiling behaviour.

<!-- S26 -->
The crude is heated to around
three hundred and fifty
degrees Celsius.
Hot vapour rises up the column
while cooler liquid flows downward.

<!-- S27 -->
Different parts of the mixture
condense at different levels.
Near the top,
the lightest hydrocarbons.
Gases.
Below them, naphtha,
boiling up to about
a hundred and ninety degrees.

<!-- S28 -->
Then kerosene,
roughly a hundred and ninety
to two hundred and fifty.
Then the diesel range,
very roughly two hundred and fifty
to three hundred and fifty.

<!-- S29 -->
And at the bottom, the stuff
that really doesn't want to evaporate.
Heavy oil and residue.
Those numbers are deliberately vague,
and that isn't me being careless.

<!-- S30 -->
The fractions overlap,
and where one ends and the next begins
is something the refinery chooses.
It's a setting, not a law of nature.

<!-- S31 -->
And at this point, you might think.
Great.
We've separated the petrol
from the diesel.

<!-- S32 -->
But...
not really.

<!-- S33 -->
Here's the big secret
of modern refining.
A refinery doesn't just
separate hydrocarbons.
It changes them.

<!-- S34 -->
Here's why it has to.

<!-- S35 -->
Take a good, light crude
and simply distil it.
About twenty per cent of the barrel
comes off in the petrol range.

<!-- S36 -->
Twenty per cent.

<!-- S37 -->
Meanwhile, petrol is close to half
of everything a refinery sells.
And it gets worse.
That straight-run petrol has
an octane rating of about fifty.

<!-- S38 -->
Your car wants ninety-five.

<!-- S39 -->
Too little petrol.
And what there is,
knocks too easily.
Both gaps have to be closed
by chemistry.

<!-- S40 -->
First, quantity.
We can break large molecules
into smaller ones.
This is called cracking.

<!-- S41 -->
And the name is wonderfully literal.
Large hydrocarbon molecules
are chemically broken
into smaller molecules.
One important method
is catalytic cracking.

<!-- S42 -->
Another is hydrocracking,
which uses hydrogen under high pressure
together with catalysts.
This matters most
when the refinery is working
with heavy crude.

<!-- S43 -->
Suddenly those enormous molecules
aren't something to be sold off cheaply.
They're raw material for petrol.
So the refinery isn't just asking...
what's already in this crude oil?

<!-- S44 -->
It's asking...
what can we turn it into?

<!-- S45 -->
Now for the second gap.
Quality.

<!-- S46 -->
Petrol isn't one chemical.
It's blended from several
different refinery streams.
Some hydrocarbons are reformed
into molecules with better properties
for petrol engines.

<!-- S47 -->
Others are rearranged
by processes such as isomerisation.
And cracked products
contribute as well.

<!-- S48 -->
The result is a carefully
controlled mixture.
And that octane number
measures its resistance to knocking.

<!-- S49 -->
The scale is beautifully arbitrary.

<!-- S50 -->
One particular molecule, heptane,
was defined as zero.
Another, iso-octane,
was defined as a hundred.
Everything else is measured
against those two.

<!-- S51 -->
So when you see ninety-five
at a filling station,
that number isn't telling you
how much energy is in the fuel.

<!-- S52 -->
It's telling you how well it resists
igniting before it is supposed to.

<!-- S53 -->
The petrol in your tank is therefore
the result of a whole chain
of physical separation,
chemical transformation
and precise blending.

<!-- S54 -->
Diesel follows the same story.
Some of it comes straight
from distillation.
The rest is built by the same
kind of chemistry.

<!-- S55 -->
And diesel has its own requirements.
Its ignition quality is measured
by the cetane number,
not the octane number.

<!-- S56 -->
So diesel isn't simply
the stuff that's heavier than petrol.
It's a deliberately manufactured fuel.

<!-- S57 -->
There's another problem.
Crude oil contains sulphur.
And we don't want large amounts
of it ending up in modern fuels.

<!-- S58 -->
So refineries run processes
such as hydrotreating.
Hydrogen reacts with
the sulphur-containing molecules.

<!-- S59 -->
And here's a nice refinery trick.
The sulphur doesn't get thrown away.
It comes out the other end
as solid, elemental sulphur.

<!-- S60 -->
Something that started
as an undesirable contaminant
gets sold as a product.

<!-- S61 -->
So what actually comes out
of that hundred and fifty-nine litres?

<!-- S62 -->
In American refineries,
roughly seventy-four litres of petrol.
Forty-seven of diesel and heating oil.
Seventeen of jet fuel.

<!-- S63 -->
And then the rest.
Petroleum coke, refinery gas,
bitumen, lubricants,
feedstocks for the chemical industry,
and sulphur.

<!-- S64 -->
Which is why saying...
one barrel gives you
so many litres of petrol...
is misleading.

<!-- S65 -->
A refinery isn't a petrol factory.
It's a chemical factory
making many things at once.

<!-- S66 -->
And now we arrive at
the question we started with.
Suppose you had a barrel of crude oil.
Could you make your own petrol?

<!-- S67 -->
In principle...
yes.

<!-- S68 -->
Nothing about it
violates the laws of physics.
You can heat crude oil.
You can separate fractions
by boiling point.
You can transform hydrocarbons.

<!-- S69 -->
But there's a rather large problem.
Actually...
There are several.

<!-- S70 -->
You might think.
I'll just build a still.
Heat the crude oil.
Collect whatever boils off
in the petrol range.
Done.
But you already know what you'd get.
About a fifth of the barrel,
at octane fifty.

<!-- S71 -->
You haven't removed the sulphur.
You haven't produced a fuel
that behaves correctly
in a modern engine.

<!-- S72 -->
So simple distillation
gets you part of the way.
But only part of the way.

<!-- S73 -->
Fine.
I'll build a real refinery.

<!-- S74 -->
Okay.
Let's add catalytic cracking.
And hydrocracking.
And reforming.
And isomerisation.
And hydrotreating.
Let's add hydrogen production.
High-pressure equipment.
Catalysts.

<!-- S75 -->
Heat exchangers.
Distillation columns.
Compressors.
Pumps.
Control systems.
Storage tanks.
Safety systems.
Waste treatment.
And laboratories
to continuously analyse the products.

<!-- S76 -->
Congratulations.
You have just started building...
a refinery.

<!-- S77 -->
And suddenly the reason
refineries are enormous
becomes obvious.
They aren't enormous because someone
couldn't find a sufficiently
large cooking pot.

<!-- S78 -->
They're enormous because
that is what modern fuel takes.

<!-- S79 -->
And here's the part
I find most fascinating.
When you put petrol into your car,
you aren't putting crude oil into it.
You aren't even putting
one particular substance into it.

<!-- S80 -->
You're putting a carefully
engineered mixture of hydrocarbons.
Some molecules may have started
as light components
of the original crude.

<!-- S81 -->
Others were created
by breaking larger molecules apart.
Others were chemically rearranged.
So the petrol in your tank
is not something
that was found inside the Earth.

<!-- S82 -->
It is something that was
manufactured from crude oil.

<!-- S83 -->
Not liquid dinosaur juice.
Not simply distilled crude oil.
But the end product of one
of the most complicated
industrial processes
humans have developed.

<!-- S84 -->
All so that, eventually,
you can put a nozzle into your car,
press a button,
and drive away.

<!-- S85 -->
Not bad for a barrel of black goo.
