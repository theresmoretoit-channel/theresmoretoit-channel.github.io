<!-- S01 -->
This is the story of a schoolteacher
in Virginia.
Forty years old, married,
with an ordinary, stable life.

<!-- S02 -->
Then, in 2000,
something in him changed.
He developed sexual urges
towards children,
urges he had never had before.
And he began to act on them.

<!-- S03 -->
He was convicted
and sent to a treatment programme.
Even there, he couldn't stop,
and he was thrown out.

<!-- S04 -->
The night before his sentencing,
he went to hospital
with a headache that wouldn't go away.
He told the doctors he knew
what he was doing was wrong.
He just couldn't stop himself.

<!-- S05 -->
A brain scan found a tumour
the size of an egg,
pressing on a part of the brain
that helps us hold back our impulses.

<!-- S06 -->
Surgeons removed it.
The urges went away.
He finished the programme
and went back home.

<!-- S07 -->
Less than a year later,
the headaches came back.
So did the urges.

<!-- S08 -->
The tumour had grown back.
Surgeons removed it again,
and again, the urges disappeared.

<!-- S09 -->
So here's the question.
Was he responsible for what he did?

<!-- S10 -->
Most people would say: not really.
Something physical in his brain
changed what he wanted,
and he never chose that tumour.

<!-- S11 -->
But what if there was no tumour?
What if every choice you've ever made
was shaped by things you never chose?

<!-- S12 -->
Your genes. Your childhood.
The people around you.
The state of your brain
at this very second.

<!-- S13 -->
His case only looks special
because we can see the cause.
It's right there, on a scan.

<!-- S14 -->
For the rest of us, the causes
are spread out, tangled and invisible.
But invisible doesn't mean
they aren't there.

<!-- S15 -->
So let's ask a stranger question.
Not whether we have free will,
but what it would actually mean
if we didn't.

<!-- S16 -->
For a while, it looked like science
had already settled it.

<!-- S17 -->
In 1983, a researcher called
Benjamin Libet asked people
to do something very simple:
flick their wrist,
whenever they felt like it.

<!-- S18 -->
He measured their brain activity,
and had them watch a dot circling a clock
and note the exact moment
they decided to move.

<!-- S19 -->
The brain went first.
A slow build-up of electrical activity
began about half a second
before the movement.

<!-- S20 -->
The feeling of wanting to move
came later,
only about a fifth of a second
before the movement.

<!-- S21 -->
In other words, the brain seemed
to get going a third of a second
before the person was even aware
of wanting to move.

<!-- S22 -->
Later experiments pushed it further.
In 2008, researchers used brain scans
to predict which of two buttons
people would press,
up to seven seconds before
they were aware of choosing.

<!-- S23 -->
It looked as if consciousness
wasn't the boss at all.
More like a press office,
announcing decisions
that had already been made.

<!-- S24 -->
But look closer,
and it gets less dramatic.
That 2008 prediction was right
about sixty per cent of the time.
A coin toss gets fifty.

<!-- S25 -->
And in 2012, another team offered
a different explanation
for Libet's signal.

<!-- S26 -->
The brain is never quiet.
Its activity drifts up and down
all the time.
When it happens to drift over a threshold,
a movement gets triggered.

<!-- S27 -->
So if you only look at the moments
just before a movement,
you will always see a rise.
Not because the decision came early,
but because that's what
crossing a line looks like.

<!-- S28 -->
So the experiments that were supposed
to disprove free will
don't quite do that.

<!-- S29 -->
But here's the uncomfortable part.
The real problem never depended
on those experiments.

<!-- S30 -->
Think of any decision you made today.
What to eat. What to say.
Whether to keep watching this video.

<!-- S31 -->
That decision had causes.
Your mood. Your hunger. Your habits.
The way your neurons
happened to be wired this morning.

<!-- S32 -->
And each of those causes
had causes of its own,
going back to your parents, your genes,
and things that happened
long before you were born.

<!-- S33 -->
So where in that chain is the part of you
that's free from all of it?
The part that steps in from outside
and decides?

<!-- S34 -->
Maybe the chain isn't complete.
Maybe somewhere, deep in physics,
there's genuine randomness.

<!-- S35 -->
But would that help?
If a choice popped out of pure chance,
it wouldn't be yours either.
It would be a dice roll
that happened to land in your head.

<!-- S36 -->
So that's the trap.
If your decision was caused,
you didn't control the cause.
If it wasn't caused, it was random.

<!-- S37 -->
Either way, it's hard to see
where free will is supposed to fit.

<!-- S38 -->
Some philosophers reply
that free will was never about
escaping causes.
Being free means acting
on your own reasons,
without anyone forcing you.

<!-- S39 -->
By that definition,
you're free most of the time.
But it's not the freedom
most people mean
when they ask whether free will exists:
the sense that, in exactly
the same moment,
we could have done otherwise.

<!-- S39b -->
There's a catch.
You only ever live one version of events.
You can't go back to that moment
and choose again to see what happens.
So whether you could have done otherwise
is something nobody can check,
not even you.

<!-- S40 -->
Nobody has proved
that this feeling is an illusion.
But suppose it is.
What would actually change?

<!-- S41 -->
Start with punishment.
Today, we punish partly
to protect other people,
and partly because we feel
some people deserve to suffer.

<!-- S42 -->
Without free will,
that second reason gets shaky.
If nobody could ultimately
have chosen otherwise,
nobody ultimately deserves
to suffer for it.

<!-- S43 -->
But the first reason survives.
We would still need to keep
dangerous people away from others.

<!-- S44 -->
Think of how we treat someone
with a dangerous infectious disease.
We might keep them in quarantine.
But we don't say they deserve it.

<!-- S45 -->
Prisons built on that idea
would look different.
Less about revenge.
More about prevention and repair.

<!-- S45b -->
A sentence wouldn't be measured
by how angry the crime makes us,
but by how dangerous the person still is,
and what it would take to change that.

<!-- S45c -->
That cuts both ways.
Someone who's no longer a risk
could go home sooner.
Someone who still is
might stay longer
than a judge would set today.

<!-- S45d -->
The question in court would shift
from "How much does he deserve?"
to "What will stop this
from happening again?"

<!-- S46 -->
What about guilt?
It wouldn't disappear.
You'd still know
that what you did hurt someone.

<!-- S47 -->
You could still apologise,
make up for the damage,
and try to do better.
None of that needs a self
standing outside the chain of causes.

<!-- S48 -->
Achievement wouldn't vanish either.
If you trained for years
to run a marathon,
the training really happened.
Your discipline came from somewhere,
but you still did something hard.

<!-- S48b -->
What would change is how we see
the people who didn't make it.
If your talent, your health
and the people who believed in you
all came from somewhere,
so did someone else's lack of them.

<!-- S48c -->
Success would still deserve admiration.
Failure just wouldn't prove
that someone didn't try.

<!-- S49 -->
And regret still has a job.
It's how a brain learns
not to repeat a mistake.

<!-- S50 -->
It just doesn't need the belief
that in that exact moment,
with that exact brain,
you could have done otherwise.

<!-- S51 -->
But there's a worry.
If people stop believing in free will,
won't they stop behaving well?

<!-- S52 -->
In 2008, two psychologists tested that.
Some volunteers read a passage
arguing that free will is an illusion.
Then everyone took a test
where cheating was easy.

<!-- S53 -->
The people who'd read
about free will being an illusion
cheated more.
It became one of the best-known findings
on the subject.

<!-- S54 -->
But when other labs
repeated the experiment
with twenty times as many people,
the effect didn't show up.

<!-- S55 -->
So there's no good evidence
that losing faith in free will
makes people worse.

<!-- S56 -->
So what would we really lose?
Probably less than it seems.

<!-- S57 -->
We would still plan. Still decide.
Still love, argue, try and fail.
Your brain would still weigh
its options.
It just wouldn't be doing it
from outside the universe.

<!-- S57b -->
And it wouldn't mean
you could sit back
and let fate do the work.

<!-- S57c -->
Your thinking, your effort,
your decision to try again,
those aren't outside the chain of causes.
They're part of it,
often the part that decides
what happens next.

<!-- S58 -->
What might change
is how we look at other people.
Instead of asking,
"How could anyone
make such a terrible choice?",
we might ask more often,
"What made that choice possible?"

<!-- S59 -->
With the teacher, we asked that question,
because the cause showed up on a scan.

<!-- S60 -->
If free will doesn't exist,
everyone deserves the same question.
Their causes just don't show up
on a scan.

<!-- S61 -->
So here's a question for you.
If you found out tomorrow
that free will doesn't exist,
who would you judge differently?
Let me know in the comments.
