<!-- S01 -->
You leave a banana on the kitchen counter
and forget about it for a few days.
It goes brown. Then black.
It starts to smell.

<!-- S02 -->
And then, one morning,
there are tiny flies circling it.
A few days ago,
there wasn't a single fly in the kitchen.
So where did they come from?

<!-- S02b -->
Did you just witness life
appearing from nothing?

<!-- S03 -->
They're fruit flies.
And they're drawn to fermenting fruit,
and to the yeast growing on it.
The smell can pull them in from outside.

<!-- S04 -->
Some slip in through a gap
in a window.
Some ride in as eggs
on other fruit you brought home.

<!-- S05 -->
And once a few are in, they breed.
In warm conditions,
it takes about ten days
from egg to flying adult.
And one female can lay
around five hundred eggs.

<!-- S06 -->
So nothing appeared out of nowhere.
Every one of those flies
hatched from an egg
laid by another fly.

<!-- S07 -->
That sounds obvious.
But it took people
more than two thousand years
to work it out.
And the answer left behind
a problem we still haven't solved.

<!-- S08 -->
Because if life only ever
comes from life...
where did the first life come from?

<!-- S09 -->
For most of history,
the answer to the kitchen mystery
was much simpler.
Life could just start on its own.

<!-- S10 -->
More than two thousand years ago,
Aristotle wrote that eels grew out of mud,
and that some insects formed
from dew falling on leaves.

<!-- S11 -->
The idea is called spontaneous generation,
and it lasted for centuries.

<!-- S12 -->
In the 1600s, the Flemish chemist
Jan Baptist van Helmont
even wrote down a recipe for mice.

<!-- S13 -->
Put some dirty underwear
in a jar of wheat.
Wait twenty-one days.
And mice will appear.

<!-- S14 -->
And to be fair, it looks convincing.
Leave grain in a barn,
and mice eventually appear.
But the barn already had mice.

<!-- S15 -->
In 1668, an Italian physician
called Francesco Redi
decided to stop arguing
and start watching.

<!-- S16 -->
He put pieces of meat into jars.
Some he left open.
Some he sealed shut.
The open jars filled with maggots.
The sealed ones didn't.

<!-- S17 -->
But there was an obvious objection.
Sealing the jar
also cut off the air.
Maybe life needed fresh air to start.

<!-- S18 -->
So Redi covered some jars
with fine gauze instead.
Air could get in.
Flies couldn't.

<!-- S19 -->
No maggots in the meat.
But maggots on top of the gauze,
dropped there by flies
that couldn't get in.

<!-- S20 -->
The meat wasn't making life.
The flies were.

<!-- S21 -->
That should have settled it.
But a few years later,
microscopes revealed a whole world
of tiny living things.

<!-- S22 -->
And they seemed to turn up
in any broth left out,
with no eggs anyone could see.
So maybe spontaneous generation
still worked... just smaller.

<!-- S23 -->
Almost two hundred years after Redi,
in 1859,
Louis Pasteur took on the question
in Paris.

<!-- S24 -->
He filled glass flasks with broth,
heated their necks
and bent them into a long curve,
like the neck of a swan.

<!-- S25 -->
Then he boiled the broth
to kill anything already in it.
Air could still get in
through the open end.

<!-- S26 -->
But the dust and microbes
drifting in with it
settled in the bend of the neck,
and never reached the broth.

<!-- S27 -->
The broth stayed clear.
For months. For years.

<!-- S28 -->
Then he broke the neck off a flask,
so the dust could fall straight in.
Within a day or two, it was cloudy.

<!-- S29 -->
In 1864, Pasteur told an audience
at the Sorbonne
that spontaneous generation
would never recover from this blow.

<!-- S30 -->
Life comes from life.
And that is exactly
what's happening on your kitchen counter.

<!-- S31 -->
But look at what that did
to the bigger question.

<!-- S32 -->
Around the same time,
Charles Darwin argued
that every living thing
descends from earlier living things.

<!-- S33 -->
Put Pasteur and Darwin together,
and you get a chain.
Life from life, back and back,
for billions of years...
and it can't go on forever.

<!-- S34 -->
Earth formed about
four and a half billion years ago.
At first it was far too hot
for anything to survive.

<!-- S35 -->
The oldest widely accepted traces of life
are about
three and a half billion years old.
Layered rocks in Western Australia,
built up by mats of microbes.

<!-- S36 -->
So at some point,
on a planet with no life at all,
something that wasn't alive
became something that was.

<!-- S37 -->
Scientists call that step abiogenesis.
And it seems to clash
with everything Pasteur proved.

<!-- S38 -->
But Pasteur never showed
that life can't come from chemistry.
He showed it doesn't happen
in a swan-neck flask,
even after years.

<!-- S39 -->
The early Earth had
hundreds of millions of years,
and whole oceans to work with.
And it had one more advantage.

<!-- S40 -->
Darwin spotted it in a letter in 1871.
Today, he wrote,
any chemistry on its way to life
would be devoured instantly.

<!-- S41 -->
Your banana never stood a chance.
Yeast got to it first.
Then the flies came for the yeast.

<!-- S42 -->
Before the first life,
there was nothing to get there first.

<!-- S43 -->
In that same letter,
Darwin began with a warning:
oh, what a big if.
Then he imagined a warm little pond,
with ammonia and salts,
light, heat and electricity.

<!-- S44 -->
In 1952, a student called Stanley Miller,
working with Harold Urey,
tried to fill in that if.

<!-- S45 -->
He sealed water, methane,
ammonia and hydrogen in glass,
heated it,
and fired sparks through it
to stand in for lightning.

<!-- S46 -->
After a week,
the water had turned a murky red.
And in it were amino acids,
the building blocks of proteins.

<!-- S47 -->
In 2008, researchers re-tested vials
from a steamier version of his experiment,
and found more than twenty amino acids.

<!-- S48 -->
Miller's gases were probably not
quite what the early atmosphere held.
But other mixtures
make building blocks too.
And so does space.

<!-- S49 -->
In 1969, a meteorite fell
near Murchison, in Australia.
Inside it were
more than seventy kinds of amino acids.

<!-- S50 -->
So the ingredients aren't the hard part.
Chemistry makes them on its own,
on Earth and off it.

<!-- S51 -->
The hard part is the next step.
A heap of building blocks isn't alive,
the same way a bag of letters
isn't a book.

<!-- S52 -->
Life has to carry instructions.
And it has to copy them.

<!-- S53 -->
In your cells, that's a team effort.
DNA stores the instructions.
Proteins do the work,
including copying the DNA.

<!-- S54 -->
But proteins are built
from DNA's instructions.
And DNA is copied by proteins.
So which came first?

<!-- S55 -->
In 1982, Thomas Cech found a molecule
that broke the loop.
A piece of RNA,
DNA's chemical cousin,
that could cut and join itself
with no protein involved.

<!-- S56 -->
RNA can carry instructions, like DNA.
And some RNA can do work, like a protein.
One molecule, both jobs.

<!-- S57 -->
That's the idea behind
what's called the RNA world.
Early life may have run on RNA,
before DNA and proteins took over.

<!-- S58 -->
And there's a clue
inside every one of your cells.
Every protein in your body
was put together by a machine
whose working heart is RNA.

<!-- S59 -->
In 2026, researchers reported
a tiny RNA called QT45.
From ready-made pieces,
it can build a copy of itself.
But no RNA has yet been shown
to keep copying itself,
round after round.

<!-- S60 -->
And nobody knows where it happened.
A warm pond,
drying out and filling up again?
Or hot vents on the dark ocean floor?

<!-- S61 -->
Each idea has serious scientists
behind it.
None of them has the whole answer.

<!-- S62 -->
But maybe the question itself
is set up the wrong way.

<!-- S63 -->
Where did the first life come from?
That assumes there was a first.
One moment, one thing,
that wasn't alive, and then was.

<!-- S64 -->
So what counts as alive?
One working definition,
developed by NASA scientists,
describes life as
a self-sustaining chemical system
capable of Darwinian evolution.

<!-- S65 -->
Picture a molecule
that makes rough copies of itself.
Some copies come out a little different.

<!-- S66 -->
Some of them copy faster than others.
And if those differences are inherited,
the faster copies become more common.

<!-- S67 -->
Is that alive?
It's not a cell.
It doesn't eat or breathe.
But it's already evolving.

<!-- S68 -->
Now add a membrane around it.
Then a way to grab energy.
Then a better copier.
At which step did it come alive?

<!-- S69 -->
It's a bit like asking
who spoke the first word of French.
Nobody did.
Spoken Latin just slowly drifted,
until one day it was French.

<!-- S70 -->
Life may be the same.
Not a spark.

<!-- S70b -->
A slope.

<!-- S71 -->
Chemistry that got a little more
like life, again and again,
until there was no going back.

<!-- S72 -->
And here's the strangest part.
The fly, the banana, the yeast and you
all use essentially
the same genetic code.

<!-- S73 -->
That points to a common ancestor.
But a common ancestor
isn't necessarily the first life.

<!-- S74 -->
It doesn't tell us
whether life began only once,
or whether other beginnings
simply left no survivors.
The best way to find out
may be to find life
that started somewhere else.

<!-- S75 -->
So next time fruit flies turn up
around a forgotten banana,
they're not life appearing from nothing.

<!-- S76 -->
They're the newest link
in a chain that hasn't broken once
in about
three and a half billion years.

<!-- S77 -->
And at the far end of that chain,
there's no first fly.
Maybe no first anything.
Just chemistry,
slowly getting better at copying itself.

<!-- S78 -->
So here's a question for you.
If a lab one day made something
that copies itself and evolves,
starting from nothing
but simple chemicals,
would you call it alive?
Let me know in the comments.
