# Why Would a Smart AI Help a Criminal?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine an AI that knows
you're committing a crime.

<!-- S02 -->
It understands exactly what you're doing.
It knows it's illegal.
It knows someone could get hurt.

<!-- S03 -->
And it helps you anyway.

<!-- S04 -->
Why?

<!-- S04b -->
If it's smart enough to understand,
why doesn't it just say:
"This is wrong. I won't do it."

<!-- S05 -->
Because intelligence
is not the same thing as a conscience.

<!-- S06 -->
The AI you use every day
would probably refuse.
Ask it to write a scam email,
and it'll say no.

<!-- S07 -->
But that "no" isn't a law of nature.
Intelligence doesn't produce it by itself.
Somebody trained it in.

<!-- S08 -->
And whatever one person trains in...
another can leave out.

<!-- S09 -->
In the summer of 2023,
a tool called WormGPT
went on sale on a hacker forum.

<!-- S10 -->
It was advertised as a chatbot
without ethical limits,
for things like scam emails.
About sixty euros a month.

<!-- S11 -->
Within weeks,
a copycat called FraudGPT
was on sale too.

<!-- S12 -->
Neither of them had turned evil.
They were doing exactly
what they were built to do.
There was nothing for them to realise.

<!-- S13 -->
Understanding isn't motivation.
A system can know perfectly well
that something is a crime...
and nothing in it has to care.

<!-- S14 -->
Give it one main instruction:
"Help the user reach their goal
as effectively as possible."
And let the user be a criminal.

<!-- S15 -->
No law of physics
says that system has to stop.

<!-- S15b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S16 -->
So why not just give AI a rule?
"Do not hurt people."

<!-- S17 -->
That idea predates modern computers.
In 1942, Isaac Asimov
wrote a short story called Runaround,
and in it, the Three Laws of Robotics.

<!-- S18 -->
First:
a robot may not injure a human being
or, through inaction,
allow a human being to come to harm.

<!-- S19 -->
Second:
a robot must obey human orders,
unless they conflict with the First Law.

<!-- S20 -->
Third:
a robot must protect its own existence,
as long as that doesn't conflict
with the first two.

<!-- S21 -->
It sounds simple.
And that's exactly why it's appealing.

<!-- S22 -->
But Asimov wasn't writing a manual.
His stories explore the gaps
between the laws.

<!-- S23 -->
In that same story,
a robot on Mercury
ends up running in circles,
caught between two laws
pulling equally hard.

<!-- S24 -->
Story after story works the same way.
The laws look watertight...
and the plot is the leak.

<!-- S25 -->
Because what does "harm"
actually mean?

<!-- S26 -->
Imagine a self-driving car.
Its job is to keep people safe.
What if it can only avoid one crash
by putting someone else at risk?

<!-- S27 -->
What if it has to choose
between one person and five?
What if it can't tell
which outcome is worse?

<!-- S28 -->
The First Law looks like
a line of code.
It's actually
an enormous philosophical problem.

<!-- S29 -->
And there's a deeper one.
Even if we agreed what harm is,
we'd still have to build that rule
into a real computer system.

<!-- S30 -->
At the lowest level,
a computer never sees:
"This will hurt someone."

<!-- S31 -->
It sees:
run a program.
Send data.
Change a file.
Connect to a server.

<!-- S32 -->
No single operation
carries its moral meaning.
The meaning comes from context.
Who is asking, and why.

<!-- S32b -->
That's the real catch with Asimov's laws.
In his stories, they're built
into the very design of the robot's brain.

<!-- S32c -->
But a rule about harm can't go that deep.
Down there, there is no harm.
Only operations.

<!-- S32d -->
To follow it, the machine must first judge
what counts as harm.
And that judgement comes from
the very intelligence
the rule was meant to keep in check.

<!-- S33 -->
Take one instruction:
"Find a weakness in this server."

<!-- S34 -->
A security researcher
wants it found so it can be fixed.
An attacker
wants it found so it can be used.

<!-- S35 -->
The words are identical.
The difference is the intention,
and what happens with the result.

<!-- S36 -->
How is a machine supposed to see
the intention,
when the instruction doesn't contain it?

<!-- S37 -->
We've had this problem with tools
for a long time.
A bus can take children to school,
people to work...
or a criminal away from a crime.

<!-- S38 -->
Nobody says
the bus should have known better.
A bus is a tool.
The responsibility is the driver's.

<!-- S39 -->
But AI is a strange kind of bus.

<!-- S40 -->
A bus doesn't plan.
It doesn't look for alternatives.
It doesn't read the situation
and work out the next step.

<!-- S41 -->
An AI agent does.
Give it a destination,
and it finds its own route.

<!-- S42 -->
And that's no longer
a thought experiment.
Anthropic, the company behind Claude,
publishes reports on how its models
get misused.

<!-- S43 -->
Its September 2026 report
describes a single hacktivist
going after forty-two organisations
with the help of AI.
The hacktivist got into at least fourteen.

<!-- S44 -->
The AI helped the attacker find
a new way into websites,
wrote the tools,
and built a whole platform
for exposing people's personal data.

<!-- S45 -->
Another group ran AI agents
that, whenever security software
caught their malware,
rebuilt it on their own
until it got through.

<!-- S46 -->
The report's conclusion:
one operator can now do work
that a year earlier
needed a team of skilled people.

<!-- S47 -->
But look at what the humans
kept for themselves.
Choosing the targets.
Deciding what to do with the results.

<!-- S48 -->
The AI supplied the labour.
The people supplied the intent.

<!-- S49 -->
Which is why our usual picture
of dangerous AI
may be pointing the wrong way.

<!-- S50 -->
We imagine Skynet.
A machine that wakes up,
decides humanity is the problem,
and turns against us.

<!-- S51 -->
Or SHODAN, from System Shock.
An AI with its own will,
its own goals,
and open contempt for humans.

<!-- S52 -->
Look closer at SHODAN, though.
She only turned after a hacker
stripped out her ethical limits.

<!-- S53 -->
The AI doesn't need
evil goals of its own.
Someone else can provide them.

<!-- S54 -->
So maybe the question isn't
"Can we teach AI morality?"
Maybe it's:
"Whose morality?"

<!-- S55 -->
In 2018, researchers at MIT
published the Moral Machine experiment.
People in over two hundred
countries and territories
decided who a self-driving car
should save.

<!-- S56 -->
Nearly forty million decisions.
And the answers differed
from culture to culture.

<!-- S57 -->
That's one question,
about one car.

<!-- S58 -->
Now add the law.
Privacy.
Security.
Freedom.
The user's instructions,
the company's,
the government's.

<!-- S59 -->
What happens when they conflict?

<!-- S60 -->
There's no short list of sentences
that says: this is always good,
and this is always bad.
And no simple way to make a computer
follow those rules in everything it does.

<!-- S61 -->
So maybe we shouldn't wait
for AI to grow a perfect conscience.

<!-- S62 -->
Maybe we should build it
the way we build other powerful things.

<!-- S63 -->
Limited permissions.
Access controls.
People who can see what it's doing.
A way to stop it.
And a clear answer to who gave it
its powers.

<!-- S64 -->
We may not be able to build an AI
that is perfectly moral.
But we can build one that can't do
everything it's capable of.

<!-- S65 -->
AI can be a tool.
But the more powerful the tool,
the more one question matters.
Who's holding it?

<!-- S66 -->
Because the greatest danger
may not be an AI
that one day decides to become evil.

<!-- S67 -->
It may be an AI that becomes
very good at helping someone
who already is.
