# Why Is Computer Stuff Called That?

<!-- S01 -->
This is a log.

<!-- S02 -->
And so is this.

<!-- S03 -->
Somehow, a piece of wood became
something your computer writes
all day long.

<!-- S04 -->
And the story of how it got there
involves a ship, a rope,
and a sailor counting knots.

<!-- S05 -->
Computers are full of words like this.
Mouse. Desktop. Cloud. Bug.
Words that meant something else
long before there were computers.

<!-- S06 -->
Some of them are obvious.
Some were chosen very carefully.
And some have travelled so far
that hardly anyone remembers
where they started.

<!-- S07 -->
Let's start with the easy ones.

<!-- S08 -->
The mouse.
It's small, it sits on your desk,
and the early ones had a cord
coming out of the back.

<!-- S09 -->
In the 1960s, the team that built it
thought the cord looked like a tail.
Nobody remembers who said it first.
But the name stuck.

<!-- S10 -->
It wasn't a plan.
It just looked like a mouse.

<!-- S11 -->
The desktop is a different story.
Your computer has files,
inside folders, on a desktop.
And when you're done with them,
they go in the bin.

<!-- S12 -->
None of that is an accident.
In the 1970s, researchers at Xerox
designed screens that deliberately
looked like an office.
Apple later added the bin.

<!-- S13 -->
Instead of teaching people
a whole new vocabulary,
they gave them one they already knew.
Desk. Folder. Document. Bin.

<!-- S14 -->
There's no desk inside your computer.
And there's definitely no tiny bin.
But you know exactly what they mean.
That was the whole point.

<!-- S14b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S15 -->
And that idea, using something you can see
to explain something you can't,
is behind most of these words.

<!-- S16 -->
Take the cloud.

<!-- S17 -->
Long before anyone kept photos in it,
engineers drawing network diagrams
had a problem.
Somewhere in the middle
was a huge, complicated network
they didn't need to show.

<!-- S18 -->
So they drew a cloud.
A fuzzy shape that meant:
there's a lot going on in here,
and you don't need to know what.

<!-- S19 -->
That's still exactly what it means.
Your files in the cloud
aren't in the sky.
They're on somebody else's computers,
probably in a data centre,
possibly on another continent.

<!-- S20 -->
The cloud is simply the part
you don't need to see.

<!-- S21 -->
The web is a picture too.
When Tim Berners-Lee named
the World Wide Web in 1990,
he was describing a shape.
Documents linked to other documents,
with no centre and no edge.

<!-- S22 -->
Like a spider's web,
every thread leads to another thread.
And, just like a fly,
you can easily get stuck in it
for three hours.

<!-- S23 -->
Then there's the packet.

<!-- S24 -->
In the mid-1960s, a British scientist,
Donald Davies, needed a name
for small pieces of data
travelling across a network.

<!-- S25 -->
He chose packet, a small parcel,
partly because the word would translate
easily into other languages.

<!-- S26 -->
And it fits.
When you watch a video online,
you're not receiving it in one piece.
You're getting hundreds of thousands
of tiny parcels,
each with an address on the front.

<!-- S27 -->
Which brings us to another word
from the world of deliveries.
Load.

<!-- S28 -->
A truck carries a load.
But not everything a truck hauls around
is cargo someone has paid to send.
There's fuel, there's the driver,
there's the truck itself.

<!-- S29 -->
The part that actually earns money
is the pay load.
The payload.

<!-- S30 -->
Rockets kept the word.
A rocket is mostly fuel,
and the payload is the small part on top
that the whole launch is for.

<!-- S31 -->
A network packet is no different.
Every packet has a label,
with an address and delivery instructions.
And a payload.
The bit you actually wanted.

<!-- S32 -->
Now let's move from the road to the water.

<!-- S33 -->
A stream flows.
You don't have to wait
for the whole river to arrive
before you can drink from it.

<!-- S34 -->
That's streaming.
The film starts playing
while the rest of it is still on the way.

<!-- S35 -->
But water doesn't always arrive evenly,
and neither does data.
So we need a buffer.

<!-- S36 -->
A buffer is the thing
on the end of a railway carriage
that takes the jolt
when it bumps into the next one.

<!-- S37 -->
A data buffer does the same job.
It absorbs the bumps,
so the video doesn't stop
every time the connection stumbles.

<!-- S38 -->
And when data has to pass
through several stages, one after another,
programmers call it a pipeline.

<!-- S39 -->
That one was plumbing from the start.
In 1964, Doug McIlroy at Bell Labs
wrote that programs should connect
like lengths of garden hose.

<!-- S40 -->
Data flows, gets buffered,
and runs through pipes.
Somewhere along the way,
computing quietly turned into plumbing.

<!-- S41 -->
And then there's padding.

<!-- S42 -->
Padding is what you put around something
to protect it.
A padded jacket. A padded envelope.
A padded chair.

<!-- S43 -->
On a web page, padding is the space
between the edge of a box
and the words inside it.
Almost every button you've ever clicked
has a little cushion around the text.

<!-- S44 -->
And outside the padding is the margin,
a word borrowed straight
from the edge of a page.

<!-- S45 -->
So far, all of these words
have something in common.
You can see the picture behind them.
Once someone points it out,
it's obvious.

<!-- S46 -->
But some words have travelled so far
that the original picture
has completely faded.

<!-- S47 -->
Remember the log?

<!-- S48 -->
Five hundred years ago,
a ship had no speedometer.
Just the sea going past.

<!-- S49 -->
So sailors threw a piece of wood
overboard, tied to a long rope.
The wood stayed put in the water
while the ship sailed away from it.

<!-- S50 -->
Later, knots were tied along the rope
at regular intervals.
A sailor counted how many knots
slipped overboard
while a small sand glass emptied.

<!-- S51 -->
That number was the ship's speed.
And that's why we still measure
the speed of ships in knots.

<!-- S52 -->
The piece of wood was called the log.
Every reading was written down,
first on a board, then in a book.
The log book.

<!-- S53 -->
Over time, the log book grew
into a full diary of the voyage.
Speed, weather, position,
anything that happened on board.

<!-- S54 -->
And when computers needed a place
to write down everything that happened,
the name was already waiting.

<!-- S55 -->
Every line in a server log
is a distant descendant
of a sailor counting knots on a rope.
Just with less wood.

<!-- S56 -->
Booting is even stranger.

<!-- S57 -->
In the nineteenth century,
there was a joke about pulling yourself up
by your own bootstraps.
The little loops at the top of a boot.

<!-- S58 -->
It was meant to sound absurd.
You can't lift yourself off the ground
by pulling on your own boots.
It was a way of describing
something impossible.

<!-- S59 -->
But a computer has to do
something very similar.
When you switch it on,
it needs a program to load its programs.
And loading programs is a program's job.

<!-- S60 -->
So it starts with a tiny piece of code
that loads a slightly bigger one,
which loads a bigger one still,
until the whole system is running.

<!-- S61 -->
The computer pulls itself up
by its own bootstraps.
Engineers called it bootstrapping,
and then just booting.

<!-- S62 -->
A joke about the impossible
became the first thing
every computer does
when you switch it on.

<!-- S63 -->
And then there's the bug.

<!-- S64 -->
You've probably heard the story.
In 1947, the team working on
the Harvard Mark II computer
found a moth stuck in a relay.

<!-- S65 -->
They taped it into the computer's notes,
with a comment:
"First actual case of bug being found."

<!-- S66 -->
Into the log book, of course.

<!-- S67 -->
But look at that comment again.
First actual case.
It's a joke.
And it only works if everyone already knew
what a bug was.

<!-- S68 -->
And they did.
In 1878, Thomas Edison was writing
about the bugs in his inventions,
the little faults and difficulties
that show up when you build something new.

<!-- S69 -->
So the moth didn't give us the word.
It was just the first bug
you could actually hold.

<!-- S70 -->
Now here's a word that sounds
much more sinister than it is.
Daemon.

<!-- S71 -->
On many computers, little programs
run quietly in the background,
handling the mail, the printer, the clock.
Programmers call them daemons.

<!-- S72 -->
Not because they're evil.
The name came from MIT in the 1960s,
and it was borrowed from physics.

<!-- S73 -->
In 1867, the physicist
James Clerk Maxwell imagined
a tiny being, later called
Maxwell's demon,
guarding a little door
in a wall that split a box of gas in two.

<!-- S74 -->
It let the fast molecules through one way,
and the slow ones the other,
quietly sorting them, never resting.

<!-- S75 -->
A tireless little helper,
working in the background.
That's exactly what the programmers at MIT
wanted their programs to be.

<!-- S76 -->
And in ancient Greek,
a daimon wasn't a devil at all.
It was a spirit that watched over you.

<!-- S77 -->
And finally, the worm.

<!-- S78 -->
A computer worm is a program
that copies itself
from one computer to the next,
all on its own.

<!-- S79 -->
The name came from a novel.
The Shockwave Rider, from 1975,
in which a programmer turns a tapeworm
loose in a giant network.

<!-- S80 -->
A few years later, researchers at Xerox
wrote real programs that spread
between the machines on their network.
And they borrowed the name from the book.

<!-- S81 -->
The same lab, as it happens,
that gave us the desktop.

<!-- S82 -->
A log that was a piece of wood.
Booting, which began as a joke.
A bug that was older than computers.
A daemon that was a physicist's helper.
A worm from a science fiction novel.

<!-- S83 -->
And that's really the answer
to the question in the title.

<!-- S84 -->
Almost everything a computer does
is invisible.
You can't see a packet,
or a buffer, or a cloud.

<!-- S85 -->
So we name the invisible world
after the visible one.
A desk. A river. A truck. A parcel.
A rope with knots in it.

<!-- S86 -->
And after a while,
we forget they were ever metaphors.

<!-- S87 -->
We didn't invent a language for computers.
We borrowed one from the world
we already understood.

<!-- S88 -->
This turned out to be so much fun
that it would be a shame to stop here.
So in the next video,
we'll look at a few more of these words.
