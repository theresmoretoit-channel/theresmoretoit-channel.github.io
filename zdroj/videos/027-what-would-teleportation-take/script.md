<!-- S01 -->
Imagine that tomorrow,
we invent teleportation.
You step into a machine.

<!-- S02 -->
A few seconds later,
you step out on the other side
of the planet.
No airplane.
No rocket.
No road.
Just... there.

<!-- S03 -->
Sounds amazing.
But what exactly happened to you?

<!-- S04 -->
Did you travel?
Were you copied?
Were you destroyed and rebuilt?

<!-- S05 -->
Or did we somehow bend space itself
and simply make the distance
disappear?

<!-- S06 -->
Because "teleportation"
is not actually one technology.
There are several completely
different ways it could work.
And some of them are much more
disturbing than others.

<!-- S07 -->
Let's start with one
of the simplest ideas.
The Portal approach.

<!-- S08 -->
You don't disassemble anything.
You don't scan anything.
You simply connect two locations.

<!-- S09 -->
Imagine that space
normally looks like this.
And somehow, we create a shortcut.
You walk through it.

<!-- S10 -->
From your perspective, you are still
the same physical object.
You were not copied.
You were not reconstructed.

<!-- S11 -->
In fact, this is arguably
not teleportation at all.
It's more like removing the distance
between two places.

<!-- S12 -->
In physics, the closest familiar idea
is a wormhole.
A hypothetical shortcut
through spacetime.

<!-- S13 -->
But there is a small problem.
We don't know how to create
a traversable wormhole.
Or whether one could be kept open.

<!-- S14 -->
General relativity allows
some strange geometries of spacetime.
But turning one of them
into a practical doorway
is an entirely different problem.

<!-- S15 -->
And then there are some wonderfully
inconvenient questions.
What happens if the portal closes
while you're halfway through?

<!-- S16 -->
And perhaps most importantly.
How much energy would it take
to manipulate spacetime
on this scale?

<!-- S17 -->
So the Portal solution
has a major advantage.
You don't have to
reconstruct the person.

<!-- S18 -->
But instead, you have to do something
even more ridiculous.
You have to manipulate
spacetime itself.

<!-- S19 -->
Now let's try
something completely different.
The classic transporter.

<!-- S20 -->
You enter the machine.
The machine scans you.
Your body is disassembled.
The information is transmitted.
And another machine reconstructs you.

<!-- S21 -->
Scan.
Data.
Transmit.
Rebuild.

<!-- S22 -->
This sounds like something
a sufficiently advanced computer
could do.
But immediately we run into
the first gigantic problem.

<!-- S23 -->
What exactly do we scan?
A human body contains
roughly seven octillion atoms.
That's a seven followed
by about twenty-seven zeros.

<!-- S24 -->
And knowing where the atoms are
may not be enough.
We also need to know
what those atoms are doing.

<!-- S25 -->
What molecules are present?
What are the electrical states
of the neurons?
And how much of the quantum
state matters?

<!-- S26 -->
The more accurately we want
to reconstruct the original person,
the more information we need.
And this leads to
a rather unpleasant question.
How do we make the scan?

<!-- S27 -->
A human being is not a statue.
Especially the brain.
The brain is constantly changing.

<!-- S28 -->
Neurons are firing.
Ions are moving.
Membranes are changing
their electrical potentials.

<!-- S29 -->
So imagine trying to take
a perfect snapshot of the brain.
Not just its shape.
Its physical state.
At one exact moment.

<!-- S30 -->
So perhaps we should
stop everything first.
Freeze the chemistry.
Stop the brain.
Take the measurement.
And then reconstruct the person.

<!-- S31 -->
Sounds reasonable.
Except...

<!-- S32 -->
How do you safely stop a human brain
without destroying the very
information you are trying
to preserve?

<!-- S33 -->
We have now built
a teleportation machine
whose first step is this.
"Please enter the machine
so we can temporarily kill
all biological activity
in your brain."

<!-- S34 -->
Not exactly great marketing.

<!-- S35 -->
And even if we solve that,
there is another problem.
How much data is a human being?

<!-- S36 -->
It's tempting to say this.
"Just count the atoms
and store their coordinates."
But that's not enough.

<!-- S37 -->
The amount of information
depends on the precision.
Describe every position
to millimetre accuracy,
and that's one amount of data.

<!-- S38 -->
Micrometre accuracy?
Much more.
Nanometre accuracy?
Much more again.

<!-- S39 -->
And if you imagine describing matter
down to extremely tiny scales,
the numbers become absurd.

<!-- S40 -->
You might hear people go
all the way down to the Planck length.
But that is not a pixel size
of the universe.
Divide a body into cubes that size
and you get a number
with about a hundred and three zeros.

<!-- S41 -->
And a smaller pixel does not tell us
what information belongs in it.
We aren't taking
a three-dimensional photograph.
We're describing a physical system.

<!-- S42 -->
Quantum mechanics makes
the situation even stranger.
We cannot imagine that every particle
has a definite list of properties
that a scanner can read out.

<!-- S43 -->
Measurement itself
is part of quantum mechanics.
And there is a fundamental restriction
known as the no-cloning theorem.
An unknown quantum state
cannot simply be copied perfectly.

<!-- S44 -->
Quantum teleportation
does exist experimentally.
But it doesn't teleport matter.
It transfers a quantum state
using entanglement
and classical communication.

<!-- S45 -->
And it does not leave you
with two perfect copies.
So real quantum teleportation
is very different
from a Star Trek transporter.

<!-- S46 -->
Let's be generous.
Let's assume we have solved
the scanning problem.
We now possess
the complete blueprint.

<!-- S47 -->
And we send it to another planet.
Congratulations.
We've solved teleportation.
Right?

<!-- S48 -->
No.
We now have to build you.

<!-- S49 -->
The receiving machine needs matter.
A lot of it.
It needs the right elements.

<!-- S50 -->
Carbon.
Hydrogen.
Oxygen.
Nitrogen.
And many others.

<!-- S51 -->
But having the right atoms
isn't enough.
We need the right molecules.
The right proteins.
The right organs.
And eventually...
the right brain.

<!-- S52 -->
It is not enough to manufacture
seventy kilograms
of human-shaped matter.
We need to put the right molecules
in the right places.

<!-- S53 -->
Notice what the machine cannot send.
It sends information.
It does not send atoms.
So the receiving end needs
its own warehouse of matter,
sorted by element.

<!-- S54 -->
And then it has to place them.
Suppose the machine puts down
a billion atoms every second.
Seven octillion atoms would take
longer than the universe
has existed.

<!-- S55 -->
Make it a trillion atoms a second,
and you are still assembling
for two hundred million years.

<!-- S56 -->
And they cannot go down in any order.
Most proteins only work
once they are folded.
A cell only works
once it is running.
Much of you is not a structure.
It is a process that has to be
switched on while it is being built.

<!-- S57 -->
Imagine the receiving machine saying.
"Okay, I have all the ingredients.
Now I just need to assemble
seven octillion atoms
into one functioning human."

<!-- S58 -->
At this point,
the teleportation machine
has started looking suspiciously like
the most advanced factory
in the universe.
It is essentially
a molecular three-dimensional printer.

<!-- S59 -->
The brain is where it gets
really interesting.
Suppose we successfully
rebuild every neuron.
But one synapse is wrong.
Would you notice?

<!-- S60 -->
What if a million synapses
are slightly different?
What if the structure is correct
but some electrical states
are different?

<!-- S61 -->
What if the memories are preserved
but the exact state
of the brain is not?
At what point does
the reconstructed person
stop being you?

<!-- S62 -->
And this brings us to
the strangest problem of all.

<!-- S63 -->
Imagine the transporter scans you.
It creates a perfect
reconstruction on Mars.
But the original is still
standing here on Earth.

<!-- S64 -->
Mars-you opens your eyes.
You remember everything.
You remember walking
into the transporter.
You remember your family.

<!-- S65 -->
You remember pressing the button.
And you say.
"Okay. I'm on Mars."

<!-- S66 -->
But Earth-you
is still standing in the machine.
And you say.
"No. I'm on Earth."

<!-- S67 -->
Which one is you?

<!-- S68 -->
There is no obvious
physical experiment
that makes this question go away.
Because both people have exactly
the same history
up to the moment of reconstruction.

<!-- S69 -->
Now let's make it even worse.
Suppose the transporter works
by destroying the original.

<!-- S70 -->
The receiving machine
builds an identical person.
From the outside,
everything looks perfect.

<!-- S71 -->
The new person looks like you.
Talks like you.
Remembers your childhood.
Knows your passwords.
And says.
"I've been teleported."

<!-- S72 -->
But from the perspective
of the original consciousness...
there may have been
no journey at all.

<!-- S73 -->
You died.
And somebody else woke up
believing they were you.

<!-- S74 -->
The transporter may therefore
be interpreted as this.
Scan.
Kill.
Copy.
Rather than.
Scan.
Travel.

<!-- S75 -->
And that is not
a technical question anymore.
It is a question about
what personal identity actually means.

<!-- S76 -->
Suppose the transporter malfunctions.
Instead of one person on Mars,
it creates two.

<!-- S77 -->
Both copies are perfect.
Both remember the same childhood.
Both sincerely believe
they are the original.

<!-- S78 -->
Which one is the real you?

<!-- S79 -->
And if one of them is destroyed...
did we kill you?
Or did we kill your identical twin?

<!-- S80 -->
There may be no objective answer
unless we first decide
what "being the same person"
actually means.

<!-- S81 -->
There is yet another possibility.
Don't teleport the body.
Teleport the mind.
Scan the brain.
Transmit the information.
Run it somewhere else.

<!-- S82 -->
Perhaps in another biological brain.
Perhaps in a synthetic brain.
Perhaps in a robot.

<!-- S83 -->
Now the question becomes this.
If your mind runs in a machine...
are you still you?

<!-- S84 -->
And if we can run
two copies simultaneously...
which one is conscious?
Maybe both.
Maybe neither.

<!-- S85 -->
Maybe consciousness is not something
that can simply be copied.
We don't currently know.

<!-- S86 -->
Science fiction has another trick.
Perhaps we temporarily change
how you interact with matter.
You become "out of phase."
You walk through a wall.

<!-- S87 -->
Until you ask.
If you don't interact with the wall,
why would you interact with the air?
And why don't you fall
through the floor?

<!-- S88 -->
If you can selectively interact
with some particles but not others...
we've just invented
a completely new kind of physics.
So again, the idea is simple.
The mechanism is not.

<!-- S89 -->
We have now imagined
several very different machines.

<!-- S90 -->
The easiest teleportation to explain
is not necessarily
the easiest teleportation to build.
And the easiest one philosophically
may be the hardest one physically.

<!-- S91 -->
A Portal style teleportation
preserves your physical continuity...
but requires control over spacetime.

<!-- S92 -->
A Star Trek style transporter
may be easier to imagine
technologically...
but creates the horrifying question
of whether the person who arrives
is actually you.

<!-- S93 -->
So imagine that tomorrow
somebody offers you
a teleportation device.
It has been tested a million times.
Every test is successful.

<!-- S94 -->
Every person who comes out
is indistinguishable
from the person who went in.
The machine is perfectly safe.

<!-- S95 -->
Except for one thing.

<!-- S96 -->
Nobody can prove
that the consciousness
on the other side
is the same consciousness
that entered.

<!-- S97 -->
Would you use it?

<!-- S98 -->
Because perhaps the biggest problem
with teleportation isn't this.
"How do we move a human
from one place to another?"

<!-- S99 -->
Perhaps it's this.
"How do we know that the person
who arrives is the person who left?"

<!-- S100 -->
And maybe that is the one question
no teleportation machine
can ever answer.
