<!-- S01 -->
By May 2025,
the chief prosecutor
of the International Criminal Court
had lost access to his email.

<!-- S02 -->
Not because of a hack.
Not because of a power cut.
Because of a signature in Washington.

<!-- S03 -->
That February, the United States
had put him under sanctions.
And his email ran on Microsoft,
an American company.

<!-- S04 -->
Microsoft says it never stopped
serving the court itself.
But it was in contact with the court
throughout the process
that led to his disconnection.

<!-- S05 -->
The exact chain of decisions is disputed.
But the dependency is not.
A sanction signed in Washington
reached an inbox in The Hague.

<!-- S06 -->
Now scale that up.
Your tax office, your hospitals, your police
all depend on software and services
from a handful of companies abroad.

<!-- S07 -->
Could your country still function
if that software doubled in price overnight?
Or if its maker
was no longer allowed to sell it to you?

<!-- S08 -->
Governments know all this.
And they keep signing the contracts anyway.
So why?

<!-- S09 -->
Take the Czech Republic.
In September 2024,
its Interior Ministry signed
a framework agreement for Microsoft products.

<!-- S10 -->
The ceiling?
Just over fourteen billion Czech crowns,
before VAT.
That's well over half a billion euros.

<!-- S11 -->
It runs for four years,
covering Microsoft licences and support
for ministries and other state institutions.

<!-- S12 -->
It's a ceiling, not a bill.
The money is spent order by order,
in smaller tenders between approved suppliers.

<!-- S13 -->
Pooling demand gives the government
more bargaining power.
But it also makes the same ecosystem
the default across many institutions.

<!-- S14 -->
And the Czechs are not unusual.
A study for the European Parliament
found in December 2025
that American firms dominate
almost every layer of Europe's digital stack.

<!-- S15 -->
So how did it get like this?
Mostly by making sensible decisions,
one at a time.

<!-- S16 -->
A modern government
is a machine for processing information.
Taxes, identity cards, court rulings,
land records, emergency calls.

<!-- S17 -->
And inside its offices,
the software looks very familiar.
Windows. Office. Outlook.
Active Directory. Teams.

<!-- S18 -->
Nothing exotic.
The same tools millions of companies use.
And that's exactly why governments chose them.

<!-- S19 -->
Staff already know them.
Administrators know how to run them.
Documents and applications expect them.

<!-- S20 -->
When you need twenty thousand computers,
picking what everyone knows
isn't a scandal.
It's the obvious decision.

<!-- S21 -->
But a company can change its strategy.
A state can't simply stop working
while it thinks about it.

<!-- S22 -->
So why not just switch?
Picture a ministry
with twenty thousand staff.

<!-- S23 -->
Accounts in Active Directory.
Email on Exchange.
Workflows in SharePoint.
Excel macros nobody remembers writing.

<!-- S24 -->
Replacing that isn't a matter
of installing Linux and LibreOffice.
You migrate documents, rewrite applications,
rebuild logins, retrain people,
and test everything.

<!-- S25 -->
And you do it
without the tax office closing
for a single day.

<!-- S26 -->
That's called vendor lock-in.
Leaving a supplier becomes so hard,
or so expensive,
that it stops being a real option.

<!-- S27 -->
And here's the strange part.
Nobody has to force you to stay.
There doesn't even need to be
a clause in the contract.

<!-- S28 -->
Every year, more applications
are built on the system you already have.
More staff learn it.
More data piles up in its formats.

<!-- S29 -->
The technology itself
becomes the contract.
And every year, the way out
gets a little longer.

<!-- S30 -->
Now suppose the supplier raises its prices.
Or moves a feature you rely on
into a more expensive subscription.

<!-- S31 -->
You can negotiate.
You can threaten to leave.
But if leaving takes five years
and costs billions,
everyone at the table knows it.

<!-- S32 -->
The British government
has official guidance on exactly this.
It warns that switching providers
can become difficult, time-consuming
and disproportionately expensive.

<!-- S33 -->
And then it says something surprising.
In the cloud, the advantages
of being locked in
can often outweigh the disadvantages.

<!-- S34 -->
So the question isn't
how to avoid every dependency.
That's impossible.
It's whether you know which ones you have,
and whether there's a real way out.

<!-- S35 -->
And price is the smaller problem.
The bigger one is what happened
in The Hague.

<!-- S36 -->
A software company doesn't float
above the law.
It answers to the country it belongs to,
including its sanctions and export rules.

<!-- S37 -->
Most of the time, that never matters.
Until relations between two countries
go bad.

<!-- S38 -->
No, Microsoft can't switch off
the Czech Republic on a whim.
There are contracts, courts,
and a lot of money in the way.

<!-- S39 -->
But the court in The Hague
shows the real shape of the risk.
Not a whole country going dark.
One person, targeted by another government.

<!-- S40 -->
Think of the difference
between buying a computer
and renting a service.

<!-- S41 -->
A computer you bought keeps working
even if its maker disappears.
A service you rent exists
only as long as someone keeps providing it.

<!-- S42 -->
Which brings us to the data.
A government might keep its files
in a data centre inside its own borders.
That sounds reassuring.

<!-- S43 -->
But where a server stands
doesn't decide which laws apply
to the company running it.

<!-- S44 -->
America's CLOUD Act of 2018
lets US authorities, with legal process,
require American providers to hand over data
in their possession, custody or control,
even when it's stored abroad.

<!-- S45 -->
In June 2025,
the French Senate questioned
Microsoft France's director
of public and legal affairs,
who was testifying under oath.

<!-- S46 -->
Could he guarantee
that French citizens' data,
entrusted to Microsoft by the state,
would never be handed to US authorities
without France's explicit consent?

<!-- S47 -->
No, he said, he couldn't.
But he added that it had never happened.
No such request, he said,
had ever affected a European company
or public body.

<!-- S48 -->
Both answers can be true at once.
Where your data is stored
and who has legal control over it
are two different things.

<!-- S49 -->
At this point,
someone is shouting at the screen:
just install Linux!

<!-- S50 -->
Munich tried.
Starting in 2004, the city moved
around fifteen thousand office computers
to its own Linux, called LiMux.

<!-- S51 -->
In 2017, three years after
a new mayor took office,
the city council voted
to go back to Windows.

<!-- S52 -->
Why Munich reversed course
is still debated.
But the desktop migration was only one part.
The harder challenge was everything around it:
old applications, support, users
and political commitment.

<!-- S53 -->
Others are trying now.
The German state of Schleswig-Holstein
has moved nearly eighty per cent
of its workstations outside the tax offices
to LibreOffice.

<!-- S53b -->
It has moved almost forty-four thousand
mailboxes to open-source email.
And it says it already saves
more than fifteen million euros
in licence fees.

<!-- S53c -->
In its State Chancellery,
more than two hundred people
now work on Linux.
About twenty desks are still waiting,
tied to specialised applications.

<!-- S54 -->
And the International Criminal Court,
the one in The Hague,
confirmed in October 2025
that it would move to openDesk,
an open-source office suite
developed for the German government.

<!-- S55 -->
Open source has real advantages.
Anyone can inspect the code.
You can change it.
And more than one company can maintain it.

<!-- S56 -->
But open source isn't free infrastructure.
Someone still has to patch it,
support it,
and fix it at three in the morning.

<!-- S57 -->
So the real goal
isn't to get rid of Microsoft.
It's to make sure Microsoft
isn't the only realistic option.

<!-- S58 -->
That's what people mean
by digital sovereignty.
It sounds like a slogan,
but the idea is practical.

<!-- S59 -->
No country can make every chip
and write every program itself.
But it can know where
its critical dependencies are,
keep control of its data,
and insist on open standards.

<!-- S60 -->
The aim isn't to become
technologically isolated.
It's to avoid becoming
technologically helpless.

<!-- S61 -->
Here's a simple test.
If your biggest software supplier
stopped serving you tomorrow,
could you still reach your own data?

<!-- S62 -->
Could you move your applications?
Could someone else take over?
And how long would it take?
A week. A month. Five years.

<!-- S63 -->
If nobody knows,
that's the answer.
An exit plan that exists only on paper
isn't an exit plan.

<!-- S64 -->
It has to be technically possible,
affordable,
and tested before the crisis,
not during it.

<!-- S65 -->
Buying software from a company
isn't the mistake.
Companies often build it far better
than governments ever could.

<!-- S66 -->
The mistake is becoming unable
to function without them.

<!-- S67 -->
In the end, that's what
digital sovereignty means.
Being able to choose
your technology suppliers.
And being able to choose again.

<!-- S68 -->
A government can rent its software.
It shouldn't rent
its ability to function.

<!-- S69 -->
So here's a question for you.
If your country's biggest software supplier
walked away tomorrow,
what would break first?
Let me know in the comments.
