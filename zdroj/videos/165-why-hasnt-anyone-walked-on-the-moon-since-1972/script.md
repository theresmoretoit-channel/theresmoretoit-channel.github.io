<!-- S01 -->
In April 2026,
four astronauts flew to the Moon.

<!-- S02 -->
They swung around its far side,
further from Earth
than any human had ever been.
They looked down at the craters
from about six and a half
thousand kilometres away.

<!-- S03 -->
And then they turned around
and came home.

<!-- S04 -->
Nobody has stood on the Moon
since December 1972.
That's more than fifty years.

<!-- S05 -->
We did it six times
with computers weaker
than a modern graphing calculator.
So why can't we just do it again?

<!-- S06 -->
The usual answer is money.
And it's not wrong.

<!-- S07 -->
At its peak in 1966,
NASA was getting more than four per cent
of everything the US government spent.

<!-- S08 -->
Today, it gets less than half a per cent.

<!-- S09 -->
In today's money, Apollo cost
well over a quarter of a trillion dollars.
Nobody has been willing to pay that again.

<!-- S10 -->
But that only moves the question.
Why was anyone willing to pay it
the first time?

<!-- S11 -->
In April 1961,
Yuri Gagarin became the first person
to orbit the Earth.
He was Soviet.

<!-- S12 -->
Six weeks later,
President Kennedy stood before Congress
and set a goal:
before the decade was out,
land a man on the Moon
and return him safely to the Earth.

<!-- S13 -->
It wasn't primarily about science.
In a meeting that was recorded,
Kennedy told the head of NASA:
"I'm not that interested in space."

<!-- S14 -->
What he was interested in
was beating the Soviet Union.

<!-- S15 -->
And the public wasn't that keen either.
For most of the 1960s,
polls found that fewer than half
of Americans thought the Moon
was worth the money.

<!-- S16 -->
The Soviets, meanwhile,
were building their own Moon rocket.
It was called the N1,
almost as tall as the Saturn V,
with thirty engines in its first stage.

<!-- S17 -->
It flew four times.
It failed four times.

<!-- S18 -->
The second attempt came
less than two weeks before Apollo 11.
Seconds after lift-off,
the rocket fell back onto its launch pad
and exploded.

<!-- S19 -->
After the fourth failure,
the programme was quietly shut down.
For years, the Soviet Union claimed
it had never been racing to the Moon
at all.

<!-- S20 -->
So once Apollo 11 had landed,
the race was over.

<!-- S21 -->
And without a race,
the reason to keep going was gone.

<!-- S22 -->
Three more missions had been planned:
Apollo 18, 19 and 20.
All three were cancelled,
even though much of the hardware
was already built.

<!-- S23 -->
Leftover Saturn V stages
now lie on their sides
in museums.

<!-- S24 -->
So that's the answer, right?
The race ended, the money went away.

<!-- S25 -->
Not quite.
Because the money and the reason
are back.

<!-- S26 -->
In 2017, the United States
announced it was going back.
Two years later, it got a name, Artemis,
and a deadline: a landing in 2024.

<!-- S27 -->
Then 2025.
Then 2026.
Then 2027.
Now it's 2028.

<!-- S28 -->
And it isn't cheap.
NASA's own inspector general
worked out that a single Artemis launch,
the rocket, the capsule
and the ground systems,
costs more than four billion dollars.

<!-- S29 -->
Its main engines
came from the Space Shuttle.
After every launch,
the entire stage is thrown away.

<!-- S30 -->
By 2025, the whole programme
was expected to have cost
more than ninety billion dollars.

<!-- S31 -->
That sounds like a lot.
But in today's money,
it's about a third
of what Apollo cost,
over roughly the same number of years.
So every year, there's far less of it.

<!-- S32 -->
And it isn't the first attempt.
In 1989 and again in 2004,
American presidents announced
a return to the Moon.

<!-- S33 -->
The first plan died in Congress.
The second was cancelled in 2010,
after billions had been spent.

<!-- S34 -->
A Moon programme takes longer
than any president stays in office.
Every new one can cancel it.

<!-- S35 -->
But even with steady money,
it wouldn't be quick.
There are three more reasons,
and they're less obvious.

<!-- S36 -->
The first is risk.

<!-- S37 -->
In January 1967,
a fire broke out inside an Apollo capsule
during a test on the launch pad.
All three astronauts inside were killed.

<!-- S38 -->
Less than two years later,
Apollo 8 sent three men around the Moon.

<!-- S39 -->
Neil Armstrong later said
he thought they had
a ninety per cent chance
of getting home from Apollo 11,
but only a fifty-fifty chance
of landing on the first attempt.

<!-- S40 -->
The White House had a speech
ready to read,
in case he and Buzz Aldrin
were stranded on the Moon.

<!-- S41 -->
Apollo 13 nearly ended in disaster.
An oxygen tank exploded
on the way to the Moon,
and the crew only just made it back.

<!-- S42 -->
Today, nobody would accept those odds.

<!-- S43 -->
When the heat shield on an uncrewed
Artemis test came back
with chunks missing,
NASA spent nearly two years
working out why
before it would put a crew inside.

<!-- S44 -->
That's the right call.
But it's a kind of caution
Apollo could never afford.

<!-- S45 -->
The second reason is
that we took apart the ladder.

<!-- S46 -->
You may have heard that NASA
lost the plans for the Saturn V.
That's a myth.
The drawings still exist.

<!-- S47 -->
But a drawing isn't a rocket.

<!-- S48 -->
At its peak, Apollo employed
around four hundred thousand people,
at some twenty thousand
companies and universities.
Much of what they knew
was never written down.

<!-- S49 -->
When those factories closed
and those people retired,
the knowledge went with them.

<!-- S50 -->
In 2012, NASA engineers
took an old Moon rocket engine apart
and scanned every part into a computer.

<!-- S50b -->
Every engine had been built by hand,
with quirks no drawing recorded.

<!-- S51 -->
The tools, the suppliers,
the production lines
that built Apollo were gone.
They had to be built again.

<!-- S52 -->
The third reason is
that we're not trying to do
the same thing.

<!-- S53 -->
Apollo landed far from the poles,
on the side facing Earth,
for up to three days at a time.

<!-- S54 -->
It planted flags,
collected rocks,
and left.

<!-- S55 -->
Artemis is aiming for the south pole.
The Sun barely rises there,
and the shadows are long and deep.

<!-- S56 -->
But inside some of those craters
there's ice,
which could one day mean
water, air and fuel.

<!-- S57 -->
The goal isn't to visit.
It's to stay.

<!-- S58 -->
And that changes the machines.
One of the landers NASA has picked
is a version of SpaceX's Starship,
around fifty metres tall.

<!-- S59 -->
To reach the Moon,
it has to be refuelled in orbit
by a whole series of tanker launches.

<!-- S60 -->
Nobody has ever moved
that much super-cold fuel in space.
Which is why the next Artemis mission
won't go to the Moon at all.
It will practise in orbit around Earth.

<!-- S61 -->
So put it all together.
A Moon landing needs a huge budget,
for a decade or more,
a willingness to lose people,
and an army of specialists
built from scratch.

<!-- S62 -->
Which means the real mystery
isn't why nobody has gone back since.
It's how we managed to go
in the first place.

<!-- S63 -->
Between 1969 and 1972,
NASA launched a crew to the Moon
roughly every six months.
The first two Artemis flights,
only one of them crewed,
were almost three and a half years apart.

<!-- S64 -->
In today's money, Apollo cost
several times more
than the Manhattan Project,
which built the atomic bomb
in the middle of a world war.

<!-- S65 -->
That kind of effort
usually only happens in wartime.
And in a way, it was wartime.
Just a cold one.

<!-- S66 -->
For a few years,
one country had a rival it feared,
a deadline it had promised to meet,
and the money to back it up.

<!-- S67 -->
Take any one of those away,
and nobody would have walked on the Moon in 1969.

<!-- S68 -->
And here's the twist.
One of those ingredients
might be coming back.

<!-- S69 -->
China is planning to land
its own astronauts on the Moon
before 2030.
It has already started testing
the lander and the rocket.

<!-- S70 -->
Which means that for the first time
since the 1960s,
there's a race again.

<!-- S71 -->
And this time,
the question isn't just
whether anyone can get there.
It's whether they'll keep going
once the race is over.

<!-- S72 -->
The last person to stand on the Moon,
Gene Cernan,
said in 1999
that the disappointing thing
was that he was still the last.
He died in 2017.
And he remained the last.

<!-- S73 -->
So here's a question for you.
If there were no race to win,
what reason would be good enough
to go back?
Let me know in the comments.
