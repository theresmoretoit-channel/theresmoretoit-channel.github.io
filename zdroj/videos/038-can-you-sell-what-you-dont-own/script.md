<!-- S01 -->
Imagine I offer to sell you
a barrel of oil.
There's just one problem.
I don't own the oil.
In fact, I don't even want the oil.
I just think its price is going down.

<!-- S02 -->
So I sell it to you today...
and I plan to buy it back later,
hopefully for less.
If that sounds backwards,
that's because it is.

<!-- S03 -->
Welcome to short selling.

<!-- S04 -->
Normally, making money
is pretty intuitive.
You buy something for ten dollars.
You sell it for twenty.
Congratulations.
You made ten dollars.

<!-- S05 -->
Short selling flips
the whole thing upside down.
You sell first.
And only later do you buy.

<!-- S06 -->
The idea is simple.
Suppose oil is trading
at eighty dollars a barrel.
I think the price is going to fall.

<!-- S07 -->
So I sell an oil contract
for eighty dollars.
Then the price falls to sixty.
I buy it back for sixty.

<!-- S08 -->
And I keep the twenty-dollar difference.
At least in theory.

<!-- S09 -->
Because there's
a rather important detail.
What happens if I'm wrong?
If oil goes from eighty
to one hundred dollars,
I'm in trouble.

<!-- S10 -->
I sold something for eighty...
and now I have to buy it back
for one hundred.
I lose twenty dollars.

<!-- S11 -->
And if oil goes to two hundred?
I lose one hundred and twenty.
If it goes to five hundred?
Well...
you get the idea.

<!-- S12 -->
When you buy something normally,
the most you can lose
is what you paid for it.
With a short position,
there is theoretically no upper limit
to how much you can lose.

<!-- S13 -->
Because there is no upper limit
to how high the price can go.
And this creates
a very strange asymmetry.

<!-- S14 -->
If I buy oil for eighty dollars
and it falls to zero,
I lose eighty.
But if I short oil at eighty
and it goes to one hundred and sixty...
I've lost eighty.

<!-- S15 -->
If it goes to eight hundred...
I've lost seven hundred and twenty.
The upside is limited.
The downside isn't.

<!-- S16 -->
So why would anyone do this?

<!-- S17 -->
Because markets aren't just
about making money.
Sometimes you want to get rid of risk.

<!-- S18 -->
Imagine you're an airline.
Fuel is one of your biggest costs.
And you're worried that oil prices
might suddenly rise.

<!-- S19 -->
So you can buy futures contracts
that effectively lock in
a price for oil.
If oil goes up,
your fuel becomes more expensive...
but your futures position makes money.

<!-- S20 -->
You've exchanged
an uncertain future price
for a more predictable one.
Someone else takes the other side.
Maybe they're a speculator
who thinks oil will fall.

<!-- S21 -->
Maybe they're another company
with the opposite risk.
Maybe they're a market maker.

<!-- S22 -->
They don't have to believe
the exact same thing you do.
They just have to be willing
to take the other side of the contract.
And this is where
things start getting weird.

<!-- S23 -->
Because when we say
we're trading oil...
we often aren't trading oil.
We're trading a contract.

<!-- S24 -->
A futures contract
can say, essentially.
At a certain date,
under certain conditions,
one party will deliver
a certain amount of oil...
and another party will accept it.

<!-- S25 -->
But here's the important part.
Most futures contracts never result
in somebody receiving
a truck full of oil.

<!-- S26 -->
The traders simply exit their positions
before the contract expires.
So I can sell
a futures contract for oil...
and later buy the same contract back.

<!-- S27 -->
No barrel needs to travel anywhere.
The gain or loss is settled financially.
And suddenly our original question
gets even stranger.

<!-- S28 -->
Does the oil actually have to exist?
Not necessarily.

<!-- S29 -->
A futures contract can refer to oil
that will be produced in the future.
The oil doesn't have to be sitting
in a warehouse with your name on it.

<!-- S30 -->
And if everyone closes their positions
before delivery...
the physical oil may never enter
the transaction at all.

<!-- S31 -->
What we're really trading is a promise.
A standardised promise
that has a price.
And once you realise that...
you start seeing
these contracts everywhere.

<!-- S32 -->
But surely there has to be
some connection to the real world.
And there is.

<!-- S33 -->
There are companies
that actually produce oil.
There are refineries
that actually consume it.

<!-- S34 -->
There are pipelines, ships,
storage tanks
and millions of barrels of physical
crude moving around the planet.

<!-- S35 -->
The financial market doesn't float
completely free of reality.
If the futures price becomes
wildly different from the physical price,
traders can exploit the difference.

<!-- S36 -->
They can buy the cheap thing
and sell the expensive thing.
That's called arbitrage.

<!-- S37 -->
So physical reality
still acts as an anchor.
Usually.

<!-- S38 -->
But what happens when
the physical world suddenly
can't keep up with the financial one?
Well...
we actually got to see that happen.

<!-- S39 -->
In April twenty twenty,
during the COVID nineteen pandemic,
oil demand collapsed.
People stopped driving.
Airlines stopped flying.
Factories shut down.

<!-- S40 -->
But oil production
couldn't simply stop overnight.
And suddenly there was a problem.
There was too much oil...
and not enough storage space.

<!-- S41 -->
The oil wasn't imaginary.
It was very real.
There was just nowhere to put it.
And something absolutely bizarre
happened.

<!-- S42 -->
The price of a major oil futures
contract briefly fell below zero.
Yes.
Below zero.

<!-- S43 -->
Traders were effectively willing
to pay someone else
to take the contract.
Why?

<!-- S44 -->
Because if you were still
holding the contract
when it reached physical delivery...

<!-- S45 -->
you could end up with an obligation
to take delivery of oil.
And if you had nowhere to put it...
that was a very expensive problem.

<!-- S46 -->
For a moment, the question wasn't.
How much will you pay me for my oil?
It was.
How much will I pay you
to take this problem away from me?

<!-- S47 -->
And this is where finance
starts feeling less
like buying and selling things...
and more like trading obligations.

<!-- S48 -->
But we can go even further.
Because if you can trade
a contract on oil...
you don't necessarily need oil.

<!-- S49 -->
So what else
can we put into a contract?
Interest rates.
Currencies.
Stock indexes.
Inflation.
Credit risk.
Even weather.

<!-- S50 -->
And eventually...
something even more abstract.
The probability
of an event happening.

<!-- S51 -->
Imagine a market asking.
Will candidate X win the election?
A contract might pay one dollar
if the answer is yes...
and zero dollars
if the answer is no.

<!-- S52 -->
If that contract trades
for seventy cents,
the market is roughly saying.
We think there's about
a seventy percent chance.

<!-- S53 -->
You can buy it if you think
the real probability is higher.
You can sell it
if you think it's lower.

<!-- S54 -->
Now you're not trading oil.
You're not trading gold.
You're not even trading a company.
You're trading
a belief about the future.

<!-- S55 -->
Platforms such as Polymarket
have made this kind of
prediction market highly visible.
And suddenly the progression
looks almost ridiculous.

<!-- S56 -->
First, we traded things.
Then we traded contracts for things.
Then we traded contracts for things
that didn't exist yet.
Then we traded risk.
And now...
we can trade probabilities.

<!-- S57 -->
So let's go back
to our original question.
How can you sell
something you don't have?
The answer is.
You don't necessarily have to be
selling the thing.

<!-- S58 -->
You're selling a contract.
A contract that represents
an obligation, a future transaction,
a risk...
or sometimes just a bet
on what will happen.

<!-- S59 -->
And that is one of the strangest
things about modern finance.
A financial market doesn't
necessarily need a physical object.

<!-- S60 -->
It needs rules.
It needs counterparties.
It needs a way to settle the contract.
And most importantly...
it needs people who believe
the contract is worth something.

<!-- S61 -->
Which leads to
a rather uncomfortable question.
When you strip away the barrels of oil,
the warehouses,
the stock certificates
and the banknotes...
what exactly are we trading?

<!-- S62 -->
Maybe the answer is.
Expectations.
Risk.
Promises.
And beliefs about the future.
We built an enormous global machine
for trading those things.

<!-- S63 -->
And somehow...
it works.
Most of the time.
Until everyone wants
the same thing at the same time.

<!-- S64 -->
And then you might suddenly discover
that the barrel of oil
you were trading...
was never really the interesting part.
