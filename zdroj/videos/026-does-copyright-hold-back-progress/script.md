<!-- S01 -->
Imagine you find a recording
from nineteen fifty.
It is exactly what you need
for the documentary you are making.

<!-- S02 -->
Nobody sells it any more.
The company that released it
vanished decades ago.
The musician died long ago.

<!-- S03 -->
You want ten seconds of it.
And you may not be allowed to have them.

<!-- S04 -->
Not because it is secret.
Not because anyone else is using it.
But because somebody
may still own it.

<!-- S05 -->
And nobody knows who.

<!-- S06 -->
So here is the question.
Can copyright get in the way
of the thing it was built to encourage?

<!-- S07 -->
Let's start somewhere important.
Copyright is not a stupid idea.

<!-- S08 -->
Imagine you spend three years
writing a book, and publish it.
The next morning somebody
prints a million copies
and undercuts you by half.

<!-- S09 -->
You did the work.
They did not.

<!-- S10 -->
So the bargain is this.
Society gives you
a temporary monopoly.
You get your chance to earn from it.
And eventually,
it belongs to everybody.

<!-- S11 -->
That last part is the whole point.
It was never
the creator owns this forever.

<!-- S12 -->
So how long is temporary?

<!-- S13 -->
The Berne Convention,
which most countries have signed,
sets the floor at the life of the author
plus fifty years.

<!-- S14 -->
Britain, the European Union
and the United States
all went further than that.
The life of the author,
plus seventy years.

<!-- S15 -->
Work out what that means.
Someone writes a song at twenty-five.
They live to eighty-five.
Their song is locked up
for a hundred and thirty years.

<!-- S16 -->
Steamboat Willie,
the first Mickey Mouse cartoon
ever released, came out
in nineteen twenty-eight.
It became free to use
on the first of January,
two thousand and twenty-four.

<!-- S17 -->
Ninety-five years.

<!-- S18 -->
And that number matters,
because of the way
progress actually happens.

<!-- S19 -->
Nobody starts from nothing.
Someone discovers something.
Someone else studies it.
Someone improves it.
And someone builds on that.

<!-- S20 -->
Which leaves us with a tension.
Copyright says
we want to encourage creation.
But creating usually means:
let me use something
that already exists.

<!-- S21 -->
Music shows this best,
because a song is not one thing.

<!-- S22 -->
There is the composition.
The lyrics. The arrangement.
The recording itself.
The performance.
And anything sampled from elsewhere.

<!-- S22b -->
Every one of those
can belong to somebody different.

<!-- S23 -->
So you take two seconds
of an old recording.
You slow it down.
You cut it into pieces.
You bury it under a new beat.

<!-- S24 -->
The original is barely audible.
You have made something new.
Legally, it may still count.

<!-- S25 -->
And sometimes
nothing was copied at all.
There are only so many notes.
Only so many chord progressions.
Only so many rhythms.
With millions of songs,
resemblance is not suspicious.
It is arithmetic.

<!-- S26 -->
In two thousand and eighteen
an American appeal court upheld
a verdict of over five million dollars
against the writers of Blurred Lines.

<!-- S27 -->
The dissent pointed out
that the two songs differed
in melody, harmony,
and rhythm.
It warned that the majority
was effectively letting
someone copyright a musical style.

<!-- S28 -->
So the law cannot simply ask
whether two things sound alike.
It has to ask what was actually copied.

<!-- S28b -->
A melody? A rhythm?
A protected musical expression?
Or something that belongs
to everybody?

<!-- S29 -->
Software has the same problem,
only far more openly.
Modern software is built
out of other software:
libraries, frameworks,
operating systems, protocols.

<!-- S30 -->
Now take the harder case.
You buy a device.
You take it apart.
You work out how it does what it does.
And you build your own version.

<!-- S31 -->
You did not copy the product.
You extracted the knowledge.

<!-- S32 -->
In nineteen ninety-two
an American court agreed.
Copying a games console's code
in order to understand it
counted as fair use.

<!-- S33 -->
Not because copying is harmless,
but because there was no other way in.

<!-- S34 -->
In two thousand and twenty-one
the American Supreme Court
went further still.
Google had reused
eleven thousand five hundred lines
of somebody else's interface.

<!-- S35 -->
Four tenths of one per cent
of the whole system.

<!-- S36 -->
The court called it fair use,
six votes to two,
because it let programmers
carry what they already knew
into somewhere new.

<!-- S37 -->
Which takes us somewhere uncomfortable.

<!-- S38 -->
Think of a photograph
from nineteen thirty.
The photographer may be dead.
Their family may not know
the photograph exists.
The publisher may be gone.

<!-- S39 -->
You want it for a documentary.
You cannot ask permission,
because you cannot find
anybody to ask.

<!-- S40 -->
That is an orphan work.
It exists.
It has value.
Nobody is earning from it.
And using it is still a risk.

<!-- S41 -->
Europe tried to fix this
in twenty twelve.
You may use an orphan work,
provided you first carry out
what the law calls
a diligent search.

<!-- S42 -->
So the price of using
an abandoned photograph
is the price of proving
that you went looking for its owner.

<!-- S43 -->
And so the work stays unused.
Not because anyone
wants to keep it from you.
But because nobody knows
who is allowed to say yes.

<!-- S44 -->
So should we simply
abolish copyright?

<!-- S45 -->
Probably not.

<!-- S46 -->
Imagine a film that costs
a hundred million dollars.
Thousands of people.
Years of work.
And on the day it opens,
somebody sells a perfect copy
for almost nothing.

<!-- S47 -->
That destroys the reason
to make the expensive thing at all.

<!-- S48 -->
"Everything should be free"
does not solve the problem.
It just moves it somewhere else.

<!-- S49 -->
So the question is not
copyright or no copyright.
It is how much.

<!-- S50 -->
And some of the answers
already exist.
If you want to record
your own version
of somebody else's song
in the United States,
you do not need their permission.

<!-- S51 -->
You file a notice.
You pay a fixed rate.
That is the entire process.

<!-- S52 -->
That system was written
in nineteen oh nine.

<!-- S53 -->
Compulsory licensing
is not a thought experiment.
It is older than
almost everybody using it.

<!-- S54 -->
The same idea could cover sampling.
You used two seconds?
Report it. Pay the rate. Done.

<!-- S55 -->
No months of negotiation.
No hunting for whoever owns
three seconds of a recording
from nineteen seventy-four.

<!-- S56 -->
And the law could take
transformation more seriously.
Pirating a film is not the same thing
as making a documentary about it.

<!-- S57 -->
Uploading somebody's song
is not the same thing
as sampling two seconds of it.

<!-- S58 -->
Copying a rival's code
is not the same thing
as building something
that works alongside it.

<!-- S59 -->
If you want the next one,
there is a subscribe button.

<!-- S60 -->
And then, of course,
we built generative AI.

<!-- S61 -->
Can a model learn
from copyrighted books?
From copyrighted music?
Can it imitate
somebody's particular style?
And if it can,
who owns what comes out?

<!-- S62 -->
In twenty twenty-five
two American courts
answered part of it.
Training a model
on copyrighted books
was transformative enough
to count as fair use.

<!-- S63 -->
But one of those same rulings
found that keeping a library
of pirated books was not.

<!-- S64 -->
Which is exactly the distinction
this whole argument
has been circling.
Not, did you use it.
But, what did you do with it.

<!-- S65 -->
AI did not create that question.
It just made it
impossible to keep avoiding.

<!-- S66 -->
Because there is no free option here.
Strong protection gives people
a reason to invest.
It also makes existing culture
harder to reuse.

<!-- S67 -->
Weak protection makes
building on the past easier.
It also makes expensive work
harder to pay for.

<!-- S68 -->
Which is why the goal
was never to maximise protection,
and never to minimise it either.
The goal was to maximise
how much gets made.
And those are not the same thing.

<!-- S69 -->
So perhaps the real question
is not how we stop people copying.
It is how we stop free-riding
without stopping building.

<!-- S70 -->
And the most important thing
about copyright may not be
what it hands today's creators.
It is what it leaves
for tomorrow's.

<!-- S71 -->
Because if we guard the past
too carefully...

<!-- S72 -->
we end up with a system
so good at protecting
what has already been made
that making the next thing
is unnecessarily hard.

<!-- S73 -->
And that would be
a very strange way
to encourage progress.
