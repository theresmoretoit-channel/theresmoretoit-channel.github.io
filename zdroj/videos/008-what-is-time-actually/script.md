# What Is Time, Actually?

Scenes are separated by `<!-- Sxx -->`.
The text below each marker is exactly what is sent to TTS.

<!-- S01 -->
What is time?

At first glance it is one of
the simplest things in the world.

<!-- S02 -->
We have clocks.

We have calendars.

We know what time it is.

We know that yesterday was yesterday
and that tomorrow will be tomorrow.

<!-- S03 -->
But stop for a moment
and ask yourself a simple question.

What is time, actually?

And suddenly it is much stranger.

<!-- S04 -->
Because we may be struggling to separate
two completely different things.

Time itself.

And our feeling that time flows.

<!-- S05 -->
And that one distinction takes us
from ordinary clocks to Einstein,

to entropy, to the brain,

and to the question of whether
there is any objective now at all.

<!-- S06 -->
When we say that an hour has passed,
what has actually happened?

A clock ticked a certain number of times.

<!-- S07 -->
An atomic clock counted the transitions
between the energy states of an atom.

A pendulum swung back and forth.

A planet turned through a certain angle.

<!-- S08 -->
But notice something.

Clocks do not create time.

They only compare one regular change
against another change.

<!-- S09 -->
We say, for instance,
that an event lasted ten seconds.

And what we are really saying is this.

<!-- S10 -->
During that event, as many regular changes
went past as ten seconds are made of.

<!-- S11 -->
So in everyday life
we come to know time through change.

And this is where
it starts to get interesting.

<!-- S12 -->
Before Einstein, physics pictured time
very simply.

Newton imagined time as something
that exists entirely on its own.

<!-- S13 -->
The universe can change.

Objects can move.

But somewhere in the background
there is a universal clock.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

And it ticks in exactly the same way
for everybody.

<!-- S14 -->
Time was supposed to be
a vast invisible river,

flowing through the whole universe
at one steady rate.

<!-- S15 -->
But Einstein turned up with a problem.

And the problem
was the speed of light.

<!-- S16 -->
Under special relativity,
light in a vacuum has the same speed
for every observer.

And that leads to something
completely mad.

<!-- S17 -->
Time is not the same for everyone.

If two observers move at different speeds
relative to each other,

they can measure
different intervals of time.

<!-- S18 -->
So time is not a universal backdrop.

It is part of the physical system.

<!-- S19 -->
And here is the strange part.

Each of the two will say
that it is the other one's clock
that runs slow.

Not their own.

The other one's.

<!-- S20 -->
It is not an optical illusion.

It is not a fault in the clocks.

Time really does pass differently
for different observers.

And that was only the beginning.

<!-- S21 -->
Einstein's relativity joined
space and time into a single object.

Spacetime.

<!-- S22 -->
So we do not simply have
space and time side by side.

We have spacetime.

And that has one very odd consequence.

<!-- S23 -->
When we ask what happened
at the same time as some event,

the answer need not be the same
for every observer.

<!-- S24 -->
Picture two people moving
quickly relative to each other.

They can agree
that two events took place.

But they need not agree
on whether those events happened
at the same moment.

<!-- S25 -->
And that brings us to one of the strangest
ideas in all of physics.

There is no universal now.

<!-- S26 -->
We feel that there is one single moment
that is genuinely present.

Right now.

You are watching this video.

This is the present.

<!-- S27 -->
But special relativity tells us
there is no single universal slice
of the present

that holds for the whole universe.

<!-- S28 -->
What counts as now for me
need not count as now

for an observer far away
who is moving very fast.

<!-- S29 -->
And here a rather interesting
philosophical possibility opens up.

Perhaps the universe is not something
that keeps changing

from the past, through the present,
into the future.

<!-- S30 -->
Perhaps reality is more like
a four-dimensional structure of events.

This view is often called
the block universe.

<!-- S31 -->
But be careful.

Relativity on its own does not prove
that the block universe
is definitely the right description.

<!-- S32 -->
It is an interpretation
that follows very naturally
from the relativistic picture.

And if it is right,

then our idea of flowing time
starts to look very strange indeed.

<!-- S33 -->
So why do we experience time
as a one-way street?

Here is something everybody knows.

A broken egg.

<!-- S34 -->
Drop an egg on the floor and it breaks.

But you never see the pieces
gather themselves back up

and the egg leap off the ground
into your hand.

Why not?

<!-- S35 -->
Because of something we call entropy.

Very roughly,
a system has an enormous number
of untidy states available to it,

and far fewer tidy ones.

<!-- S36 -->
A whole egg is one very particular state.

Shards on the floor can be arranged
in an unimaginable number of ways.

<!-- S37 -->
So it is far more likely that a system
moves from order towards disorder.

And that is how we get
the arrow of time.

<!-- S38 -->
But wait.

That cannot be the whole story.

Because entropy does not increase
everywhere.

Look around you.

<!-- S39 -->
Crystals form.

Stars form.

Planets form.

And eventually…

things like us form.

<!-- S40 -->
Life is an extraordinarily complex,
highly structured system.

So how can something that ordered exist

in a universe that is supposedly
moving towards disorder?

<!-- S41 -->
Because the second law does not say
that entropy must increase everywhere.

It says that for an isolated system,
the total entropy does not decrease.

<!-- S42 -->
The Earth takes in energy from the Sun
in a fairly concentrated form.

<!-- S43 -->
And it eventually sends that energy
back out into space

as more dispersed thermal radiation.

In between,
life gets to do interesting things.

<!-- S44 -->
And now one of the loveliest paradoxes
in the whole of science.

Many of the basic laws of physics
work almost equally well
when we run time backwards.

<!-- S45 -->
Picture a film of planets
going round their orbits.

Play it in reverse.

At first glance
nothing looks wrong at all.

<!-- S46 -->
Many basic equations behave the same way.

They do not say: here is the past.

Here is the future.

They never say
that time must run in this direction.

So where does our arrow of time
come from?

<!-- S47 -->
One of the main explanations
is entropy again.

Our universe began in a state
of extraordinarily low entropy.

<!-- S48 -->
And from that very odd starting point,
entropy has been rising ever since.

That creates a direction
which we experience as

past, and then future.

<!-- S49 -->
And here we run into
an even deeper problem.

The second law may explain
why entropy keeps increasing.

<!-- S50 -->
But only if we already start
with a universe
in a very special state.

A state of extraordinarily low entropy.

<!-- S51 -->
So perhaps the real question is not
why entropy increases.

Perhaps it is this.

Why was the universe ever so ordered
in the first place?

<!-- S52 -->
We do not yet have a complete answer.

We thought we were asking
what time is.

<!-- S53 -->
But now we have found
an even stranger question.

Why does the universe
have a direction at all?

<!-- S54 -->
But entropy alone does not explain
our whole feeling of time.

Because we do not only measure time.

We live through it.

<!-- S55 -->
We feel that the past has already been,

that the present is happening now,

and that the future
is still on its way.

Why?

<!-- S56 -->
One important reason is memory.

We hold records of the past.

We hold no memories of the future.

<!-- S57 -->
When I remember yesterday's breakfast,
my brain holds information
about an event that really happened.

<!-- S58 -->
But I hold no equally available record
of what I am going to eat tomorrow.

And that creates
a very powerful asymmetry.

<!-- S59 -->
The brain is constantly taking
information in,

storing some of it,

comparing the new against the old,

and predicting what comes next.

<!-- S60 -->
And out of that process
comes our subjective feeling.

I am moving through time right now.

<!-- S61 -->
And there is another problem.

Our awareness cannot take in
the whole world
in a single mathematical instant.

<!-- S62 -->
The brain needs time
to process information.

A signal from the eye
has to reach the brain.

The brain has to work on it.

<!-- S63 -->
It has to compare it
against other information.

And only then
does a conscious perception appear.

<!-- S64 -->
So our now is not
a perfectly sharp point.

It is more like the outcome
of an enormous amount of processing.

<!-- S65 -->
The brain builds a model of reality
that is consistent enough
for us to function.

And that model contains something
we call the present.

<!-- S66 -->
And we can watch this happening
every single day.

We sit in a waiting room for two hours.

And it feels like a whole day.

<!-- S67 -->
Then we spend six hours
playing a brilliant game.

We look at the clock.

And we say: is it really that late?

<!-- S68 -->
The clocks were working
in exactly the same way.

The physics did not change.

What changed
was our experience of time.

<!-- S69 -->
Attention, emotion,
the amount of new information,
and memory

all shape how long
a stretch of time feels.

<!-- S70 -->
So when we say
that the day went by terribly fast,

we may not be talking
about physical time at all.

We are talking about
our model of time inside the brain.

<!-- S71 -->
And here we should be careful.

Saying that time is merely an illusion
is far too easy.

<!-- S72 -->
Time is not something
we simply made up.

Relativistic effects on time are real.

Atomic clocks measure them.

<!-- S73 -->
Satellite navigation
has to correct for them.

Time is part of our most successful
physical theories.

<!-- S74 -->
So time is certainly not
just an invention of the human brain.

But something else
may well be an illusion.

<!-- S75 -->
Our idea that time really flows.

And perhaps also our idea
that there is one universal moment.

Now.

<!-- S76 -->
Picture a whole life.

Childhood.

School.

A first love.

Work.

Family.

Today.

And one day,
the last moments of that life.

We experience all of it as a journey
from the past into the future.

<!-- S77 -->
But it may be more accurate
to picture a whole life
as a sequence of events in spacetime.

Every moment has its own place
in that structure.

<!-- S78 -->
And our awareness always holds
information about some earlier states,

and builds expectations
of the states still to come.

<!-- S79 -->
And that may be exactly what creates
the feeling that time is flowing.

This does not mean
that we know that time does not flow.

We do not know that.

<!-- S80 -->
It is one of the deep open problems
on the border
between physics and philosophy.

<!-- S81 -->
So what is time?

Perhaps the best answer
is a surprisingly modest one.

We do not entirely know.

<!-- S82 -->
In modern physics,
time is part of the structure
that describes events

and the relations between them.

<!-- S83 -->
Relativity showed us
that it is not universal.

Thermodynamics gives us the arrow of time
through rising entropy.

<!-- S84 -->
And our brain builds,
out of those physical processes,

a subjective experience of the past,
the present and the future.

<!-- S85 -->
So perhaps there are
two quite different things.

Physical time.

And lived time.

And they need not be the same thing.

<!-- S86 -->
So the next time you glance at a clock
and read the hour off its face,

you might remember
what it is not showing you.

<!-- S87 -->
There is no invisible river
running through the universe.

The clock shows you
a relation between changes.

<!-- S88 -->
And your brain turns that
into something far stranger.

The feeling that there is a past,

that there is a right now,

and that the future
is still waiting ahead of us.

<!-- S89 -->
And perhaps the greatest mystery
is not where time is flowing to.

It is why we feel
that it is flowing anywhere at all.

<!-- S90 -->
And if that feeling is only the way
our brain reads change,
memory and entropy,

then the strangest thing about time
may not be that it exists.

It is that we can experience it at all.
