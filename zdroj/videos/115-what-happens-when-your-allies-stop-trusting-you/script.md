# What Happens When Your Allies Stop Trusting You?
**Script v0.7**

<!-- S01 -->
There is one sentence in the NATO treaty
that every member has agreed to.
An armed attack
against one or more of them
shall be considered
an attack against them all.

<!-- S02 -->
It is Article Five.
In more than seventy-five years,
it has been invoked exactly once.

<!-- S03 -->
Not for Poland.
Not for the Baltic states.
Not for anyone in Europe.

<!-- S04 -->
On the twelfth of September, 2001,
the allies agreed to invoke it.
A few weeks later, they confirmed it.
The attack on the United States
was an attack on them all.

<!-- S05 -->
Over the next twenty years,
soldiers from dozens of countries
followed the US into Afghanistan.
More than a thousand of them
never came home.

<!-- S06 -->
Britain lost four hundred and fifty-seven.
Canada lost a hundred and fifty-eight.
Denmark, a country of six million,
lost forty-four.

<!-- S07 -->
Twenty-five years later,
a large survey asked Europeans
how they see the United States.

<!-- S08 -->
Only eleven per cent called it an ally.
The most common answer was colder.
A necessary partner.

<!-- S09 -->
That is not hatred.
In a way, it is worse.
It is an ally doing the maths.

<!-- S10 -->
So what happens to the most powerful
country on Earth
when its allies stop trusting it?
Not to them.
To the United States.

<!-- S10b -->
The first part of the answer
is not dramatic.
Nobody storms out.
What happens is quieter,
and much harder to undo.

<!-- S22 -->
Trust sounds like something soft.
Something for friends and families.
Between countries,
it is a working tool.

<!-- S23 -->
Imagine two countries
building a fighter jet together.
It will take ten years,
billions in shared factories,
and parts that only fit each other.

<!-- S24 -->
If both expect the partnership
to survive the next election,
that plan makes sense.

<!-- S25 -->
But if one of them starts to wonder
whether the other will walk away
the moment it is convenient,
the maths changes.

<!-- S26 -->
It builds a second factory at home.
It buys from two suppliers, not one.
It keeps a backup plan
it hopes it will never need.

<!-- S27 -->
Nobody announces anything.
Nobody leaves an alliance.
They just stop planning
on the assumption that you'll be there,
and start planning
for the possibility that you won't.

<!-- S28 -->
And in the last few years,
the allies of the United States
were given a lot of reasons
to do exactly that.

<!-- S29 -->
Canada, which followed the US
into Afghanistan,
was hit with tariffs
and told it should become
the fifty-first state.

<!-- S30 -->
Denmark, which lost forty-four soldiers
there, was told the United States
wanted Greenland,
and for a year, would not rule out force.

<!-- S31 -->
Ukraine, in the middle of a war,
saw US intelligence switched off
for a week.
Today, Europe is Ukraine's
largest source of military aid,
and Europeans now pay
for many of the US weapons
that reach Ukraine.

<!-- S32 -->
And when diesel at home
passed six dollars a gallon,
Washington eased its sanctions
so that Russian diesel
could flow to the United States.

<!-- S33 -->
Russia.
The country that many US allies
are spending billions to deter.

<!-- S34 -->
Even if all of it came,
it would cover less than two weeks
of US demand.
And the United States
exports more diesel than it imports.

<!-- S35 -->
Every one of these
can be explained on its own.
A negotiating tactic. An election.
A price at the pump.

<!-- S36 -->
But allies do not plan
around explanations.
They plan around patterns.

<!-- S37 -->
And the lesson they drew was simple.
A US promise could be taken back
the next time something else
mattered more.

<!-- S38 -->
So the allies started building
the backup plan.

<!-- S39 -->
The European Union set up
a hundred and fifty billion euros
in loans to buy weapons together.
And the rules favour Europe:
at least sixty-five per cent
of the cost of the parts
has to come from Europe
or its close partners.

<!-- S40 -->
Spain shelved plans
for the US-made F-35 fighter
and is looking at European jets.
Canada is reviewing
its own F-35 order.

<!-- S41 -->
In South Korea,
a poll this year found
that nearly two thirds of people
want their own nuclear weapons.

<!-- S42 -->
Read that one again.
A country protected for seventy years
by the US nuclear umbrella
now wonders whether it needs
a bomb of its own.

<!-- S43 -->
That is one of the risks
when security guarantees
lose their credibility.
The question stops being
who will protect us,
and becomes how we protect ourselves.

<!-- S44 -->
And then there is Canada.

<!-- S45 -->
Canada sends about two thirds
of its exports to the United States.
The two share the longest border
on Earth.

<!-- S46 -->
Yet in 2026, Canada became
the first country outside Europe
to join the EU's joint
arms-buying scheme.

<!-- S47 -->
And in a poll this spring,
when Canadians were asked
whether their country should become
a full member of the European Union,
fifty-seven per cent said they would
support it, at least somewhat.

<!-- S48 -->
That is not government policy.
Ottawa says full membership
is not on the table.
But ask yourself why so many Canadians
are willing to entertain the idea.

<!-- S49 -->
Now here is the part
that should sting in Washington.

<!-- S50 -->
For decades, the United States
asked its allies to spend more
on their own defence.
It was a fair request.

<!-- S51 -->
And now they are.
NATO allies have agreed to aim
for five per cent of their economies
by 2035.

<!-- S52 -->
The US got exactly what it asked for.
Except that some of that money
now builds Europe's own industry,
so that, over time, Europe depends less
on US suppliers.

<!-- S53 -->
There is a difference
between asking an ally
to carry more of the load,
and convincing it that one day
it may have to carry all of it.

<!-- S54 -->
The first makes an alliance stronger.
The second makes it optional.

<!-- S54b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S54c -->
So that is what happens to the allies.
They hedge.
But the question was never about them.
It was about the United States.

<!-- S11 -->
To answer that, start with a question
that sounds almost rude.
What was the United States
actually paying for?

<!-- S12 -->
After the Second World War,
it helped rebuild Western Europe,
kept troops across the world,
and promised to defend countries
that could not defend themselves.

<!-- S13 -->
That was never charity.
Washington had its own interests,
and it never hid them.
But what it got back
rarely showed up on a bill.

<!-- S14 -->
Take the wounded and the sick
from Iraq and Afghanistan.
For two decades, tens of thousands
of US and allied troops
passed through one hospital.
It was not in the United States.
It was in Landstuhl, Germany.

<!-- S15 -->
The aircraft that flew them there
landed at Ramstein,
a US air base on German soil,
under agreements already in place.

<!-- S16 -->
Or take computer chips.
The most advanced ones are made
with machines that only one company
in the world builds.
It is Dutch.

<!-- S17 -->
When Washington wanted to slow down
China's chip industry,
the Netherlands and Japan
joined export restrictions
on advanced chipmaking equipment.

<!-- S18 -->
Or take money itself.
Most of the world's foreign currency
reserves are still held in US dollars.
That helps the United States
borrow more cheaply
than it otherwise could.

<!-- S19 -->
A French finance minister
had a name for that in the 1960s.
An exorbitant privilege.

<!-- S20 -->
Bases. Hospitals. Export controls.
Sanctions that actually bite,
when key partners join them.
And an alliance that went to war
when the US was attacked.

<!-- S21 -->
None of that is free.
But almost none of it
is bought with money either.
It is bought with trust.

<!-- S55 -->
So when the trust goes,
the allies are left with options.
And a country with options
can say no.

<!-- S56 -->
No to the next round of export controls.
No to the next sanctions package.
No to the base. No to the airspace.
No to the vote at the United Nations.

<!-- S57 -->
Not out of hostility.
But because saying no
no longer costs what it used to.

<!-- S58 -->
And here is what makes it
so hard to undo.

<!-- S59 -->
Suppose the next government
in Washington changes course.
It apologises,
renews every promise,
and means it.

<!-- S60 -->
The factory in Europe
is already built.
The new supplier has been tested.
The contracts are signed.
None of that disappears
when relations improve.

<!-- S61 -->
And the allies have learned something
that cannot be unlearned.
A promise from Washington
may last only
until the next election.

<!-- S62 -->
So even the best promise
now comes with an expiry date.
That is the real cost.
Not one bad deal,
but every future deal worth less.

<!-- S63 -->
None of this means
the United States is collapsing.
It still has the strongest military,
the deepest markets,
and the world's most important currency.

<!-- S64 -->
But power was never only
what a country can do on its own.
It was also what others did
because it asked.

<!-- S65 -->
For eighty years,
when something went wrong in the world,
the first call went to Washington.
Not because anyone had to call.
Because everyone assumed
it would answer.

<!-- S66 -->
The allies have not become enemies.
They are still trading,
still talking,
still sitting at the same tables.

<!-- S67 -->
They have just started
keeping other numbers on speed dial.

<!-- S68 -->
And in Denmark, forty-four families
still remember the cost
of answering that call.

<!-- S69 -->
The call came from Washington.

<!-- S69b -->
If you are watching this
in the United States,
this is not just a story
about your allies.
It is about what your country
stands to lose.

<!-- S69c -->
Some of these changes
are already difficult to reverse.
Others are still being decided.

<!-- S69d -->
The United States is losing
the advantages of trust,
one quiet decision at a time.
And that is still a choice.
