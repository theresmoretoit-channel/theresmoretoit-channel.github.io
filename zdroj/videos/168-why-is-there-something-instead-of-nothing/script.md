<!-- S01 -->
When the universe was very young,
it made matter and antimatter
in almost exactly equal amounts.

<!-- S02 -->
And when matter meets antimatter,
both of them disappear.
All that's left is a flash of light.

<!-- S03 -->
So by now, the universe should be empty.
No stars, no planets, no people.
Just a faint glow of light,
spreading out forever.

<!-- S04 -->
But it isn't empty.
Because the two amounts
weren't quite equal.

<!-- S05 -->
For every billion particles
of antimatter,
there were about a billion and one
particles of matter.

<!-- S06 -->
The billions met and destroyed each other.
The one in a billion was left over.
And everything you have ever seen
is made of that leftover.

<!-- S07 -->
We even know the ratio.
The early universe left behind
a faint glow that still fills space.
For every proton and neutron,
there are more than a billion
particles of light.

<!-- S08 -->
Why the extra one?
Nobody knows yet.
Our best theory of particles
allows a tiny imbalance,
but nowhere near enough.

<!-- S09 -->
Physicists are hunting for the rest.
One suspect is the neutrino,
the particle that passes
through almost everything.

<!-- S10 -->
But even if they find the answer,
that would only tell us
why there's matter instead of just light.
Not why there's anything at all.

<!-- S11 -->
Why is there a universe
to make matter in?
Why is there something,
instead of nothing?

<!-- S12 -->
In 1714, the German philosopher
Gottfried Leibniz asked exactly that.
And he added something
that sounds simple, but isn't.

<!-- S13 -->
Nothing, he wrote,
is simpler and easier than something.

<!-- S14 -->
At first glance,
nothing seems easier to explain.
No rules, no beginning, no cause.
With something, you have to explain all three.
<!-- S15 -->
So the strange thing
isn't that the universe is weird.
It's that there's a universe at all.

<!-- S16 -->
Physics has several ideas
about where the universe came from.
But there's a problem
with every one of them.
They all start with something.

<!-- S17 -->
Let's start with the easiest version
of nothing.
Take a box.
Remove everything inside it.

<!-- S18 -->
Every atom. Every speck of dust.
Every particle of light.
Cool it down as far as physics allows.

<!-- S19 -->
That's nothing, surely.
Empty space.

<!-- S20 -->
It isn't.
According to quantum physics,
empty space is full of fields,
and those fields can never be
perfectly still.

<!-- S21 -->
They keep trembling,
even in their lowest possible state.
That lowest state is called the vacuum.
And the vacuum is busy.

<!-- S22 -->
You can even measure it.
Put two metal plates in a vacuum,
far less than a hair's width apart,
and they get pushed towards each other.

<!-- S23 -->
Nothing touches them.
In the usual picture,
the push comes from the vacuum itself.
It's called the Casimir effect,
after the Dutch physicist
who predicted it in 1948.

<!-- S24 -->
And that gave some physicists an idea.
If empty space is restless,
maybe a whole universe
could come out of it.

<!-- S25 -->
In 1973, the physicist Edward Tryon
asked whether the universe
might be a fluctuation of the vacuum.

<!-- S26 -->
But where would the energy come from?

<!-- S27 -->
Maybe it doesn't have to.
Matter carries positive energy.
Gravity, in a sense,
carries negative energy.
Add them up,
and the total might be exactly zero.

<!-- S28 -->
In 2012, the physicist Lawrence Krauss
wrote a bestselling book on this idea.
Its title was
"A Universe from Nothing".

<!-- S29 -->
And a philosopher of physics,
David Albert, wrote a review
that went straight for the title.

<!-- S30 -->
Quantum fields, he pointed out,
aren't nothing.
They're stuff.
Stuff that follows laws.

<!-- S31 -->
That explains how the house was built.
It doesn't explain the bricks.

<!-- S32 -->
So take away even more.
No vacuum. No fields.
No space at all,
and not even time.

<!-- S33 -->
In 1982, the physicist Alexander Vilenkin
described a universe that appears
with no space and no time before it.

<!-- S34 -->
In his model, a tiny universe appears
through a quantum process
called tunnelling.
Then it starts to expand.

<!-- S35 -->
This is one of the closest things
physics has come to nothing.
<!-- S36 -->
But the laws of physics
are still there.
They allow the universe to appear.
And nobody has explained
why those laws exist
in the first place.

<!-- S37 -->
Each step took something away.
None of them got to zero.

<!-- S38 -->
Physics describes how one state
changes into another.
But every explanation needs
something to start with.

<!-- S39 -->
A state. A rule. Something.
True nothing has none of these.
And that's where physics
reaches its limit.

<!-- S40 -->
In 1948, on BBC radio,
the philosopher Bertrand Russell
debated this with a Jesuit priest,
Frederick Copleston.

<!-- S41 -->
Copleston started
with the things around us.
Stars, planets, people.
None of them had to exist.
Each could have been otherwise.

<!-- S42 -->
Philosophers call that contingent.
Something that exists,
but could have failed to.

<!-- S43 -->
And Copleston argued
that adding up contingent things
doesn't help.
The whole universe is contingent too.
It can't be the reason for itself.

<!-- S44 -->
So, he concluded,
something must exist necessarily.
Something that could not
have failed to exist,
and needs no reason beyond itself.
For Copleston, that was God.

<!-- S45 -->
It's the answer Leibniz gave too.
And notice what it does
to the question "what explains God?"
For Copleston, that question
doesn't arise.
A necessary being is where
the questions stop.

<!-- S46 -->
Russell wasn't persuaded.
Every person who exists has a mother,
he said.
That doesn't mean
the human race has a mother.

<!-- S47 -->
Each thing in the universe
may need a cause.
That doesn't mean
the universe as a whole needs one.

<!-- S48 -->
And he doubted that "necessary"
could apply to a thing at all.
Only statements can be necessary,
he said. Not things.

<!-- S49 -->
Russell's own view was simpler.
The universe, he said,
is just there,
and that's all.

<!-- S50 -->
To Copleston, that was refusing to play.
Years later, he wrote that if you won't
even sit down at the chessboard,
you can't be checkmated.

<!-- S51 -->
For Copleston, the chain of reasons
ended in God.
For Russell, there was
no further explanation to give.

<!-- S52 -->
And physics doesn't escape this either.
If a law explains the universe,
what explains the law?
Each of these chains ends somewhere.
The question is where.

<!-- S53 -->
There's another way out,
and it turns Leibniz upside down.
<!-- S54 -->
In 1996, the philosopher Peter van Inwagen
asked how many ways
a world could be.

<!-- S55 -->
There are endlessly many ways
for there to be something.
One star, or two, or a billion.
Different laws, different everything.

<!-- S56 -->
But there's only one way
for there to be nothing.

<!-- S57 -->
His idea was simple.
If every way a world could be
were equally likely,
the empty one
would practically never come up.
<!-- S58 -->
Leibniz thought nothing
was the simplest state of all.
Van Inwagen thought it was
far less likely than something.

<!-- S59 -->
Not everyone is convinced.
Why should every possibility
be equally likely,
like tickets in a lottery?
And endless possibilities
don't come with odds attached.
<!-- S60 -->
And maybe the question itself
is stranger than it looks.
Ask what nothing is like,
and you're already treating it
as a kind of something.

<!-- S61 -->
Physics can tell us a lot.
One day it may even tell us
why there's matter instead of light,
and how a universe might grow
from almost nothing.

<!-- S62 -->
What it can't tell us
is why there's anything
for its laws to be about.

<!-- S63 -->
And philosophy hasn't settled it either.
Every explanation we've heard
either starts with something,
or stops at something.
This is where explaining itself
runs out.

<!-- S64 -->
At least one small part of that universe
can now ask why it exists.

<!-- S65 -->
Nothingness can't wonder
why there's something.
Only something can.

<!-- S66 -->
So here's a question for you.
Is "the universe is just there"
an honest answer,
or a way of not answering?
Let me know in the comments.
