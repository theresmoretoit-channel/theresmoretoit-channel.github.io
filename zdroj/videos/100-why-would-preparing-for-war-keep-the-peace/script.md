# Why Would Preparing for War Keep the Peace?

<!-- S01 -->
Do you want peace?

<!-- S01b -->
Then prepare for war.

<!-- S02 -->
It sounds almost absurd.
If the goal is never to fight,
why build tanks, missiles and warships?

<!-- S03 -->
And yet, in 2025, the world spent
nearly two point nine trillion dollars
on the military.
The most ever recorded.

<!-- S04 -->
Most governments that spend it
say the same thing.
It's not for starting a war.
It's for preventing one.

<!-- S05 -->
So is that a contradiction?
Or is preparing for war one of the reasons
war sometimes doesn't happen?

<!-- S06 -->
Start with the simplest version.
Two countries.
One is thinking about attacking the other.
If the target is weak,
the attack looks cheap.
A few weeks. A quick victory. Done.

<!-- S07 -->
Now change one thing.
The target can fight back,
and make the war long, bloody, expensive.
The attacker's question changes.
It's no longer "can we win?"
It's "is winning worth the price?"

<!-- S08 -->
That's deterrence.
You don't have to defeat your enemy.
You have to convince them
that attacking you is a terrible deal.

<!-- S09 -->
The idea is old.
Its famous Latin version is
si vis pacem, para bellum.

<!-- S10 -->
It's usually credited to Vegetius,
a Roman who wrote about warfare
about sixteen hundred years ago.

<!-- S11 -->
He never wrote those exact words.
What he wrote was longer:
let him who desires peace
prepare for war.

<!-- S12 -->
It wasn't a boast, either.
His book was a complaint
that Rome had let its army decay.
Soldiers, he wrote, had stopped drilling,
and even stopped wearing their helmets
and body armour.

<!-- S12b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S13 -->
Weakness invites attack.
That part of the idea holds up well.
There's a catch, though.
Your enemy can see your preparations too.

<!-- S14 -->
Say Country A is worried about Country B,
so it builds more tanks.
From the inside, that's pure defence.

<!-- S15 -->
Country B can't see inside A's head.
It only sees the tanks.
So it builds tanks of its own.
Now A is worried again.
More tanks.
Then B. Then A.

<!-- S16 -->
Neither side wants a war.
Both think they're only defending.
Both end up more armed, and less safe.

<!-- S17 -->
In 1950, the political scientist
John Herz gave this a name:
the security dilemma.

<!-- S18 -->
It isn't just theory.
In 1906, Britain launched HMS Dreadnought,
a battleship so advanced
it made every other one outdated.

<!-- S19 -->
Including Britain's own.
Its huge lead in older ships
suddenly counted for much less.
Germany, already building a fleet,
raced to match the new design.
Each side said it was only for defence.

<!-- S20 -->
The race didn't cause
the First World War on its own.
It certainly didn't prevent it.

<!-- S21 -->
Then came nuclear weapons,
and the logic got even stranger.
At the peak, in 1986,
the world had around
seventy thousand nuclear warheads.

<!-- S22 -->
Almost all of them belonged
to just two countries:
the United States and the Soviet Union.
Far more than either side needed
to devastate the other.

<!-- S23 -->
Why would that make anyone safer?

<!-- S24 -->
Because even after a first strike,
the other side could still strike back.
Attacking would be suicide.

<!-- S25 -->
It became known as
mutually assured destruction.
Or, fittingly, MAD.
I won't destroy you,
because you can destroy me.
You won't destroy me, for the same reason.

<!-- S26 -->
The weapons weren't useful
because they could be used.
They were useful because using them
would be a catastrophe.

<!-- S27 -->
Which gives military power
a strange property.
When it works, nothing happens.
No battle. No victory.
No headline.
Just... nothing.

<!-- S28 -->
Since 1949, no outside power has invaded
the territory the NATO treaty protects.

<!-- S29 -->
Is that deterrence?
Or would nobody have tried anyway?
We can't know.
History doesn't let us run it twice.
We only ever see the wars that happened.

<!-- S30 -->
Deterrence does fail, though.
It doesn't depend on weapons alone.
It depends on what the other side
believes.

<!-- S31 -->
In 1981, Britain announced it would retire
its only regular warship
in the South Atlantic.

<!-- S32 -->
It was meant to save money.
In Argentina, it was read as one more sign
that Britain was losing interest.

<!-- S32b -->
The ship itself couldn't have stopped
an invasion.
What mattered was what
withdrawing it seemed to say.

<!-- S33 -->
In 1982, Argentina invaded.

<!-- S34 -->
Britain had a powerful navy.
Argentina's own inquiry later found
that the junta had thought a British
military response almost impossible.

<!-- S35 -->
Deterrence needs two things.
The power to strike back,
and an opponent convinced you will.
Both sides are judging that
with incomplete information.
Sometimes, in a matter of minutes.

<!-- S36 -->
In September 1983,
a Soviet early-warning system
reported a missile launched
from the United States.
Then four more.

<!-- S37 -->
The officer on duty, Stanislav Petrov,
had to judge whether the warning was real.
He reported it as a false alarm.

<!-- S38 -->
He was right.
A satellite had mistaken sunlight
off high clouds for rocket engines.

<!-- S39 -->
A machine built to remove all doubt
had got it wrong.
It took a human's doubt to say so.

<!-- S40 -->
That leaves countries
walking a narrow line.
Strong enough that attacking them
looks irrational.
Not so threatening that a neighbour
decides to strike first.

<!-- S41 -->
From the outside, defensive
and offensive preparations
can look almost exactly the same.

<!-- S42 -->
After the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962,
the two superpowers built something new.

<!-- S43 -->
Not a weapon.
A direct line
between Washington and Moscow.
During the crisis, one Soviet letter
took nearly twelve hours
to reach the White House.
In 1963, the hotline went live.

<!-- S44 -->
It wasn't the famous red telephone.
It was a teleprinter.
It meant the two sides could talk
before anyone had to shoot.

<!-- S45 -->
So, does preparing for war keep the peace?

<!-- S46 -->
Sometimes, yes.
A country that can defend itself
is harder to bully, and harder to invade.

<!-- S47 -->
Weapons don't create peace on their own.
They also create fear, arms races,
and room for catastrophic mistakes.

<!-- S48 -->
Deterrence only works when the other side
believes the threat, understands it,
and doesn't panic.

<!-- S49 -->
The best deterrent may be a weapon
that is never fired.
And the proof that it worked
may be nothing happening at all.

<!-- S50 -->
Do you want peace?

<!-- S50b -->
Then prepare for war.

<!-- S51 -->
But perhaps...
prepare even harder for peace.
