# What If 9/11 Never Happened?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine waking up on the morning
of September 11,
2001.
You get up.
You go to work.
You turn on the television.
And nothing happened.

<!-- S02 -->
No planes crashed
into the World Trade Center.
No plane hit the Pentagon.
No plane crashed in Pennsylvania.

<!-- S03 -->
New York looks completely normal.
It is just another Tuesday.
At first, almost nothing seems different.

<!-- S04 -->
But now imagine something much harder.
Fast-forward 25 years.
It is 2026.

<!-- S05 -->
You are living in a world
where September 11
never happened.
What would that world look like?

<!-- S06 -->
And more importantly.
How much of our world today
exists because of something
that happened that morning?

<!-- S07 -->
Let's start with the obvious.
There would probably be no invasion
of Afghanistan in October
2001.

<!-- S08 -->
The Taliban would still control
most of the country.
Osama bin Laden would still be wanted
by the United States.

<!-- S09 -->
But the enormous military campaign
that followed would probably never happen
in the same form.

<!-- S10 -->
Thousands of American soldiers
would never be sent there.
And the war would have taken
a very different human toll.

<!-- S11 -->
But even here, we have to be careful.
History doesn't come with an undo button.
The United States already had reasons
to be concerned about terrorism.

<!-- S12 -->
Al-Qaeda already existed.
The Taliban already sheltered bin Laden.
And the Middle East was already
politically unstable.

<!-- S13 -->
So removing September 11
doesn't mean terrorism disappears.
It means one particular chain of events
never begins.

<!-- S13b -->
And that distinction
is extremely important.

<!-- S14 -->
Now it gets harder.
In 2003,
the United States invaded Iraq.
Officially, the invasion
was not a response
to September 11.

<!-- S15 -->
Iraq was not responsible
for the attacks.
But politically, that morning
completely changed the environment
in which the decision was made.

<!-- S16 -->
The American public had just experienced
the largest terrorist attack
in the country's history.

<!-- S17 -->
The government was operating
under a new sense of threat.
And the idea that another
catastrophic attack
could happen at any moment
was no longer hypothetical.

<!-- S18 -->
Without September 11,
would George Bush
still have invaded Iraq?

<!-- S18b -->
Maybe.
Maybe not.

<!-- S19 -->
There were already arguments
for regime change in Iraq.
There were already concerns
about Saddam Hussein.

<!-- S20 -->
There were already people in Washington
advocating military action.
But the political landscape
would have been radically different.

<!-- S21 -->
And that means one of the biggest
geopolitical events of this century
becomes uncertain.
No invasion?
Perhaps no occupation.
Perhaps no insurgency
in the same form.

<!-- S22 -->
Perhaps no chain of events
that eventually helped create
the environment in which
Islamic State emerged.

<!-- S22b -->
But notice the wording.
Perhaps.

<!-- S23 -->
We are not watching
an alternate universe through a window.
We are making an educated guess.
And history becomes much harder
the further we go.

<!-- S24 -->
So let's bring the thought experiment
closer to home.
Imagine going to an airport
in 2026,
without that morning
ever having happened.

<!-- S25 -->
You arrive at the terminal.
You check in.
And airport security
is still annoying.

<!-- S25b -->
But probably not this annoying.

<!-- S26 -->
Before that morning,
security procedures were very different.
Knives and other objects
that are now prohibited
could sometimes be taken
through security.

<!-- S27 -->
Cockpit doors were not designed
around the assumption that passengers
might try to take over the aircraft.

<!-- S28 -->
And the enormous security infrastructure
we now take for granted
had not yet become part
of everyday life.

<!-- S28b -->
The Transportation
Security Administration
did not even exist.

<!-- S29 -->
Today, we simply accept all of this.
Take out your laptop.
Put your liquids
into a tiny plastic bag.
Walk through a scanner.
And wait.

<!-- S30 -->
And for 25 years,
you took off your shoes.
Until, in 2025,
that rule quietly ended.
Because the scanners got better.
Not because the fear did.

<!-- S31 -->
It feels normal.
But it isn't inevitable.
It is a response to history.
And this is where the consequences
become much bigger.

<!-- S32 -->
After September 11,
the United States dramatically expanded
its ability to collect
and analyse information.

<!-- S33 -->
The Patriot Act gave the government
new powers.
Intelligence agencies
received greater resources.
Mass surveillance became a central issue
in national security.

<!-- S34 -->
And eventually, Edward Snowden revealed
just how extensive
some of these programmes had become.
But here is the interesting question.
Would mass surveillance
have happened anyway?

<!-- S35 -->
Probably to some degree.
The technology was already developing.
The internet was growing rapidly.
Computers were becoming more powerful.

<!-- S36 -->
Governments were already interested
in communications intelligence.
So perhaps the surveillance state
would still have grown.

<!-- S37 -->
But maybe more slowly.
Maybe under different laws.
Maybe with stronger restrictions.
Maybe the public
would have reacted differently.

<!-- S38 -->
Again, we don't know.
And this uncertainty is the real problem
with our experiment.

<!-- S39 -->
Because now things become
almost impossible to predict.
Remove September 11.
Remove the invasion of Afghanistan.
Possibly remove the Iraq War.

<!-- S40 -->
And you have changed
the political history of the Middle East.
But that doesn't mean you get peace.

<!-- S41 -->
Iran is still there.
Saudi Arabia is still there.
Israel and Palestine
still have their conflict.
Syria still has its internal problems.

<!-- S42 -->
Saddam Hussein is still ruling Iraq.
And governments still have
their own interests.
There would still be wars.
Still be terrorism.
Still be revolutions.
Still be political disasters.

<!-- S43 -->
History doesn't become peaceful
because one disaster disappears.
It simply becomes a different history.

<!-- S44 -->
So would 2026 be better?
Maybe.
Maybe not.
There are things
we can reasonably expect to be different.
But there is no reliable way
to calculate the entire alternate world.

<!-- S45 -->
And perhaps that tells us
something important about our own world.
We often look at history
as if it was inevitable.
As if the present
was always waiting for us.
But it wasn't.

<!-- S46 -->
The world we live in is the result
of billions of decisions, accidents,
mistakes, inventions, deaths,
coincidences
and completely unpredictable events.

<!-- S47 -->
Change just one of them,
and the present might look
completely different.

<!-- S48 -->
And there is one way
to think about this
that we have not used yet.
Not through governments.
Not through wars.
Through individual lives.

<!-- S49 -->
Nearly 3,000 people
who died on September 11
would still be alive.
Their children would grow up
with parents.
Their parents would grow old
with their children.

<!-- S50 -->
Some people would marry.
Some would have children.
Some would start businesses.
Some would become grandparents.

<!-- S51 -->
There would be entire families
in 2026
that simply do not exist
in our timeline.

<!-- S52 -->
And there is something almost impossible
to comprehend about that.
A single morning
didn't just change history.
It changed millions
of private histories.

<!-- S53 -->
People met different people.
Moved to different cities.
Joined the military.
Changed careers.
Lost the people they loved.

<!-- S54 -->
They made decisions
they would never otherwise have made.
And those decisions
affected other people.
And those people affected others.

<!-- S55 -->
A historical event
doesn't only create headlines.
It creates an enormous tree
of consequences.

<!-- S56 -->
So let's go back to the beginning.
September 10,
2001.

<!-- S57 -->
Imagine that day.
People went to work.
Planes flew across the United States.
People complained about traffic.

<!-- S58 -->
Someone was late for a meeting.
Someone bought a coffee.
Someone made plans for the weekend.
Nobody knew that history
was about to split in two.

<!-- S59 -->
Except in our version of history,
it doesn't.
September 11 arrives.
Nothing happens.

<!-- S59b -->
And the strange thing is,
nobody notices.

<!-- S60 -->
There is no breaking news.
No emergency broadcast.
No moment when everyone remembers
exactly where they were.
The day simply passes.

<!-- S61 -->
And 25 years later,
people live in a world
shaped by events that never happened.

<!-- S62 -->
They probably don't spend much time
wondering about them.
Because you don't miss a history
you never lived.

<!-- S63 -->
And maybe that is
the most unsettling thing about history.
The world we know
is not the only world
that could have existed.

<!-- S63b -->
It is simply the one
we happened to get.

<!-- S64 -->
And somewhere in the enormous space
of all the possible histories,
there are countless versions
of our world
that were only one event away.

<!-- S65 -->
Including one
where September 11,
2001,
was just another Tuesday.
