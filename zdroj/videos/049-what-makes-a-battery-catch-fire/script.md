<!-- S01 -->
Another battery caught fire this week.
A power bank on a plane.
A scooter left charging in a stairwell.
Sometimes an entire building.

<!-- S02 -->
And the headline is always the same.
Lithium-ion batteries are dangerous.

<!-- S03 -->
There is something odd about that.
Because there is one in your pocket.
One in your laptop.
Probably one parked outside.

<!-- S04 -->
Billions of them, sitting in drawers
and bags and walls,
doing nothing dramatic whatsoever.

<!-- S05 -->
So which is it?
Did we quietly hand a dangerous technology
to almost everybody on Earth?

<!-- S06 -->
Here is the short answer.
A lithium cell carries its own fuel.
The flammable part isn't in the room.
It is sealed inside the battery.

<!-- S07 -->
That is why a battery fire
is so hard to smother,
and why it can start again
an hour after you think it is out.

<!-- S08 -->
But the short answer is the boring one.
Almost every one of those billions
never does anything of the sort.

<!-- S09 -->
So the question is narrower than the headline.
What has to go wrong first?

<!-- S10 -->
It helps to remember how new this is.
Sony sold the first commercial
lithium-ion cell in 1991.

<!-- S11 -->
Lithium is the lightest metal there is,
so the chemistry stores a great deal
of energy for very little weight.

<!-- S12 -->
And then it got cheap.
Between 2010 and 2023,
the average price of a battery pack
fell by almost ninety percent.

<!-- S13 -->
Light and cheap is a powerful combination.
It is why one technology can sit
inside a phone and underneath a car.

<!-- S14 -->
From the outside, a phone battery
is deeply boring.
A flat silver rectangle.

<!-- S15 -->
Inside, it is not boring at all.

<!-- S16 -->
There is a positive electrode.
A negative electrode.
And a liquid electrolyte between them.

<!-- S17 -->
Charging pushes lithium ions
through that liquid,
from one electrode to the other.
Using the phone lets them drift back.

<!-- S18 -->
The electrons cannot take that route.
So they go the long way round...
through your phone.
That is the part we actually wanted.

<!-- S19 -->
And in the middle sits the component
nobody ever thinks about.

<!-- S20 -->
The separator.
A plastic film that holds
the two electrodes apart.

<!-- S21 -->
In a modern cell it is typically
under twenty micrometres thick.
A human hair is around seventy.

<!-- S22 -->
So that is the design.
A great deal of energy.
A flammable liquid.
And a film keeping the halves apart.

<!-- S23 -->
Enormously useful.
Not very forgiving.

<!-- S24 -->
Batteries do not usually burn
of their own accord.
Something has to go wrong first.

<!-- S25 -->
Crushed. Overheated. Overcharged.
Or simply built badly
on a Tuesday afternoon.

<!-- S26 -->
The worst case is an internal short.
The two sides meet somewhere
they were never meant to meet.

<!-- S27 -->
And now the energy of the whole cell
pours through one tiny spot.
That spot gets hot.

<!-- S28 -->
And here is the awkward part.
The separator is plastic.
Plastic does not do well when heated.

<!-- S29 -->
Heat it enough and it softens,
shrinks away from the hot spot,
and gives way.
So the hole gets bigger.

<!-- S30 -->
A bigger hole carries more current.
More current makes more heat.
More heat opens a bigger hole.

<!-- S31 -->
Meanwhile the heat is starting
chemical reactions in the cell,
and those reactions give off heat
of their own.

<!-- S32 -->
Heat makes reactions.
Reactions make heat.
Nothing in that loop slows it down.

<!-- S33 -->
It has a name.
Thermal runaway.

<!-- S34 -->
The battery stops being a power supply
and becomes a heater
that is feeding itself.

<!-- S35 -->
The electrolyte boils.
Pressure builds.
The cell splits open, and what comes out
is hot and flammable.

<!-- S36 -->
And in some chemistries,
the breaking electrode
gives off oxygen as it goes.

<!-- S37 -->
Which is the part people find
genuinely unnerving.
In those cells, the fire
has brought some of its own air.

<!-- S38 -->
Smothering it does very little.
Firefighters mostly use water to cool
what is around it,
and wait for it to finish.

<!-- S39 -->
Because cells are rarely alone.
In a bike or a car they sit
shoulder to shoulder, in packs.

<!-- S40 -->
And a cell in thermal runaway
is a very hot lump of metal
pressed against the next one along.

<!-- S41 -->
That is the difference between
a phone that has a bad afternoon
and a fire that takes the stairwell with it.

where the next question takes us,

<!-- S42 -->
So. Are they dangerous?
Yes.

<!-- S43 -->
And that answer is almost useless.

<!-- S44 -->
A chainsaw is dangerous.
A gas hob is dangerous.
The question was never
whether a thing can hurt you.

<!-- S45 -->
It is how often it does.

<!-- S46 -->
And that is where headlines
quietly mislead us.

<!-- S47 -->
Here is what rare actually looks like.
In 2016, Samsung recalled
two and a half million phones
because they were catching fire.

<!-- S48 -->
The recall cost Samsung
more than five billion dollars.

<!-- S49 -->
The number of incidents reported
in America was ninety-two,
out of roughly a million phones sold there.

<!-- S50 -->
That is about one in ten thousand.

<!-- S51 -->
One in ten thousand was enough
to end a flagship phone
and get it banned from aeroplanes.

<!-- S52 -->
Now hold that number in your head
and count the batteries around you.

<!-- S53 -->
When one fails on an aeroplane,
or in a block of flats,
everybody hears about it.

<!-- S54 -->
The ones that charged quietly
on a bedside table last night
are not news.

<!-- S55 -->
We see the failures.
We never see the denominator.

<!-- S56 -->
Which turns the question
into a better one.
Not are lithium batteries safe.
Safe enough for what?

<!-- S57 -->
A phone battery holds
about a dozen watt-hours.
An electric bike, around five hundred.

<!-- S58 -->
An electric car carries
tens of thousands.
The same family of chemistry,
entirely different consequences.

<!-- S59 -->
And the cell is not the whole battery.
A proper pack has electronics
watching it the entire time.

<!-- S60 -->
Voltage, current, temperature.
And the authority to cut the battery off
when the numbers stop making sense.

<!-- S61 -->
So a battery is really a system.
Cells, sensors, software,
a case, and the charger you plug it into.

<!-- S62 -->
Which means any one of them
can ruin it.

<!-- S63 -->
A flaw in the separator.
A speck of metal in an electrode.
A bad weld.
A protection circuit left out
to save a few pence.

<!-- S64 -->
And none of that is visible.
The cheap pack and the careful pack
look identical, right up until
one of them doesn't.

<!-- S65 -->
Which is where New York
becomes the interesting example.

<!-- S66 -->
In 2023, the city recorded
two hundred and sixty-eight fires
started by these batteries.
Eighteen people died.

<!-- S67 -->
Most of it traced back to one place.
Cheap replacement packs
for electric bikes and scooters,
often rebuilt from salvaged cells.

<!-- S68 -->
So the city did something
distinctly unglamorous.
From that September, selling a pack
that had not been certified
became illegal.

<!-- S69 -->
The timing is striking.
By 2025 the annual death toll
had fallen from eighteen to one.

<!-- S70 -->
The chemistry had not changed.
What had changed was which batteries
were reaching the market.

<!-- S71 -->
So why not simply make them safe?

<!-- S72 -->
Because we are asking
for everything at once.
Small, light, cheap, powerful,
quick to charge and long-lived.

<!-- S73 -->
And the more energy we pack
into a small space,
the more there is to release
when something finally goes wrong.

<!-- S74 -->
That trade-off is not
an engineering failure.
It is the entire point of the technology.

<!-- S75 -->
So what comes next is mostly
more of the same, done better.
Better separators, better cooling,
better ways to catch a flaw
before it leaves the factory.

<!-- S76 -->
Some chemistries already trade
energy for calm.
Lithium iron phosphate stores less
per kilogram and is much harder
to push into runaway.

<!-- S77 -->
Which is why it keeps turning up
where weight matters least.
A battery bolted to the ground
does not care what it weighs.

<!-- S78 -->
Solid-state batteries get the headlines.
Swap the flammable liquid for a solid,
and the part that burns
is simply not there.

<!-- S79 -->
In principle.
Those two words are doing
an enormous amount of work.

<!-- S80 -->
So we end up back at the paradox
we started with.

<!-- S81 -->
Batteries did not become dangerous.
They became everywhere.

<!-- S82 -->
We found a cheap, portable way
to keep an extraordinary amount
of energy sitting perfectly still.

<!-- S83 -->
The hard part was never storing it.
It is persuading it to stay put.
