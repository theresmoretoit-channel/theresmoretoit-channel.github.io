# What Would It Take to Prove God?

<!-- S01 -->
Imagine someone tells you:
“God exists.”

<!-- S02 -->
Okay.
So you ask:
“How do you know?”

<!-- S03 -->
And they give you an answer.
“Because I have proof.”

<!-- S04 -->
That sounds simple.
But what would that proof actually have to prove?

<!-- S05 -->
And what if someone else told you:
“God does not exist.”
Could they prove that?

<!-- S06 -->
Could we ever definitively prove either one?

<!-- S07 -->
Maybe the problem is deeper than the question of whether God exists.
Maybe we first need to figure out what it would even mean to prove it.

<!-- S08 -->
Suppose I tell you there is an apple inside this box.
We can open it.

<!-- S09 -->
If there is an apple inside, we can observe it.
We can photograph it, weigh it, and let other people examine it.
It's a claim we can test.

<!-- S10 -->
Mathematical proof works differently.
We don't have to observe anything.

<!-- S11 -->
We can start with definitions and rules, and logically demonstrate that a particular conclusion must follow.

<!-- S12 -->
Science works differently again.
A scientific theory makes predictions that can be compared with reality.

<!-- S13 -->
And ideally, there should be some possible observation that could show us that the theory is wrong.

<!-- S14 -->
And this is where the question of God becomes difficult.
Because there isn't just one concept of God.

<!-- S15 -->
Some people imagine a God who interacts with the physical world, answers prayers, and performs miracles.

<!-- S16 -->
Others understand God as something outside space and time.
And some philosophers describe God as the foundation of existence itself.

<!-- S17 -->
These are not exactly the same hypothesis.
So we can't simply say:
“Science hasn't found God.”

<!-- S18 -->
We first have to ask:
What exactly are we supposed to look for?

<!-- S19 -->
For centuries, philosophers have offered arguments for God's existence.

<!-- S20 -->
Take the cosmological argument.
It starts with two premises.
The universe began to exist.

<!-- S21 -->
And everything that begins to exist has a cause.
So what caused the universe?

<!-- S22 -->
Perhaps something outside the universe itself.
And that first cause could be called God.

<!-- S23 -->
But notice something important.

<!-- S24 -->
Even if we accept that the universe has a first cause, it doesn't automatically follow that this cause is a personal, all-powerful, perfectly good God.
That's another assumption.

<!-- S25 -->
And the same problem appears in other arguments.
Take fine-tuning.

<!-- S26 -->
Some physical constants have values that allow stars, chemistry, planets, and eventually life to exist.
Why?

<!-- S27 -->
One possibility is that it's simply the result of chance.

<!-- S28 -->
Another is that there are enormous numbers of universes with different physical parameters, and we happen to live in one that permits observers.

<!-- S29 -->
Or perhaps a deeper law we haven't discovered yet makes these values unavoidable.

<!-- S30 -->
And another possibility is that the universe was designed.

<!-- S31 -->
But again:
The observation itself doesn't say:
“God did this.”
That's an interpretation of the observation.

<!-- S32 -->
That doesn't necessarily make the philosophical argument worthless.
It simply means that an argument is not the same thing as an empirical proof.

<!-- S33 -->
Now we're getting closer to something we could potentially test.

<!-- S34 -->
Suppose someone says:
“God healed a person who had an incurable disease.”

<!-- S35 -->
We can examine the medical records.
We can look for alternative explanations.

<!-- S36 -->
We can investigate whether similar cases occur without prayer.
We can compare evidence from different cases.

<!-- S37 -->
If we see something we can't explain, all we can conclude is:
We don't know what caused it.

<!-- S38 -->
History is full of things people once considered mysterious that later received natural explanations.

<!-- S39 -->
But the reverse is also important.
If science gives us a natural explanation for something, that doesn't prove that God doesn't exist.

<!-- S40 -->
“There's a natural explanation”
is not the same statement as
“God doesn't exist.”

<!-- S41 -->
Maybe this should be easier.
We just need to find something that makes God's existence impossible.

<!-- S42 -->
But again:
Which God?

<!-- S43 -->
Suppose someone claims that God is all-powerful and perfectly good.
Now we encounter the problem of evil.

<!-- S44 -->
The world contains enormous amounts of suffering.
Wars.
Diseases.
Earthquakes.
Children dying from illnesses.

<!-- S45 -->
If an all-powerful and perfectly good God exists, why does this happen?

<!-- S46 -->
This is a serious philosophical problem.
A believer can propose different explanations.
Free will.
The possibility that suffering is necessary for some greater good.

<!-- S47 -->
The limitations of human knowledge.
Or the possibility that our understanding of what is good is fundamentally different from God's.

<!-- S48 -->
We might find these explanations unconvincing.
We might think they fail to solve the problem.

<!-- S49 -->
But if we want to be precise, we have to distinguish between:
“This explanation doesn't convince me”
and
“This explanation is logically impossible.”

<!-- S50 -->
Those are very different claims.

<!-- S51 -->
There is another interesting problem.
Suppose God exists.

<!-- S52 -->
And suppose God does not want his existence to be completely obvious.

<!-- S53 -->
Then perhaps God could create a world where there are reasons to believe...
but never conclusive proof.

<!-- S54 -->
And this leads to the problem of divine hiddenness.

<!-- S55 -->
If there is a God who wants humans to know him, why is it so unclear whether he exists?

<!-- S56 -->
Why are there people who genuinely search for God and remain unconvinced?

<!-- S57 -->
And why are there so many different religions, making different claims about what God wants?

<!-- S58 -->
These are legitimate questions.
They may simply mean that a particular concept of God has difficulty explaining the world we observe.

<!-- S59 -->
What if we define God in a way that makes him impossible to observe?

<!-- S60 -->
Imagine a being that:
doesn't exist in space,
doesn't exist in time,
doesn't obey physical laws,
leaves no measurable traces,
and whose existence cannot possibly be disproved by any observation.

<!-- S61 -->
Can we scientifically search for such a being?
Not really.

<!-- S62 -->
But there's an even more interesting question.

<!-- S63 -->
If no possible observation could ever show that such a being doesn't exist...
what would it mean to claim that it does exist?

<!-- S64 -->
This is similar to a famous thought experiment by Carl Sagan.

<!-- S65 -->
Imagine I tell you:
“There is an invisible dragon in my garage.”

<!-- S66 -->
You can't see it.
You can't hear it.
You can't weigh it.
It leaves no footprints.

<!-- S67 -->
And whenever you bring in a scientific instrument, the dragon somehow avoids detection.

<!-- S68 -->
How could you prove that the dragon isn't there?
You couldn't.

<!-- S69 -->
But that doesn't mean the dragon is there.

<!-- S70 -->
And this illustrates a very important principle of reasoning:
The inability to disprove a claim is not evidence that the claim is true.

<!-- S71 -->
Maybe there are three different questions.

<!-- S72 -->
First:
Can we prove that God exists?

<!-- S73 -->
For particular concepts of God, we can construct philosophical arguments, historical arguments, or claims based on alleged empirical evidence.

<!-- S74 -->
But we don't have a universal, indisputable proof of God's existence.

<!-- S75 -->
Second:
Can we prove that God doesn't exist?
That can be just as difficult.

<!-- S76 -->
If God is defined in a way that is compatible with every possible observation, there is no experiment that could definitively rule him out.

<!-- S77 -->
And third:
Can we even create a test that would decide the question?

<!-- S78 -->
And here, everything depends on how God is defined.

<!-- S79 -->
If God interacts with the physical world in specific, predictable ways, some claims about God may be testable.

<!-- S80 -->
But if God is defined as something fundamentally beyond any possible observation, science has no way to test the hypothesis.

<!-- S81 -->
And this is where people often make a mistake.

<!-- S82 -->
An atheist might say:
“You don't have proof, therefore God doesn't exist.”

<!-- S83 -->
A believer might respond:
“You can't prove that God doesn't exist, therefore God does exist.”

<!-- S84 -->
Neither argument works by itself.

<!-- S85 -->
The first would be wrong if it simply treated the absence of evidence as proof of absence.

<!-- S86 -->
But if a particular kind of God should produce evidence that we would reasonably expect to find —
and that evidence consistently isn't there —
then its absence can be evidence against that hypothesis.

<!-- S87 -->
The important point is that this still doesn't automatically prove that God does not exist.

<!-- S88 -->
The second makes the opposite mistake: treating the absence of disproof as evidence of existence.

<!-- S89 -->
It is perfectly possible to say:
“I don't have sufficient reason to believe this.”
while also saying:
“I don't have proof that it is impossible.”

<!-- S90 -->
There is no contradiction.
It's simply an acknowledgment of the limits of what we know.

<!-- S91 -->
At the beginning, we wanted a simple answer.

<!-- S92 -->
Does God exist?
Yes.
Or no.

<!-- S93 -->
But perhaps the evidence doesn't let us give such a simple answer.

<!-- S94 -->
We can study the universe.
We can study the human brain.
We can investigate alleged miracles.

<!-- S95 -->
We can analyse philosophical arguments.
We can compare different religions.

<!-- S96 -->
And we can ask what we would actually expect to observe if a particular kind of God existed.

<!-- S97 -->
But eventually, we reach a boundary between claims that can be tested and claims that depend on philosophical interpretation or personal belief.

<!-- S98 -->
And perhaps the most intellectually honest answer isn't:
“God exists.”
Or:
“God doesn't exist.”

<!-- S99 -->
It's this:
It depends on what we mean by God —
and whether we've defined the claim in a way that reality can actually confirm or disprove.

<!-- S100 -->
Because sometimes the hardest part isn't finding the answer.

<!-- S101 -->
It's figuring out whether the question can have an answer at all.
