# Why Don't Elephants Get Cancer?

<!-- S01 -->
You have around thirty trillion cells.

Every single one of them contains DNA.

And every time one of those cells divides,
there is a chance
that something goes wrong.

<!-- S02 -->
A mutation.

Most mutations are harmless.

Some are repaired.

Some are simply irrelevant.

<!-- S03 -->
But occasionally, a mutation gives a cell
the ability to grow when it shouldn't.

To ignore the body's instructions.

To keep dividing.

And eventually, to become cancer.

<!-- S04 -->
Now here's the strange part.

You might expect that the biggest animals,
with the most cells
and the longest lifespans,
would have the most cancer.

But they don't.

<!-- S05 -->
And one of the most fascinating examples
is standing right in front of us.

An elephant.

<!-- S06 -->
An African elephant
can weigh several tonnes
and can live for many decades.

Compared with a human,
it has an enormous number of cells.

<!-- S07 -->
And every one of those cells
is another potential starting point
for cancer.

<!-- S08 -->
So if every cell had roughly
the same probability
of becoming cancerous,
elephants should be
walking cancer factories.

<!-- S09 -->
But observations across mammals
don't show the simple relationship
we might expect
between body size, lifespan,
and cancer mortality.

<!-- S10 -->
This is known as Peto's paradox.

And it raises a remarkable question.

<!-- S11 -->
How did evolution allow huge animals
to live for so long
without cancer becoming
an inevitable consequence
of having so many cells?

<!-- S12 -->
Elephants appear to have evolved
several layers of protection.

And one of the most famous
involves something called TP53.

<!-- S13 -->
But before we talk about TP53,
we need to understand
what a gene actually is.

<!-- S14 -->
When people hear the word gene,
they often imagine a tiny piece of DNA
with a label on it.

<!-- S15 -->
But a gene isn't really a little object
sitting inside the cell
with a job description attached.

<!-- S16 -->
DNA is a huge molecular
information system.

And a gene is a particular region
of that DNA,
containing information the cell can use
to produce a functional product.

<!-- S17 -->
Often, that product is a protein.

And proteins are the machines of the cell.

They build things.

Transport things.

Send signals.

<!-- S18 -->
So here's a useful simplification.

DNA is the information.

A gene is a specific instruction
encoded in that information.

<!-- S19 -->
A protein is often
the molecular machine
produced from that instruction.

And this distinction becomes
extremely important
when we talk about TP53.

<!-- S20 -->
Because TP53 is the gene.

And p53 is the protein produced from it.

They are not the same thing.

<!-- S21 -->
Inside your cells
is a protein called p53.

It is one of the most important
tumour-suppressing proteins we know.

<!-- S22 -->
You can think of it as one of
the cell's security systems.

Imagine a factory.

<!-- S23 -->
If one of the machines
starts behaving dangerously,
someone has to stop the production line.

That's roughly what p53 can help do.

<!-- S24 -->
When a cell experiences serious stress,
including DNA damage,
the p53 pathway can become activated.

<!-- S25 -->
p53 can then switch on genes
that stop the cell cycle.

Genes that give the cell time
to repair its DNA.

Or, if the damage is too severe,
genes that trigger apoptosis.

<!-- S26 -->
Apoptosis is essentially
programmed cell death.

The cell deliberately dismantles itself.

<!-- S27 -->
Not because the organism has failed.

Quite the opposite.

It's a sacrifice.

<!-- S28 -->
One damaged cell dies
so that the organism
doesn't have to deal with a tumour later.

<!-- S29 -->
Now imagine what happens
when this security system stops working.

A cell acquires mutations.

<!-- S30 -->
Normally, damage-control systems
should detect the problem.

But suppose the cell also loses
an important tumour-suppressor mechanism.

<!-- S31 -->
Now the cell can continue dividing.

More divisions create more mutations.

Some of those mutations
make the cell even better at surviving.

<!-- S32 -->
Eventually, you can get
a population of cells
that no longer behaves
like normal tissue.

That's cancer.

<!-- S33 -->
And TP53 is so important
that mutations affecting it occur
in a huge fraction of human cancers.

<!-- S34 -->
The National Cancer Institute estimates
that roughly half of human cancers
have TP53 mutations or deletions.

So p53 is one of the central guardians
against cancer in our cells.

<!-- S35 -->
So how many TP53 genes do humans have?

Here's where the story gets interesting.

<!-- S36 -->
A typical human cell
has two copies of TP53.

One inherited from your mother.

One from your father.

<!-- S37 -->
So when people say that elephants have
twenty copies of TP53
while humans have one,
that's misleading.

<!-- S38 -->
Humans have one TP53 gene location,
but normally two copies,
or two alleles.

<!-- S39 -->
African elephants, on the other hand,
have a remarkable expansion
of the TP53 family.

<!-- S40 -->
The original studies identified
about twenty TP53-related copies
in the elephant genome.

<!-- S41 -->
That includes the normal TP53 gene
plus numerous retrogenes.

Additional copies derived
from an RNA-based copying process.

<!-- S42 -->
And some of these
elephant TP53-related sequences
are transcriptionally active.

<!-- S43 -->
More importantly,
elephant cells show a striking response
to DNA damage.

They are much more likely
than normal human cells
to undergo apoptosis
after serious DNA damage.

<!-- S44 -->
In one experimental study,
elephant lymphocytes showed
substantially higher rates of apoptosis
following DNA damage
than human lymphocytes.

<!-- S45 -->
But there's a catch.

A very important catch.

<!-- S46 -->
For years,
the popular explanation
was essentially this.

Elephants are huge.

Huge animals have more cells.

More cells should mean more cancer.

<!-- S47 -->
So evolution gave elephants
twenty copies of TP53.

Problem solved.

It's a great story.

But biology is rarely that convenient.

<!-- S48 -->
Some of the extra elephant TP53 copies
are retrogenes,
and many of them are truncated.

They are not simply
twenty identical copies
of the normal human TP53 gene.

<!-- S49 -->
A twenty twenty-two evolutionary analysis
argued something different.

Many of these retrogenes
may have accumulated largely through
duplication and genetic drift.

<!-- S50 -->
Not because natural selection
specifically favoured every one of them
as an anti-cancer defence.

<!-- S51 -->
That doesn't mean
the elephant TP53 system is irrelevant.

Quite the opposite.

<!-- S52 -->
Other experiments indicate
that some elephant TP53-related sequences
can influence
the DNA-damage response.

<!-- S53 -->
But it means we shouldn't say
that scientists proved
that twenty copies of TP53
are the reason
elephants don't get cancer.

They haven't.

<!-- S54 -->
The real story is more interesting.

We are still figuring out
exactly what evolution did.

<!-- S55 -->
And then there's the zombie gene.

Elephants have another
fascinating trick.

It's called LIF6.

<!-- S56 -->
At some point
in the evolutionary history of elephants,
a copy of an old gene
that had become inactive
appears to have been repurposed.

<!-- S57 -->
When p53 detects
serious cellular damage,
LIF6 can be activated.

The resulting protein
can contribute to mitochondrial damage
and push the cell toward apoptosis.

<!-- S58 -->
In other words, p53 says one thing.

This cell is damaged.

And LIF6 turns that warning
into something stronger.

Then kill the cell.

<!-- S59 -->
It's a beautiful example of evolution
reusing old genetic material.

A gene that had effectively
become dormant
was recruited
into a new biological system.

<!-- S60 -->
But elephants don't have
one magic anti-cancer gene.

And this is probably
the most important lesson.

<!-- S61 -->
Elephants aren't cancer-proof.

They can and do develop cancer.

<!-- S62 -->
The famous estimate
from one large study
was that cancer accounted
for around four point eight per cent
of recorded elephant mortality.

<!-- S63 -->
That is far below the fraction
recorded for humans
in the dataset used by that study.

But we should be careful
with those numbers.

<!-- S64 -->
Comparing cancer rates
between wild animals and humans
is extremely difficult.

Animals aren't monitored the same way.

Their deaths aren't always investigated.

<!-- S65 -->
Some tumours are never detected.

And veterinary records are nowhere near
as complete as human medical records.

<!-- S66 -->
So the claim that elephants
never get cancer
is simply wrong.

<!-- S67 -->
The scientifically interesting statement
is that elephants appear
to experience less cancer
than we would expect
from their enormous size
and long lifespan.

<!-- S68 -->
And that is the real Peto's paradox.

<!-- S69 -->
So why don't humans
simply copy the elephant?

This is where things
get really exciting.

<!-- S70 -->
Imagine we could somehow
make human cells
behave more like elephant cells.

More sensitive to DNA damage.

More willing to stop dividing.

<!-- S71 -->
More willing to destroy themselves
when something goes badly wrong.

Wouldn't that make us
resistant to cancer?

Maybe.

<!-- S72 -->
But biology immediately
throws another problem at us.

Your body doesn't only need

It also needs to keep
healthy cells alive.

<!-- S73 -->
Every day, cells are damaged.

Every day, cells are replaced.

Your tissues need to regenerate.

Your immune system needs to function.

<!-- S74 -->
Your wounds need to heal.

Your organs need to maintain themselves.

If we simply made p53
permanently hyperactive,
we could destroy
perfectly useful cells.

<!-- S75 -->
So cancer protection is a balancing act.

Too little control, and you get cancer.

<!-- S76 -->
Too much control,

impaired regeneration,

Evolution has to find the sweet spot.

<!-- S77 -->
And humans are already trying

This isn't just theoretical biology.

<!-- S78 -->
Scientists are actively studying

One strategy is to restore

<!-- S79 -->
Another is to manipulate proteins

One particularly important regulator

<!-- S80 -->
MDM2 normally helps
keep p53 under control.

how the human and elephant versions
of this regulator differ.

<!-- S81 -->
Recent experiments have even tested
elephant MDM2 variants
in human cellular models.

<!-- S82 -->
So the elephant isn't necessarily going

anti-cancer gene to humanity.

<!-- S83 -->
Instead, it may give scientists
something more valuable.

New ideas about how
to control the system.

<!-- S84 -->
And here's where we can return

Elephants aren't the only animals

unusual cancer defences.

<!-- S85 -->
Different long-lived

Some change cell-cycle control.

<!-- S86 -->
Some appear to have unusual mechanisms

Some have evolved changes

<!-- S87 -->
Evolution has been running

There isn't necessarily
one universal solution to cancer.

<!-- S88 -->
And perhaps this is
the deepest part of the story.

Cancer isn't really an external enemy.

It's a consequence of something
fundamental about life.

<!-- S89 -->
Your body is made of trillions of cells.

Those cells have to divide.

They have to copy
enormous amounts of DNA.

<!-- S90 -->
They have to survive.

They have to respond
to their environment.

And every time a cell reproduces,
evolution is happening

<!-- S91 -->
Cancer is essentially evolution

<!-- S92 -->
A cell acquires a mutation.

Then another.

A cell that grows faster
gets an advantage.

<!-- S93 -->
A cell that ignores a signal

A cell that avoids death
gets an advantage.

<!-- S94 -->
Our bodies therefore have to fight

occurring inside us.

So could we become

The future is likely

<!-- S97 -->

<!-- S98 -->
And perhaps the most exciting thing
