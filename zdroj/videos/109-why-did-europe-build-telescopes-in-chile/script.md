# Why Is Europe Building Telescopes in Chile?
**Script v0.7**

<!-- S01 -->
In a desert in northern Chile,
Europe is building the largest
optical telescope ever made.

<!-- S02 -->
Its main mirror will be
thirty-nine metres across.
Stand it on its edge,
and it would be taller
than a twelve-storey building.

<!-- S03 -->
And it won't be alone.
Europe already runs some of
the world's most powerful telescopes there,
more than ten thousand kilometres
from home.

<!-- S04 -->
The organisation behind them is called
the European Southern Observatory.
And that one word, Southern,
turns out to matter more than it seems.

<!-- S05 -->
But first, the obvious problem.
The hardest part of a telescope's job
isn't the distance.
It's the last few kilometres.

<!-- S06 -->
Light from a distant galaxy
can travel for billions of years
with almost nothing in its way.
Then, in the last thousandth of a second,
it passes through our atmosphere.

<!-- S07 -->
And the air is never still.
Pockets of warmer and cooler air
bend the light slightly differently,
many times a second.
That's why stars twinkle.

<!-- S08 -->
Twinkling looks lovely.
But for an astronomer,
it smears a sharp point of light
into a trembling blur.

<!-- S09 -->
Then there's water vapour,
which swallows whole bands
of infrared light.
And clouds and city lights,
which can end a night before it starts.

<!-- S10 -->
So before you ask
how big a telescope should be,
you have to ask where on Earth
the sky gets in the way the least.

<!-- S11 -->
One of the best answers
is the Atacama Desert in Chile,
among the driest places on Earth.

<!-- S12 -->
The air above it holds
very little water vapour.
Clouds are rare. Cities are far away.
And the mountains lift the telescopes
above a large part of the atmosphere.

<!-- S13 -->
ESO's Paranal Observatory stands
two thousand six hundred metres up.
More than a quarter of the atmosphere
is already below it.

<!-- S13b -->
By the way, if you like videos
like this, consider subscribing.

It really helps the channel.

<!-- S14 -->
So that explains the desert.
But it doesn't explain the word
in ESO's name. Southern.

<!-- S15 -->
There are excellent observing sites
much closer to home,
on the Canary Islands, for example.
European astronomers use them too.

<!-- S16 -->
But they share one limitation
with every observatory in Europe.
They're in the northern hemisphere.

<!-- S17 -->
Earth is a ball.
From the north, part of the sky
is always hidden below the horizon.
And the hidden part
isn't just any part.

<!-- S18 -->
The centre of our own galaxy
lies in the southern sky.
From central Europe,
it never gets far above the horizon,
where its light has to cross the most air.

<!-- S19 -->
From Paranal,
it passes almost straight overhead.

<!-- S20 -->
The Magellanic Clouds,
two small companion galaxies of our own,
can't be seen from Europe at all.
Neither can Alpha Centauri,
the nearest star system to the Sun.

<!-- S21 -->
And in the 1950s,
when ESO was first being planned,
every telescope in the world
with a mirror two metres or wider
stood in the northern hemisphere.
The southern sky had nothing as big.

<!-- S22 -->
That's what the word Southern means.
Chile gives Europe a part of the sky
it can't see from home,
through some of the clearest air
on the planet.

<!-- S23 -->
And it paid off.
For decades, telescopes in Chile
followed stars racing around
the centre of our galaxy.

<!-- S24 -->
They were orbiting something
dark and enormously heavy:
a black hole about four million times
the mass of the Sun.

<!-- S25 -->
That work won a share
of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

<!-- S26 -->
But why is this Europe, together,
and not France or Germany on its own?

<!-- S27 -->
Because telescopes like these
cost more than most countries
would ever spend alone.
So they share the cost, the engineers,
and the observing time.

<!-- S28 -->
ESO was founded in 1962
by five countries:
Belgium, France, Germany,
the Netherlands and Sweden.
Today it has sixteen member states,
with Chile as the host.

<!-- S29 -->
Their first observatory was La Silla,
chosen in 1964.
Then came Paranal,
and the Very Large Telescope.

<!-- S30 -->
Despite the name,
the VLT isn't one telescope.
It's four.
Each with a mirror
eight point two metres across.

<!-- S31 -->
So what does all of this actually see?
Here are two examples,
and they work in very different ways.

<!-- S32 -->
The first is an instrument
on the VLT, called ESPRESSO.
It doesn't take pictures of planets.
It watches stars move.

<!-- S33 -->
When a planet orbits a star,
the star isn't perfectly still.
Both circle a shared centre of mass,
so the star sways back and forth.

<!-- S34 -->
As it moves towards us,
its light shifts slightly bluer.
As it moves away, slightly redder.
ESPRESSO measures that shift.

<!-- S35 -->
Take Proxima Centauri,
the nearest star to the Sun.
From Europe, it never rises.

<!-- S36 -->
It has a planet, Proxima b,
discovered in 2016 with HARPS at La Silla,
and confirmed in 2020 by ESPRESSO.

<!-- S37 -->
The planet swings its star
towards us and away every eleven days,
at about walking pace.

<!-- S38 -->
That's walking pace,
measured on a star
forty trillion kilometres away.

<!-- S39 -->
And ESPRESSO is built to measure even smaller motions,
down to about ten centimetres a second.
That's roughly how fast
the Earth makes the Sun wobble.

<!-- S40 -->
The second example
doesn't use visible light at all.
ALMA, run by ESO and its partners,
is sixty-six antennas
on a plateau five thousand metres up.

<!-- S41 -->
It picks up millimetre
and submillimetre waves,
from cold gas and dust:
the raw material of stars and planets.

<!-- S42 -->
And here, the dry air isn't a bonus.
It's essential.
Water vapour absorbs exactly these waves.
On a damp site,
ALMA would be nearly blind.

<!-- S43 -->
It has photographed young stars
surrounded by discs of dust,
with gaps where new planets
seem to be forming.

<!-- S44 -->
So why build an even bigger one?

<!-- S45 -->
Because the most interesting things
are often the faintest.
Small planets next to bright stars.
The very first galaxies.

<!-- S46 -->
The Extremely Large Telescope,
the ELT, will collect nearly
twenty times as much light
as one of the VLT's mirrors.

<!-- S47 -->
A mirror that size
can't be made in one piece.
It's made of
seven hundred and ninety-eight
hexagonal segments,
working together as one surface.

<!-- S48 -->
And it takes on the twinkling directly.
More than five thousand actuators
reshape one of its mirrors,
up to a thousand times a second,
to undo the blur of the atmosphere.

<!-- S49 -->
It isn't finished yet.
ESO plans first light in March 2029,
and the first science in December 2030.

<!-- S50 -->
At this point, you might reasonably ask:
why bother?
We have telescopes in space.
No air, no clouds, no twinkling.

<!-- S51 -->
Hubble has been up there since 1990,
James Webb since 2021.
And Webb, working mostly in infrared,
doesn't have to look through water vapour,
or the glow of warm air.

<!-- S52 -->
But Webb's mirror is
six and a half metres across,
and even that had to be folded up
to fit inside its rocket.
A thirty-nine-metre mirror
won't fit inside any rocket we have.

<!-- S53 -->
And on the ground,
upgrading an instrument takes a truck,
not a space mission.

<!-- S54 -->
So this isn't a contest of mirror size.
Webb sees what the ground can't.
And where the air lets light through,
the ELT will collect far more of it,
and see finer detail.
Different questions,
often about the very same objects.

<!-- S55 -->
And at the end of August 2026,
another one joined them.
NASA's Roman Space Telescope.

<!-- S56 -->
It's heading for a point called L2,
one and a half million kilometres
beyond the Earth,
where it will circle that point,
just like Webb.

<!-- S57 -->
Its mirror is the same size as Hubble's.
But it sees about a hundred times
more sky in a single shot.
Its job isn't to look closer.
It's to look wider.

<!-- S58 -->
Roman might spot something strange
across a huge patch of sky.
Webb might take a closer look.
And the ELT might split its light
into a spectrum.

<!-- S59 -->
So why is Europe building
its giant eyes in the Chilean desert?

<!-- S60 -->
Because the sky it wanted
was in the south.
And because Chile had
one of the clearest views of it.

<!-- S61 -->
There's no single best telescope.
Space or ground, big or wide,
each one sees a different part
of the same story.

<!-- S62 -->
And all of them depend on the same thing.
A tiny amount of light,
that crossed the universe
for billions of years...

<!-- S63 -->
only to land on a mirror,
built by people,
in a desert
on the other side of the world.
