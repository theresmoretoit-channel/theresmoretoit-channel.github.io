Why Are There Suddenly So Many Trans People?

13 min2026-10-21

This video starts with the basics: what transgender actually means, why gender identity and sexual orientation are different things, and what gender dysphoria can feel like. It looks at what transition is, why it isn't one particular operation, and whether being transgender is simply a fashion.

Then it turns to the numbers. Researchers estimate that in the United States, about one in a hundred people aged thirteen and over describe themselves as transgender. Among young adults aged eighteen to twenty-four, the share is roughly ten times higher than among people aged sixty-five and over. What can those numbers tell us, and what can't they?

The full narration of the video.

0:00Have you ever met a trans person?

Have you ever met a transgender person? Maybe you have. Maybe you think you haven't. But how do you know? A transgender person doesn't necessarily look a certain way. They don't have a particular personality, voice, job, or sexual orientation. Some are very obviously transgender. Others aren't. Some talk openly about it. Others never tell anyone. And some people may spend their entire lives hiding it. So perhaps the first question isn't: "Why are there suddenly so many transgender people?" Perhaps the first question should be: who are transgender people, anyway? Let's take a closer look.

0:43What does transgender actually mean?

What does transgender actually mean? When a baby is born, doctors usually assign a sex based primarily on the baby's physical characteristics. For most people, the sex they were assigned at birth and their gender identity correspond. These people are commonly described as cisgender. For some people, however, they don't. Their gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth. These people may describe themselves as transgender, or simply trans. And this distinction is important. Being transgender is not the same thing as being gay, lesbian, bisexual, or heterosexual.

Gender identity and sexual orientation are different things. One is about who you are. The other is about who you're attracted to. A trans woman is a woman who was assigned male at birth. In other words, she was born with a typically male body. A trans man is the other way round: a man who was assigned female at birth. A trans woman can be heterosexual, lesbian, bisexual, or asexual. A trans man can be heterosexual, gay, bisexual, or asexual. There is no particular sexual orientation that comes with being transgender.

2:09What does it feel like?

But what does it actually feel like? This is where things become difficult to explain. Because if you've never experienced it yourself, you may simply have no idea what it feels like. Some transgender people experience something called gender dysphoria. It can involve significant distress related to a mismatch between a person's experienced gender and aspects of their body, or the way they are perceived by other people. And this is not simply: "I don't like my body." Many people don't like parts of their bodies. Someone can hate their nose, their weight, their hair, or their height without experiencing gender dysphoria. Gender dysphoria is about something more fundamental.

For some people, particular parts of their body can feel profoundly wrong, because they don't fit the way they experience themselves. Being called by the wrong name or pronoun can also be deeply distressing for some people. Looking in the mirror can be difficult. Puberty can be especially difficult, because the body begins developing in a direction that feels increasingly incompatible with how the person understands themselves. And this experience can be difficult for someone who has never had it to imagine. Imagine waking up every morning and seeing something about your body that doesn't merely look unpleasant to you, but feels fundamentally out of place.

That analogy will never perfectly reproduce another person's experience. But it may help explain why simply saying: "Just accept your body." doesn't necessarily solve the problem. And importantly, not every transgender person experiences gender dysphoria in exactly the same way, or to the same degree. Some people experience severe distress. Others describe a more persistent sense that something about their gender doesn't fit. And some may identify as transgender without seeking medical treatment at all.

4:10There isn't one kind of transgender person

There isn't one kind of transgender person. This is another thing that's easy to forget. Transgender people aren't a uniform group. They can be young or old. They can be religious or not. They can be married, with children. They can be politically conservative or progressive. Some want hormones. Some don't. Some want surgery. Some don't. Some change their name and appearance. Some don't. And some simply want to live their lives without making their gender identity a major part of their public identity. There is no single transgender experience.

4:52What is transition?

What is transition? The word transition can also be misleading. People sometimes imagine that transition means one particular operation. It doesn't. Transition can involve several different things. There can be social transition. This can mean changing someone's name, pronouns, clothing, or appearance. There can be legal or administrative transition. This can mean changing the name or gender marker on official documents, where the law allows it. And there can be medical transition. This can include hormone therapy and, for some people, surgical procedures.

But these are not three mandatory steps. A transgender person does not have to complete all of them. There isn't a checklist that someone has to complete before their identity becomes "real." And some people decide not to transition at all. They keep these feelings to themselves. They may get married. They may have children. They may build a career. They may appear to everyone around them to be living a completely ordinary life. So the person sitting next to you at work, or someone you've known for twenty years, may have a story you know nothing about.

And that's not necessarily because they're deceiving you. It may simply be because their gender history is private.

6:18Is being transgender a fashion?

Is being transgender a fashion? It's certainly true that young people influence each other. It's also true that social media can spread ideas very quickly. And people can sometimes misinterpret their own feelings. But that's different from saying that being transgender is simply a fashion trend. For some people, these feelings appear to be persistent and deeply significant. Some spend years trying to suppress them. Some risk rejection from their families. Some risk losing relationships, jobs, or social connections. Some eventually decide that living openly is worth those risks.

That doesn't mean that every person who questions their gender is necessarily transgender. It doesn't mean that every decision about transition is automatically correct. And it doesn't mean that nobody ever changes their mind. It means that the simple explanation, "They're just doing it because it's fashionable," is probably not enough to explain the whole phenomenon.

7:21Human biology isn't a standard system

Human biology is not a perfectly standardised system. People are born with countless variations in their bodies, brains, development, and senses. Some are obvious from birth. Others aren't discovered until childhood, puberty, or adulthood. So why would we assume that gender identity must be an exception? Why would every person's brain and body have to fit together in exactly the same way? Perhaps the more surprising assumption is not that some people experience their gender differently. Perhaps it's the assumption that this could never happen at all.

Human beings are more complicated than that.

8:04Are there really more trans people now?

Are there really more transgender people now? It certainly looks that way. In the United States, researchers estimate that about one in a hundred people aged thirteen and over describe themselves as transgender, a group that also includes many non-binary people. But that number hides an enormous difference between ages. Among people aged sixty-five and over, it's about one in four hundred. Among young adults aged eighteen to twenty-four, it's nearly three in a hundred. That's roughly ten times as many. So are young people simply being born differently? These numbers don't show that.

But there is an important distinction between how many people exist and how many people are visible. A young person today can search the internet, find other people describing almost exactly the same feelings, and discover words for experiences they couldn't previously explain. Someone born in the 1950s grew up without any of that. The numbers alone can't tell us how much of the difference comes from visibility, and how much from something else. And this isn't the first time people like this have become visible.

9:19Were trans people around in the past?

Were transgender people around in the past? In 1919, a doctor called Magnus Hirschfeld opened an institute in Berlin. It studied human sexuality and gender, including people who felt that the sex they had been assigned didn't fit them. It built up a large library on sex and gender. In 1930, a Danish painter called Lili Elbe came to the institute. Over the following sixteen months, she underwent four operations. She became one of the best-known early cases of what we would now call gender-affirming surgery. She died in 1931, most likely from complications after the last one.

Two years later, in May 1933, the institute was raided. A few days later, much of its library was burned on a public square. Almost a hundred years ago, there was already a place where people like this could be seen. And then, quite suddenly, there wasn't.

Berlin wasn't unique. Many cultures have had their own ways of understanding people who didn't fit conventional gender roles. In South Asia, there are the Hijra, communities with centuries of history. In North America, some Indigenous nations have roles now often described by the modern term Two-Spirit. But people whose lives didn't fit conventional gender expectations are certainly not a new invention of the internet.

10:51Why do we notice them so much now?

So why do we notice them so much now? Partly because, in some parts of the world, people can now talk openly about experiences that used to stay hidden. So when more people say, "I am transgender," that doesn't automatically mean that more people became transgender. It can also mean that more people are visible. And visibility is not the same thing as existence.

11:18Maybe the real change isn't in the people

Maybe the real change isn't in the people. For centuries, many people who didn't fit society's expectations hid it. They lived in a role expected of them, or simply never told anyone. Today, in at least some parts of the world, there are more possibilities. But that doesn't mean everyone takes them. And it certainly doesn't mean that being transgender is easy.

11:45Next: how do you know you're trans?

In fact, the next question may be even more interesting. How does a person actually figure out that they are transgender? Because some people seem to know very early. Others spend years wondering what is wrong with them. Some are convinced that they're simply failing to be the kind of man or woman society expects them to be. And some only begin to understand themselves decades later. So if being transgender isn't simply a fashion choice… how does someone actually discover it? That's what we'll look at next.
