Internet Explorer 6 — The Browser That Wouldn't Die

14 min2026-09-12

Not because anyone liked it. Because something they needed depended on it.

This is part three, and it asks the harder question: what happens when obsolete software becomes infrastructure? The answer isn't Windows XP — Internet Explorer 7 and 8 both ran on XP, so the operating system was never what held those machines back. It was one internal application nobody fully understood any more.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Part three

Internet Explorer 6 was dead. At least, Microsoft said it was. The company had spent years telling people to upgrade. Firefox was better. Chrome was better. Internet Explorer 7 was better. Internet Explorer 8 was better. There was a countdown on Microsoft's own website. Percent by percent. Country by country. And yet, in 2010, millions of computers were still running Internet Explorer 6. Not because their owners liked it. Not because Microsoft wanted them to. Because something they needed depended on it. And that is how a browser stops being software. It becomes infrastructure.

The strange thing is, Microsoft knew. They were trying to kill Internet Explorer 6. And at the same time, they were selling companies a way to keep it alive. This is part three. Part one was how one browser became that powerful. Part two was what it did to the people building for it. Both are linked below. This one is the harder question. What happens when obsolete software becomes infrastructure?

1:19January 2010

Start in January, 2010. Internet Explorer 6 is nine years old. Firefox is six. Chrome is two. And more than one browser in ten is still Internet Explorer 6.

1:33Somebody inside Google

Then Google discovers that somebody is inside its own network. Not a defaced website. Source code. Employee accounts. The Gmail accounts of Chinese human rights activists. And at least twenty other companies had been opened the same way. The way in was a flaw in Internet Explorer that nobody had fixed, because nobody knew it was there. The attack used it against Internet Explorer 6.

2:02Operation Aurora

The researchers who took it apart gave the operation a name. Aurora. And Microsoft broke its own monthly schedule to ship the fix, three weeks early. And then something happened that had not happened before. Governments told their citizens to stop using a browser. Germany's federal security office said use something else. France said the same. Firefox. Chrome. Anything but this. In Germany, people downloaded three hundred thousand copies of Firefox over a single weekend.

2:36Google stops waiting

A few weeks later, Google stopped waiting. From the first of March, Google Docs and Google Sites would no longer work properly in Internet Explorer 6. So by the spring of 2010, nobody was arguing anymore. The browser was a security problem. Two governments said so. Google said so. And it still didn't go away. That is the part worth explaining.

3:04The usual explanation is wrong

The usual explanation is Windows XP. Old operating system, old browser, nobody upgraded, end of story. That explanation is wrong. Internet Explorer 7 came out in 2006. It ran on Windows XP. Internet Explorer 8 came out in 2009. It ran on Windows XP too. That is where it stopped. Internet Explorer 9, in 2011, would not run on Windows XP at all. Microsoft even pushed Internet Explorer 7 out through Windows Update, as a critical update. If you had automatic updates on, it came to you. So from 2006 onwards, a machine running Windows XP could have a newer browser. That is four years before anybody had heard of Aurora.

Which means that every machine still on Internet Explorer 6 in 2010 was not stuck there by its operating system.

4:06The OS wasn't the reason

The operating system wasn't the reason. The application was.

Somewhere inside that company there was a piece of software that only worked in Internet Explorer 6.

4:20What that application was

Not a website. An application. It ran on ActiveX, so it could reach the file system, a printer, a scanner, a database, a card reader. It had been written when Internet Explorer 6 was the only target anybody cared about. And it worked. That is the awkward part. It worked every morning, and people ran their day on it. Change the browser, and you are not upgrading software. You are betting that something nobody fully understands still behaves the same way.

Microsoft understood that better than anybody. Because when it shipped Internet Explorer 7 through Windows Update, its biggest customers panicked. Ten thousand machines. One automatic update. Every internal application that had ever assumed Internet Explorer 6.

5:13A tool to block its own browser

So Microsoft did something remarkable. It published a tool whose only purpose was to stop its own new browser from being installed. It was called the Blocker Toolkit. You ran it, and it wrote a setting that told Windows Update never to offer Internet Explorer 7 again. There was no expiry date. Then Internet Explorer 8 came out, and Microsoft published a blocker for that. Then one for nine. Then one for eleven. That is worth saying twice. The company that wanted you to upgrade also shipped the switch for not upgrading. Four times.

And it wasn't an accident. It was demand. Microsoft was not fighting its customers' inertia. It was supplying it.

6:01When the machine itself dies

But blocking an update only buys time. Sooner or later the machine itself dies, and somebody has to buy a new one.

And after 2009, a new machine meant Windows 7. Windows 7 would not run Internet Explorer 6. Not with a blocker. Not with a setting. The browser was simply gone. So the company had a choice. Stay on an operating system that Microsoft would stop patching in a few years. Or replace the application. Microsoft offered a third option. And this is the part that is hard to believe until you see the product page.

6:44Windows XP inside Windows 7

It was called Microsoft Enterprise Desktop Virtualization. Large companies bought it as part of a subscription. What it did was run a complete copy of Windows XP inside Windows 7. Invisibly. In the background. And inside that hidden Windows XP, it ran Internet Explorer 6. The second version went further. An administrator gave it a list of addresses. Anything on that list opened in the old browser, inside the hidden machine. The employee clicked a link and a window appeared. They never knew they had just started an entire operating system to look at one page.

7:26Killing it and selling it

So look at what Microsoft was doing in the same year. Telling the world that Internet Explorer 6 had to go. And selling its largest customers a product whose whole purpose was keeping it running. Microsoft was killing Internet Explorer 6. And Microsoft was also selling you a way to keep it alive.

And that isn't hypocrisy. That is what a dependency looks like from the inside. Even the company that made the thing can't simply take it away.

7:59A setting inside the new one

It didn't stop there, either. A few years later, Internet Explorer 11 shipped with compatibility modes for older sites, one of them behaving like Internet Explorer 8. It was there for the old internal applications and the ActiveX controls inside them. Microsoft's own documentation said many of those would simply keep working. The old browser was no longer a different machine. It was a setting inside the new one.

Up to here this sounds like a story about companies. Bad software. Cautious managers. Budgets.

8:39South Korea

In South Korea it was a story about a country. In the late nineties, Korean regulations required online payments to use a particular kind of encryption. No browser supported it. The only way to add it was ActiveX. And the only browser with ActiveX was Internet Explorer. So banking ran on ActiveX. Government sites ran on ActiveX. Shopping ran on ActiveX. Anything involving money or identity arrived with a plug-in you had to install first. A whole country's online life now depended on one company's browser. Not by accident. By regulation.

The requirement was lifted in 2014. The websites kept the plug-ins anyway. That is the difference between a bad decision and infrastructure. A bad decision can be reversed. Infrastructure has to be replaced.

9:40China and America

And you could see the same thing on a map. In January 2012, Internet Explorer 6 finally fell below one percent in the United States. In China, that same winter, a quarter of all browsers were still Internet Explorer 6. In the spring before, it had been a third. Same web. Same browser. Same winter. Because the question was never which browser is better. It was what somebody had already built on top of it.

10:11Eight hundred thousand machines

And some of what had been built on it was not small. In September 2013, eighty-five percent of the computers in Britain's national health service were still running Windows XP. Out of roughly eight hundred thousand. Support for Windows XP was going to end in April 2014. So the British government signed a deal with Microsoft. Five and a half million pounds for twelve more months of security updates across the whole public sector. Not to upgrade anything. To buy one more year of not upgrading. That is what a dependency costs once somebody puts a number on it.

And the reason that year was worth five and a half million pounds is the same everywhere. Before you can replace the old thing, you have to find out what it does. All of it. Including the parts nobody wrote down and nobody remembers. You don't just inherit the software. You inherit every assumption anybody ever made about it. And after ten years nobody knows what all of them are.

11:22How it actually ended

So how did it actually end? Not with a deadline. In December 2011, Microsoft announced that it would stop asking. From then on, Windows would upgrade Internet Explorer by itself, to the newest version the machine could run. On Windows XP, that was eight. You could still say no. Most people didn't, because most people never knew there was a question. That winter, Internet Explorer 6 went under one percent in the United States. Support for Windows XP ended in April 2014, exactly as promised. Two years after the browser had already lost.

And even that line didn't hold. Three weeks later a serious flaw turned up in Internet Explorer, and Microsoft patched it on Windows XP anyway. Internet Explorer 6 was still getting security updates somewhere in the world until January 2016. Fourteen years after it shipped.

12:24Switched off in 2022

Internet Explorer itself was finally switched off in June 2022. And Microsoft Edge still carries a mode that draws pages the way Internet Explorer did, because too many companies still need it. Microsoft has promised to keep that mode working until at least 2029. Twenty-eight years after the browser shipped. Nineteen years after it was declared dead.

12:53Browser, platform, dependency

So that is the answer to the question we started with. A browser becomes a platform. A platform becomes a dependency. A dependency becomes infrastructure. Infrastructure becomes technical debt. And technical debt becomes somebody's job.

Usually not the job of the person who built it. And once enough things depend on something, being better is no longer enough to replace it. Everybody else has to move as well. Which is the strangest thing about technology. Sometimes the thing that wins isn't the thing that survives. Sometimes it becomes the thing everyone has to survive.

13:39When the world stopped depending

Internet Explorer 6 didn't die when Microsoft stopped making it. It died when enough of the world stopped depending on it. And that took a very long time.
