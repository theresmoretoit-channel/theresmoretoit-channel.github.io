Your Body, and How Long It Lasts

Why Can't You Read Your Own Body's Logs?

11 min2026-11-06

This video follows the evidence: a builder in agony with a completely unharmed foot, a construction worker with a nail in his brain and a toothache, the rule Google's engineers use to decide what may wake them up, the alarm that can watch the wrong number, the alert that carries the wrong address, and the organ that can't feel pain in its own tissue.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Your body's error messages

Imagine your computer starts behaving strangely. A program freezes. Something crashes. What do you do? You open the logs. You check what happened before the crash. You look for the error message. And if you're lucky, you find the exact line responsible. Now imagine your own body doing the same thing. You wake up with a headache. You feel exhausted. Your stomach hurts. Something is clearly wrong, but the only message you get is this. Error. Something went wrong. Root cause: unknown. And the really strange part? It's your body. Surely, you're the system administrator. Your brain adjusts your heartbeat, regulates your temperature, and talks to your immune system.

Yet you can't open a terminal and ask what happened to me last night? So why can't you read your own logs?

0:57Two nails

In 1995, the British Medical Journal described a builder who had jumped down onto a nail. Fifteen centimetres long. It went straight through his boot. The smallest movement of that nail hurt so much that doctors gave him fentanyl and a sedative before pulling it out. Then they took off the boot. The nail had passed between his toes. His foot was completely unharmed.

Ten years later, in Colorado, a construction worker's nail gun backfired. He felt a heavy blow, and then a toothache. For six days, he took painkillers and iced his face. Then he went to a dentist, and an X-ray showed why. A ten-centimetre nail, through the roof of his mouth and into his brain. Surgeons took it out, and he was expected to recover fully. One man had no injury at all, and pain that needed a strong opioid. The other had a nail in his brain, and a toothache. So your body's error messages aren't just vague. They can be wrong in both directions. But that isn't because nothing is being recorded.

2:10Is anything being recorded?

One nerve, the vagus, connects your brain to your heart, your lungs and your gut. And by the usual estimate, around eighty percent of its fibres don't carry orders down. They carry reports up. Sensing your own insides even has a name. Interoception. Some of it reaches you. You feel hungry. You notice your heart racing. Most of it never does. You can test this right now. Without touching your wrist or your neck, try to count your heartbeats.

In the lab version of this test, most people count too few. And that's an organ thumping away inside your chest. Part of the reason may be capacity. Every day, your body replaces about three hundred and thirty billion cells, most of them blood cells. And one recent estimate puts human thought and action at about ten bits per second. By the same estimate, your senses take in around a billion. So even with every signal on a screen, you couldn't keep up. The terminal is too narrow. But capacity alone doesn't explain which few lines get through.

3:26Every page should be actionable

People who keep huge computer systems running have a rule about this. It's in Google's book on site reliability, in five words. Every page should be actionable. A page is an alert that wakes up an engineer. If nobody can do anything about it, it shouldn't wake anybody up. Now look at what your body pages you for. Hunger. Eat. Thirst. Drink. A full bladder. Find a bathroom. A hand on a hot stove. Move. In 1979, the pain researcher Patrick Wall argued that pain has more in common with hunger and thirst than with seeing or hearing. A demand for action, not a measurement.

Now look at what stays silent. High blood pressure usually has no symptoms. About a third of adults aged thirty to seventy-nine have it, and nearly half of them don't know. In one large American study, more than forty-five percent of first heart attacks were only discovered later, on an ECG. Whatever those people felt at the time, nobody took it for a heart attack. For nearly all of human history, nobody could do anything about their blood pressure. No action, so perhaps, no alert. That's a hypothesis. Nobody can rerun evolution to check it.

4:50The smoke detector

There's a second rule, and the physician Randolph Nesse named it after a smoke detector. A false alarm costs you some burnt toast. A missed one costs you the house. So defences like pain, fever and vomiting may be tuned to go off far too often. Seen that way, the builder's body wasn't malfunctioning. A nail through your boot is exactly when you want the alarm first and the facts later.

5:19The alarm that watches the wrong number

And sometimes, the alarm watches the wrong number. When you hold your breath, that desperate urge comes mainly from carbon dioxide building up, not from oxygen running out. So if your oxygen falls while carbon dioxide keeps leaving, that alarm barely goes off. Pilots are trained for this, because some people feel euphoric, and may not know they're in trouble. In 2005, Helios Flight 522 climbed with its cabin pressure falling. A warning horn sounded, and the crew seem to have taken it for a different warning. A ground engineer on the radio even asked about the pressurisation panel. The captain replied by asking where the cooling breakers were. Then the crew stopped answering. A hundred and twenty-one people died.

Now imagine that alarm hardly working. Around a thousand people worldwide have a condition once called Ondine's curse. Their automatic breathing barely responds to changes in oxygen and carbon dioxide. Some need a ventilator every time they fall asleep. Some need one all the time.

6:32The wrong address

And even when an alert does fire, it can carry the wrong address. A heart attack can show up as pain in your arm, your back, or your jaw. Something cold on the roof of your mouth hurts in your forehead. The best-known explanation is a shared line. Nerves from your organs and your skin meet on the same neurons in your spinal cord. Two machines behind one address. The brain sees a signal on that line, and blames the skin, because that's where signals usually come from. None of this means you should ignore it. Pain can be bad at saying where and why. New or unexplained chest pain is still a reason to get help, now.

7:14The organ that can't feel pain

Then there's the oddest gap of all. Brain tissue itself has no pain receptors. Surgeons can operate on awake patients once the scalp and the membranes are numb. That headache you woke up with isn't your brain hurting. It comes mostly from the tissues around it. The organ reading the alerts can't raise that one about itself.

7:39So is there any log at all?

So is there any log at all? There is. There are several… You've just never been shown them. Your blood keeps a partial record of the viruses you've met. In 2015, researchers built a test that checks a drop of blood for antibodies against two hundred and six species of virus. The average person showed about ten. Your hair grows about a centimetre a month, and it traps traces of a stress hormone, cortisol, as it grows. A few centimetres can hint at the last few months. Your DNA keeps a tally too. Smoking a pack a day for a year leaves an estimated hundred and fifty extra mutations in each lung cell.

And one log you can check yourself. A serious illness can briefly interrupt your nail growth, and leave a groove across the nail. It takes about six months to grow out.

8:32Who reads the logs?

So the logs exist. They just were never written for you. An antibody is there for your immune system. A hormone is there for the cells it reaches. The reader was never the part of you that's watching this video.

That's why we had to build the debugger on the outside. An ECG records your heart. An EEG records your brain. Blood tests, scanners, smartwatches. And each one sees only a part. A number can be perfectly accurate and still be misleading. A hundred beats a minute is perfectly normal after climbing stairs. Sitting quietly, it might mean something else.

9:17Back to where we started

So let's go back to where we started. Surely, you're the system administrator. You aren't. You never had root access.

What you call you is closer to one unprivileged process, with a tiny dashboard built to make you act, not to tell you what's going on. You can measure your body. You can study your own brain. But you have to do it with instruments, the way you'd study a stranger. And perhaps the strangest thing about being human is that the machine trying to understand itself is also the machine doing the understanding.

9:57A question for you

So here's a question for you. If you could see all of it, every marker, every day, in real time, would you actually want to? Let me know in the comments.
