How Does a Dictatorship Become a Democracy?

12 min2026-09-17

For most of history the answer was prison, exile or a wall. And yet it has happened — not once, but repeatedly, and usually without a revolution.

Taiwan spent 38 years under martial law, the longest in the world at the time. Tens of thousands were imprisoned and thousands were executed. In 2000 the party that had run the island since 1949 lost a presidential election, and handed over power. The tanks did not roll. Nobody announced that the result had been misinterpreted.

0:00Imagine you are a dictator

Imagine you are a dictator. You control the government. You control the army. You control the police. You control the newspapers. And one morning somebody walks in with an interesting suggestion. Why don't we hold free elections? So you ask the obvious question. And what happens if I lose?

0:23The whole problem in one line

That is the whole problem in one line. If losing an election costs you your position, your money, your freedom, possibly your life, why would you ever agree to one? And yet it has happened. More than once. One of the strangest examples is an island you have heard of for completely different reasons.

0:43Taiwan

Taiwan.

In 1949, the Chinese Communist Party won the civil war on the mainland, and the government of the Republic of China, led by the Kuomintang, retreated to Taiwan. If you know the usual story, you probably picture what came next. A small democratic island, safely out of reach of the mainland. Not quite.

1:09What the authoritarian state looked like

Taiwan was an authoritarian state. Martial law was declared in 1949, and it stayed in place for 38 years. Opposition was heavily restricted. The press was controlled. People suspected of political opposition could be imprisoned. In the years known as the White Terror, tens of thousands were imprisoned, and thousands were executed. This was not a democracy waiting patiently for its turn. It was a dictatorship.

1:42And then it started to change

And then it started to change. Taiwan industrialised. Factories, exports, wages. People got richer. Education expanded. Cities grew. A large middle class appeared, which had not really existed before. And alongside the money, something less visible was growing. People formed associations. Students protested. Journalists pushed at the edges of what the censors would allow. Opposition politicians organised, even though parties were banned.

2:16The party that was illegal

The government could still arrest them. It did. But a society is easy to control only while it stays simple, and this one was getting complicated. And then, in September 1986, the opposition did something that was straightforwardly illegal. They announced a new political party. Under martial law, that was enough to be arrested for. The government knew about the meeting. It had the names. And it did nothing.

2:49Martial law is lifted

The following year, Taiwan lifted martial law. The rules on parties and newspapers came off gradually after that. In 1996, Taiwan held its first direct presidential election. And then came the moment that actually matters.

3:06The year 2000

The year 2000. The Kuomintang had run Taiwan for half a century. And it lost. The opposition won the presidency. And the tanks did not roll.

3:22The tanks did not roll

The army did not overturn the count. Nobody announced that the result had been misinterpreted. The government simply changed hands. The old ruling party carried on existing. It became the opposition. And eight years later it won an election and came back. Sit with that for a moment. A party that had run an authoritarian state for decades discovered that it could lose power and survive it.

3:53So why did Taiwan do it?

So why did Taiwan do it? Did the rulers wake up one morning and become nice people? That seems unlikely. Does getting richer automatically make a country democratic? Hold that thought. Because Taiwan was not the only place where something like this happened.

4:11South Korea

Look at South Korea. For decades it was run by authoritarian governments. And for decades it was also doing the other thing. Industry, exports, schools, a huge urban middle class. And by 1987, the protests were enormous.

4:29The Olympics were coming

And there was another pressure in the room. Seoul was hosting the Olympic Games the following year. The world would be watching. The protests did not stop. And the ruling establishment accepted major reforms. The June 29 Declaration promised direct presidential elections. Notice the shape of that. It is not simply a dictator being overthrown. It is partly the people in power deciding that keeping the old system is becoming harder, and more dangerous, than changing it.

5:05Chile votes on Pinochet

Chile is stranger still. Augusto Pinochet's military government had written a constitution with a referendum built into it. In 1988, the country was asked whether Pinochet should continue as president. The regime expected the vote to hand it some legitimacy. The No side won. And the result was accepted. Elections followed in 1989, and Pinochet left the presidency in 1990. It was not clean, and it was not simple.

5:38What he kept

Pinochet remained commander-in-chief of the army for another eight years. And the political system still contained important remnants of the old regime. But the central fact stands. A dictator had put his continuation in office to a vote. And lost.

5:58Spain

Spain did yet another version of the same thing. Francisco Franco died in 1975, and Spain did not collapse into revolution. Instead, reformers inside the system began taking the system apart. Parties were legalised. Elections were held in 1977. A new constitution followed in 1978. Not a revolution. Not the destruction of the old political class. A transition.

6:38The pattern

So there is a pattern here, and it is not that dictators become democrats. It is that a regime can reach a point where its own leaders start asking a completely different question. Not how do we hold on forever. But how do we change this without destroying ourselves. Which brings us to the thing this whole video is really about.

7:01The exit problem

The exit problem.

Imagine two dictatorships. In the first one, the ruling party knows roughly what losing looks like. We would still be allowed to exist. We would keep our houses. We would keep our businesses. We could stand in the next election. We might even win it. Losing is unpleasant. It is not the end. Now the second dictatorship. If we lose, they take everything. Our families are prosecuted. Our allies go to prison. So do we. Possibly worse. Now ask which of those two groups is more likely to agree to a free election. It is not a hard question.

7:45Every reason to hold on

The second group has every reason to hold on, whatever that costs. Even if the country would be better off without them. Even if they would privately prefer a quiet way out. Their survival is now welded to the survival of the regime. And at that point, democracy stops being a reform. It becomes a gamble with their own lives on the table.

8:11China

Which may explain one of the strangest comparisons in modern politics. After Mao Zedong died, China changed enormously. Deng Xiaoping and the leaders after him opened up the economy. Markets expanded. The country became dramatically richer. Education expanded. An enormous middle class appeared. China became deeply connected to the world economy. In other words, China went through a transformation that, in other countries, had sometimes gone alongside political liberalisation. But here, it didn't.

The Communist Party kept its monopoly on national political power. Economic reform did not turn into political reform.

8:59Development is not destiny

Which tells us something worth being careful about. Economic development can create the conditions that make democracy possible. It does not make it inevitable.

9:13What actually matters

Institutions matter. Civil society matters. It matters whether opposition groups can organise, compete, and eventually take power without destroying the system they are replacing. And most of all, the people currently holding power need some reason to believe that losing will not destroy them. That is also why this is so hard to predict from the outside. Sometimes a regime is overthrown. Sometimes it collapses. Sometimes it loses a war.

9:44Taking it apart from the inside

And sometimes the people running the dictatorship quietly take parts of it apart themselves. Not because they became saints. Because the arithmetic changed. Which makes Taiwan's story more awkward than good democracy beats bad dictatorship. The dictatorship came first. The economic transformation happened under that dictatorship. The civil society that eventually demanded political freedom grew up inside it. And some of the people who took the authoritarian system apart were products of it.

10:19The paradox

That is the paradox. A dictatorship can create the conditions that make dictatorship hard to keep. And there is a deeper one underneath it. A dictator does not have to love democracy to allow it. He only has to believe that he can survive losing.

10:37The half nobody pictures

We usually imagine democracy beginning when ordinary people demand the right to choose. That is half of it. The other half is much quieter. For any of it to hold, the people who already have power have to accept something that sounds almost trivial. They might lose.

And when they do, nothing terrible has to happen. They go home. They form an opposition party. They criticise the new government.

11:08Losing is survivable

They campaign again. And sometimes they win again. The peaceful transfer of power works because losing is survivable. That is the trick. That is the whole trick.

So a regime that once jailed its political opponents ended up building a system in which those opponents could win. And the old rulers could accept it. No revolution. No civil war. A ballot box.

Not a dictatorship that was defeated. A dictatorship that turned into a place where losing was safer than refusing to change.
