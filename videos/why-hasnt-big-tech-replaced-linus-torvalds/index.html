Why Hasn't Big Tech Replaced Linus Torvalds?

8 min2026-11-05

This video follows the code: how many of the latest nineteen thousand changes Linus wrote himself, what happened when Google kept its own branch of the kernel for Android, how a change travels through thousands of maintainers before it reaches him, who actually pays his salary, what happened when he took a whole file system back out of Linux, and what the project did the one time he stepped away.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The man Big Tech can't fire

Imagine you run one of the biggest technology companies on Earth. Thousands of engineers. Billions of dollars. And one of the most important pieces of your infrastructure is controlled by a man who doesn't work for you. You can't fire him. You can't overrule him. When your engineers want to change that software, they send him their work, and wait for his answer. That man is Linus Torvalds. And the software is Linux. Google does this. So do Microsoft, Intel, Amazon and Meta. So why hasn't Big Tech replaced him?

0:39From a student's hobby to the world's servers

Linux began as a student's hobby in Helsinki in 1991. Today, it runs Android phones, cloud servers, and all five hundred of the world's fastest supercomputers. The latest release brought almost nineteen thousand changes, from about three thousand developers and more than two hundred companies. So how many of those changes did Linus write himself? Nine. And eight of them just changed the version number. In two weeks, he merged around two hundred and forty batches of changes, each one sent to him by someone he trusts. He's in charge because the code ends up with him.

And that's exactly the part a company like Google could simply walk away from.

1:27Make your own Linux

Linux is open source. Anyone can take the code and make their own version. It's called a fork, and you don't need Linus's permission. Google did exactly that. Not a whole new Linux. It took the kernel, the core that talks to the hardware, and kept its own branch of it for Android. In December 2009, Android's code was removed from the main kernel. Greg Kroah-Hartman, one of the senior maintainers, explained why: no one cared about the code, so it was removed. So Android's kernel drifted away. Chip makers and phone makers added their own changes on top.

By Google's own account, up to half the code in a phone's kernel could come from outside the main kernel. And every security fix found in Linux had to be carried over into each of those versions. Google's documentation calls that "prohibitively expensive". A new kernel release could take up to eighteen months to reach a phone. Two years after deleting Android's code, Greg put it back. His note said: "Turns out I was wrong, we want these in the tree." And since Android 12, phones that launch on a newer kernel must use one common kernel from Google, the Generic Kernel Image.

It's still Linux with Android's own changes on top. But it stays close to the main version, because that's where the fixes are.

2:56Easy to copy, expensive to keep

Making your own version is easy. Keeping it alive is the expensive part. That's why companies send their code to the main kernel. Not as a favour. Once it's in, everyone else helps look after it.

3:13How code reaches Linus

But Linus can't read nineteen thousand changes. Nobody could. Linux is split into areas. Networking. Memory. File systems. Graphics drivers. Each processor family. Each area has its own maintainers. The list of them has around three and a half thousand entries. A change goes first to the right maintainer. They review it, argue about it, ask for fixes, and collect what's ready. Then they send it up. At the top, more than a hundred of them send their collections to Linus. Linux is built everywhere. But it comes together in one place.

3:56Who pays Linus?

Now, the companies sending all that code aren't charities. These companies compete with each other every day. Would Google let Microsoft decide what goes into the software its servers run on? Linus doesn't work for any of them. So who does pay him? The Linux Foundation. And the Foundation isn't a charity standing outside the corporate world. It's an association of companies. Google, Microsoft, Intel, Meta, Amazon and others pay for its top membership, and each of them gets a seat on its board. The Foundation says it pays Linus so that he can stay independent.

That's a stated purpose, not a published guarantee. The Foundation hosts more than a thousand projects. The kernel gets about three percent of its budget. So the companies aren't putting one of their own at the top. They're supporting a place where they can work together, without any one of them in charge.

4:59The final word

But does Linus really have the final word? Here's what that looks like. In 2024, Linux got a new file system called bcachefs, a new way of storing files on a disk. Every new version has a rule. New features come in during the first two weeks. After that, only fixes. Its developer kept sending large changes after those two weeks. In August 2024, Linus wrote: "At some point, 'fix something' just turns into development. And this is that point." The arguments went on for another year. In 2025, Linus took bcachefs out of the kernel.

It still exists, as a separate add-on. Just not in the shared version. Linus has power because the people who send him code keep trusting him.

5:56When Linus is gone

So what happens when Linus is gone? We already know a little. In September 2018, Linus stepped away. He apologised for attacking people in his emails, and said he needed time to change how he behaved. Greg Kroah-Hartman, the same maintainer from the Android story, took over and finished the next version, 4.19. Linux kept going. A few weeks later, Linus came back. Since 2026, there's even a written plan. If Linus can't continue, the senior maintainers start talking within seventy-two hours about who takes over. It doesn't name a successor. It describes how to find one.

So Linux can survive without Linus. What it can't easily replace is the trust that put him at the centre in the first place.

6:51So why hasn't Big Tech replaced him?

So why hasn't Big Tech replaced Linus Torvalds? Because what they'd be replacing isn't a programmer. It's the one place where all their competing code comes together, looked after by someone none of them owns. Linus doesn't own the kingdom. Everyone just agrees that, for now, he's the right person to guard it.

7:14A question for you

So here's a question for you. When Linus does step down, should one person get the final word again, or should it be a group? Let me know in the comments.
