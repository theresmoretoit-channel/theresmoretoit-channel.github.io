What Happens When the World Thaws?

9 min2026-10-15

From Batagaika, a crater in Yakutia whose walls are still retreating, to an anthrax outbreak among reindeer on the Yamal Peninsula, from a tundra fire on Alaska's North Slope, in a place with no trace of fire for 5,000 years, to fires that smoulder under the snow all winter: what is actually frozen in the permafrost, and which part of it is the real danger.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A virus frozen for 48,000 years

In 2023, scientists took a lump of mud from 16 metres under a lake in Siberia. They thawed it out in a lab, and a virus inside it was still infectious. It had been frozen for more than 48,000 years. And it could still infect a living cell: an amoeba, not a human. The ground it came from is thawing. So what else is in there? Diseases that vanished long ago? Or something that could make the planet even warmer? Some of this is already happening. But the biggest danger in that ground may not be the one you're expecting.

0:39What permafrost is

The frozen ground is called permafrost: ground that stays at or below freezing for at least two years in a row. It lies under about 15 per cent of the land in the Northern Hemisphere. Siberia, Alaska, northern Canada, the ice-free edges of Greenland. And it isn't just ice. It's soil, rock and frozen water, packed with the remains of plants and animals. In places, it reaches down more than a kilometre. And some of it formed hundreds of thousands of years ago.

1:11Batagaika, a crater that keeps growing

Here's what happens when it thaws. This is Batagaika, in Yakutia. In the 1960s, it was a gully across a hillside stripped of its forest. Today, it's a crater almost two kilometres long. And its walls are still crumbling back, on average about ten metres a year. Without the ice holding it together, the ground simply slumps and collapses. And whatever was frozen inside meets air and water again.

1:48Could something down there infect us?

So let's start with the scary question. Could something in there make us sick? That virus from 2023 wasn't the first. In 2014, the same team in France revived one from northeast Siberia that was about 30,000 years old. By now, they've revived about a dozen. And every single one of them infects amoebas, not people. That's on purpose. Amoebas are the bait: they let you fish out ancient viruses without risking human ones. So yes, something frozen for tens of thousands of years can still infect a living cell. But an amoeba virus isn't a human disease.

2:32Anthrax on the Yamal Peninsula

For something that can kill people, we have to go to Yamal, a peninsula in Arctic Russia. Summer, 2016. After years of warming, capped by a heatwave, the ground had thawed deeper than usual. Then the reindeer started dying. Of anthrax. More than 2,000 of them. Thirty-six people were infected, and a 12-year-old boy died. Anthrax spores can survive in the soil for decades. The last outbreak in the area had been in 1941. The leading explanation: the thaw reached old infected carcasses, and the spores came back to the surface.

But it wasn't only the thaw. Vaccination had stopped in 2007, and the herds had grown huge. The thaw was the trigger, not the whole cause. Scientists do take this seriously. Nobody knows everything that's frozen in there. But there's no evidence that a wave of prehistoric diseases is about to come out of the Arctic.

3:37The bigger danger

The bigger danger isn't something waking up. It's something breaking down.

For thousands of years, Arctic plants grew every summer, pulled carbon dioxide out of the air, and died. In the cold, they barely rotted. Year after year, the carbon piled up and froze into the ground.

3:57How much carbon is down there?

So how much is down there? Let's compare it with something we already know. Since 1850, by burning coal, oil and gas, humanity has put about 500 billion tonnes of carbon into the air. The soils of the permafrost region hold about three times as much.

Around 1.5 trillion tonnes. More than the entire atmosphere holds today. More than half of it is frozen solid. The rest lies in soil that thaws only briefly each summer. Either way, it's the cold that keeps it there. It won't all come out. If emissions keep climbing, scientists estimate that perhaps 5 to 15 per cent of it could escape this century. That sounds small. But 10 per cent of that pile is more than a quarter of everything we've burned so far.

4:52Microbes and methane

When the ground thaws, ordinary soil microbes finally get to eat. Where there's plenty of oxygen, they turn that old carbon into carbon dioxide. In waterlogged ground, partly into methane. Methane traps about 80 times more heat than carbon dioxide over 20 years, though it breaks down in about a decade. Either way, the result is more heat. And more heat means more thawing, which releases more carbon. It's called the permafrost carbon feedback. It won't happen all at once. It's slow, uneven, and different from place to place.

But once that carbon is in the air, we can't freeze it back into the ground.

5:35Fire on the tundra

And there's a much faster way to get carbon out of the ground. Fire. In 2007, lightning set fire to the tundra of Alaska's North Slope. The Anaktuvuk River fire burned more than 1,000 square kilometres. In that spot, the lake sediments show no sign of fire for about 5,000 years. And that single fire released about as much carbon as the entire Arctic tundra used to absorb in a whole year. The fire also burned away the plants and peat that kept the ground insulated. Within seven years, a third of the burned area had started to sink.

And Arctic fires are getting bigger. In 2020, fires inside the Arctic Circle broke the record for emissions. Some of them don't even go out. They smoulder underground all winter and flare up again in spring. They're called zombie fires.

6:34Putting it all together

Now put it all together. The thaw, the microbes, the fires. For thousands of years, the Arctic tundra did one thing with carbon. It stored it. In 2024, the yearly Arctic report from NOAA concluded that this is no longer true. Once the fires are counted, the tundra now releases more carbon dioxide than it takes in. Only a little, for now. The forests further south still absorb more than they lose. But the ground that spent millennia pulling carbon out of the air has started giving it back.

For the people who live up there, this isn't a future problem. The ground under their homes is already moving.

7:13The ground under people's homes

By 2050, about 70 per cent of the roads, buildings and pipelines on permafrost will stand on ground that could thaw near the surface.

7:31What happens when the world thaws?

So what happens when the world thaws? Probably not a prehistoric plague. The bigger danger is a vast store of carbon, kept out of the atmosphere by the cold, leaking back into it. How much warming it will add is still uncertain. But uncertain doesn't mean small. The greatest danger in the ice isn't something waking up. It's something breaking down. And the less we warm the Arctic, the more of it stays frozen.
