Physics That Sounds Simple Until You Ask

Teleportation: What Would It Actually Take?

14 min2026-09-06

This video walks through each of them: the portal that removes the distance instead of moving you, the transporter that scans and rebuilds you, the machine that copies your mind, and the science-fiction trick of walking through walls. Along the way we work out how much information a human being actually is, why quantum mechanics makes the scan harder rather than easier, and why the receiving machine turns out to be the most advanced factory in the universe.

And then the part no engineering solves. If a perfect copy of you wakes up on Mars while the original is still standing on Earth, which one is you? Nobody can prove that the consciousness on the other side is the same consciousness that entered.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Step in, step out

Imagine that tomorrow, we invent teleportation. You step into a machine. A few seconds later, you step out on the other side of the planet. No airplane. No rocket. No road. Just… there. Sounds amazing. But what exactly happened to you? Did you travel? Were you copied? Were you destroyed and rebuilt? Or did we somehow bend space itself and simply make the distance disappear? Because "teleportation" is not actually one technology. There are several completely different ways it could work. And some of them are much more disturbing than others.

0:41The portal

Let's start with one of the simplest ideas. The Portal approach. You don't disassemble anything. You don't scan anything. You simply connect two locations. Imagine that space normally looks like this. And somehow, we create a shortcut. You walk through it. From your perspective, you are still the same physical object. You were not copied. You were not reconstructed. In fact, this is arguably not teleportation at all. It's more like removing the distance between two places. In physics, the closest familiar idea is a wormhole. A hypothetical shortcut through spacetime.

But there is a small problem. We don't know how to create a traversable wormhole. Or whether one could be kept open. General relativity allows some strange geometries of spacetime. But turning one of them into a practical doorway is an entirely different problem. And then there are some wonderfully inconvenient questions. What happens if the portal closes while you're halfway through? And perhaps most importantly. How much energy would it take to manipulate spacetime on this scale? So the Portal solution has a major advantage. You don't have to reconstruct the person.

But instead, you have to do something even more ridiculous. You have to manipulate spacetime itself.

2:12The transporter

Now let's try something completely different. The classic transporter. You enter the machine. The machine scans you. Your body is disassembled. The information is transmitted. And another machine reconstructs you. Scan. Data. Transmit. Rebuild. This sounds like something a sufficiently advanced computer could do. But immediately we run into the first gigantic problem. What exactly do we scan? A human body contains roughly seven octillion atoms. That's a seven followed by about twenty-seven zeros. And knowing where the atoms are may not be enough. We also need to know what those atoms are doing.

What molecules are present? What are the electrical states of the neurons? And how much of the quantum state matters? The more accurately we want to reconstruct the original person, the more information we need. And this leads to a rather unpleasant question. How do we make the scan?

3:18You cannot photograph a brain

A human being is not a statue. Especially the brain. The brain is constantly changing. Neurons are firing. Ions are moving. Membranes are changing their electrical potentials. So imagine trying to take a perfect snapshot of the brain. Not just its shape. Its physical state. At one exact moment. So perhaps we should stop everything first. Freeze the chemistry. Stop the brain. Take the measurement. And then reconstruct the person. Sounds reasonable. Except… How do you safely stop a human brain without destroying the very information you are trying to preserve?

We have now built a teleportation machine whose first step is this. "Please enter the machine so we can temporarily kill all biological activity in your brain." Not exactly great marketing.

4:18How much data is a person

And even if we solve that, there is another problem. How much data is a human being? It's tempting to say this. "Just count the atoms and store their coordinates." But that's not enough. The amount of information depends on the precision. Describe every position to millimetre accuracy, and that's one amount of data. Micrometre accuracy? Much more. Nanometre accuracy? Much more again. And if you imagine describing matter down to extremely tiny scales, the numbers become absurd. You might hear people go all the way down to the Planck length. But that is not a pixel size of the universe. Divide a body into cubes that size and you get a number with about a hundred and three zeros.

And a smaller pixel does not tell us what information belongs in it. We aren't taking a three-dimensional photograph. We're describing a physical system.

5:19The quantum problem

Quantum mechanics makes the situation even stranger. We cannot imagine that every particle has a definite list of properties that a scanner can read out. Measurement itself is part of quantum mechanics. And there is a fundamental restriction known as the no-cloning theorem. An unknown quantum state cannot simply be copied perfectly. Quantum teleportation does exist experimentally. But it doesn't teleport matter. It transfers a quantum state using entanglement and classical communication. And it does not leave you with two perfect copies. So real quantum teleportation is very different from a Star Trek transporter.

6:04Now build the human

Let's be generous. Let's assume we have solved the scanning problem. We now possess the complete blueprint. And we send it to another planet. Congratulations. We've solved teleportation. Right? No. We now have to build you.

The receiving machine needs matter. A lot of it. It needs the right elements. Carbon. Hydrogen. Oxygen. Nitrogen. And many others. But having the right atoms isn't enough. We need the right molecules. The right proteins. The right organs. And eventually… the right brain. It is not enough to manufacture seventy kilograms of human-shaped matter. We need to put the right molecules in the right places.

6:54Where does the matter come from

Notice what the machine cannot send. It sends information. It does not send atoms. So the receiving end needs its own warehouse of matter, sorted by element. And then it has to place them. Suppose the machine puts down a billion atoms every second. Seven octillion atoms would take longer than the universe has existed. Make it a trillion atoms a second, and you are still assembling for two hundred million years. And they cannot go down in any order. Most proteins only work once they are folded. A cell only works once it is running. Much of you is not a structure. It is a process that has to be switched on while it is being built.

Imagine the receiving machine saying. "Okay, I have all the ingredients. Now I just need to assemble seven octillion atoms into one functioning human." At this point, the teleportation machine has started looking suspiciously like the most advanced factory in the universe. It is essentially a molecular three-dimensional printer.

8:04One synapse wrong

The brain is where it gets really interesting. Suppose we successfully rebuild every neuron. But one synapse is wrong. Would you notice? What if a million synapses are slightly different? What if the structure is correct but some electrical states are different? What if the memories are preserved but the exact state of the brain is not? At what point does the reconstructed person stop being you? And this brings us to the strangest problem of all.

8:38Mars-you and Earth-you

Imagine the transporter scans you. It creates a perfect reconstruction on Mars. But the original is still standing here on Earth. Mars-you opens your eyes. You remember everything. You remember walking into the transporter. You remember your family. You remember pressing the button. And you say. "Okay. I'm on Mars." But Earth-you is still standing in the machine. And you say. "No. I'm on Earth." Which one is you?

There is no obvious physical experiment that makes this question go away. Because both people have exactly the same history up to the moment of reconstruction.

9:22Scan, kill, copy

Now let's make it even worse. Suppose the transporter works by destroying the original. The receiving machine builds an identical person. From the outside, everything looks perfect. The new person looks like you. Talks like you. Remembers your childhood. Knows your passwords. And says. "I've been teleported." But from the perspective of the original consciousness… there may have been no journey at all. You died. And somebody else woke up believing they were you. The transporter may therefore be interpreted as this. Scan. Kill. Copy. Rather than. Scan. Travel.

And that is not a technical question anymore. It is a question about what personal identity actually means.

10:12And if there are two

Suppose the transporter malfunctions. Instead of one person on Mars, it creates two. Both copies are perfect. Both remember the same childhood. Both sincerely believe they are the original. Which one is the real you?

And if one of them is destroyed… did we kill you? Or did we kill your identical twin? There may be no objective answer unless we first decide what "being the same person" actually means.

10:46Teleport the mind instead

There is yet another possibility. Don't teleport the body. Teleport the mind. Scan the brain. Transmit the information. Run it somewhere else. Perhaps in another biological brain. Perhaps in a synthetic brain. Perhaps in a robot. Now the question becomes this. If your mind runs in a machine… are you still you? And if we can run two copies simultaneously… which one is conscious? Maybe both. Maybe neither. Maybe consciousness is not something that can simply be copied. We don't currently know.

11:26Out of phase

Science fiction has another trick. Perhaps we temporarily change how you interact with matter. You become "out of phase." You walk through a wall. Until you ask. If you don't interact with the wall, why would you interact with the air? And why don't you fall through the floor? If you can selectively interact with some particles but not others… we've just invented a completely new kind of physics. So again, the idea is simple. The mechanism is not.

12:00Which one is hardest

We have now imagined several very different machines. The easiest teleportation to explain is not necessarily the easiest teleportation to build. And the easiest one philosophically may be the hardest one physically. A Portal style teleportation preserves your physical continuity… but requires control over spacetime. A Star Trek style transporter may be easier to imagine technologically… but creates the horrifying question of whether the person who arrives is actually you.

12:34Would you use it

So imagine that tomorrow somebody offers you a teleportation device. It has been tested a million times. Every test is successful. Every person who comes out is indistinguishable from the person who went in. The machine is perfectly safe. Except for one thing.

Nobody can prove that the consciousness on the other side is the same consciousness that entered. Would you use it?

Because perhaps the biggest problem with teleportation isn't this. "How do we move a human from one place to another?" Perhaps it's this. "How do we know that the person who arrives is the person who left?" And maybe that is the one question no teleportation machine can ever answer.
