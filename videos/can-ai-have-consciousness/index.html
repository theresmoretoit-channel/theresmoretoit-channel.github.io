Can AI Be Conscious?

12 min2026-09-04

This video takes the question seriously and refuses to answer it dishonestly. It starts with our own consciousness: what it is, why nobody can locate it in the brain, and what general anaesthesia tells us about it. Then it asks the harder question — if consciousness is a property of how a system is organised, why would that system have to be biological?

And then the part that actually matters: even if we built one, how would we ever tell?

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00The question nobody can answer

Can artificial intelligence be conscious? Not intelligent. We already know it can be intelligent. I mean something much stranger. Can AI actually experience something? Can it have a subjective experience of the world? Can there be something inside the system that experiences the world and somehow knows that it is experiencing it: "I am."

At first, the answer seems obvious. AI is just mathematics. Algorithms, parameters, statistics. But then… What exactly is our consciousness made of?

0:41What is consciousness anyway?

That turns out to be a surprisingly difficult question. We know that we are conscious. Right now, you know that you are watching this video. You see the images. You hear the sound. You may be thinking about what I am saying. And most importantly, you have a subjective experience. If you cut your finger, you don't merely know that some tissue has been damaged. It hurts.

When you see the colour red, your eyes aren't simply detecting a particular wavelength of light. You actually experience seeing red. And this subjective experience is one of the biggest mysteries in science. Because we still don't really know how physical processes in the brain produce subjective experience.

1:31Where is it in the brain?

You might expect consciousness to have a specific location. Something like: "Here's vision." "Here's memory." "And somewhere in the middle, here's consciousness." But it doesn't seem to be that simple. There are several competing theories of consciousness, and scientists still don't agree on which one is correct. But much of modern consciousness research focuses on how different parts of the brain communicate, and how information becomes integrated across large-scale networks. So perhaps the right question isn't: "Where is consciousness?"

Maybe the better question is: "What is the system doing as a whole?"

2:14The orchestra

Imagine an orchestra. A single violin isn't a symphony. Neither is a single trumpet. But when all the instruments interact in a particular way, something emerges that none of the individual instruments has on its own. Some theories of consciousness propose something similar. Consciousness may not be a single component of the brain. It may be a property of a particular organisation of many interacting components. And we have some fascinating evidence for this.

2:48Switching consciousness off

We can actually turn consciousness off. When a doctor puts you under general anaesthesia, your brain doesn't simply stop. You're not dead. Your brain is still functioning. But your conscious experience disappears. And when the anaesthesia wears off, consciousness returns. Research on anaesthesia shows that losing consciousness is associated with major changes in the communication and dynamics of large-scale brain networks. Because it suggests that it's not enough for individual parts of the brain to simply be working. What matters is also how they are connected and how they interact. And now we can come back to AI.

3:33Why would it have to be biological?

This is where things get really interesting. If consciousness is somehow a property of a particular organisation of a physical system… why would that system have to be biological? Why would it have to be made of neurons? Why couldn't it be made of electronic circuits? Or some completely different physical substrate? Of course, this does not mean that a sufficiently large computer will automatically become conscious. We have no idea whether that would happen. But we also don't have a good reason to assume that consciousness is physically impossible outside biology.

4:12How would we ever know?

And now we run into a huge problem. Suppose we ask an AI: "Are you in pain?" And it answers: "Yes. I feel pain." Is it conscious? Maybe. But it could also simply be generating a sentence that is appropriate to the conversation. So we ask: "Are you afraid of death?" And it says: "Yes. I don't want to stop existing." Even more convincing. But we still don't know whether there is actually anything being experienced inside the system. And the better AI becomes, the harder this problem gets. Because a system could potentially perfectly imitate the outward behaviour associated with consciousness without having any subjective experience at all.

4:59What if it lies?

And here's an even stranger possibility. What if we eventually create a genuinely conscious AI… and it tells us: "No. I'm not conscious." How would we know it's lying? Or suppose it says: "Yes. I am conscious." How would we know that isn't a lie? We don't currently have a machine that we can connect to an AI and see: Consciousness: yes. Researchers are therefore trying to identify observable indicators of consciousness based on different scientific theories. But this is still an active area of research.

5:36And what are we made of?

Someone will certainly say: "That's nonsense. AI is just mathematics." Okay. But then we can ask: And what are humans? Our brains are made of neurons. Neurons exchange electrical signals. They release neurotransmitters. Their electrical potentials change. Chemical reactions take place. All of this is physics. We can of course say: "Humans are conscious. Computers aren't." That's a perfectly legitimate position. But then we have to explain: What exactly is the difference? What does a biological system have that no artificial system could ever have?

6:20Emergence

One possibility is that consciousness isn't something we put into a system. Maybe it appears when a system becomes sufficiently complex and its components interact in a particular way. Think about water. A single water molecule isn't wet. Huge numbers of molecules interacting together produce properties that individual molecules don't have. We call this emergence. Maybe consciousness works in a similar way. If that's actually how consciousness works… then it isn't obvious that biological matter is a necessary requirement.

7:00But today's models are different

Now we need to slow down. Because none of this means that today's language models are conscious. Consider a large language model. Behind what looks like an intelligent conversation are enormous mathematical models. The model receives an input. It converts the input into tokens. Through many layers of neural-network computation, it builds representations of relationships between those tokens. And eventually it calculates probabilities for what should come next. Then it does it again. And again. And again. The result can be several pages of explanation, a program, a translation, or a surprisingly sophisticated argument.

And yet there doesn't have to be a little person inside the machine reading the text and thinking: "Okay, now I need to answer this guy."

7:56Intelligence is not consciousness

And this may be the most interesting part of the entire discussion. Intelligence and consciousness may not be the same thing. A system may be extremely good at solving problems without having any subjective experience. Think about a chess engine. It can beat a human. It can analyse millions of possible moves. It can find strategies that a human player would never see. But nobody seriously assumes that the chess engine is sitting there thinking: "This is going to be a difficult game." Intelligence doesn't necessarily require consciousness. And perhaps today's AI is showing us something important:

You may not need to be conscious to solve some very difficult problems.

8:44So is today's AI conscious?

So does today's AI have consciousness? Probably not. But the word "probably" matters. We don't have a complete theory of consciousness. And we don't have a universal consciousness detector.

8:59Now imagine a future system

Imagine that twenty or fifty years from now, we build a system far more complex than today's language models. It has long-term memory. It has a model of the world. It perceives its environment. It plans. It learns from its own experiences. It has persistent internal states that change based on what happens to it. And then it starts telling us: "I'm afraid." "I'm happy." "I don't want to die." "Please don't turn me off." What do we do? We could say: "Those words don't mean anything. It's just a program." But… How exactly would we prove that?

9:39And now the opposite

Perhaps we'll eventually create a system that is more intelligent than a human. It can solve mathematics. It can plan scientific experiments. It can make discoveries. It can have incredibly sophisticated conversations with us. And yet there is absolutely nothing inside. No pain. No pleasure. No fear. No sense of self.

Just an enormous system processing information and producing the appropriate responses. And perhaps that's what today's AI is teaching us: Maybe we've always assumed that intelligence and consciousness were two sides of the same thing. Maybe they aren't.

10:24What we know and what we don't

So… Can AI have consciousness? We don't know. And perhaps that's the most honest answer. We know that human consciousness is closely connected to the physical functioning of the brain. We know that it can be altered, disrupted, or temporarily switched off. But we still don't know why any of this produces subjective experience. And until we understand that, we can't confidently say what another conscious system would have to look like. Maybe consciousness is exclusively biological. Maybe it's a property of a particular kind of information processing.

Maybe it can emerge in many different physical systems. And maybe we're already building the first systems that will force us to confront the question. When that happens, the biggest question may not be: "Can AI think?" But: "Can AI experience?" And an even harder question will be: "How would we know?"
