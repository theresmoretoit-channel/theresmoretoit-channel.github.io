Why Is It So Hard to Be Trans?

14 min2026-10-24

This video looks at what it is like to be transgender in a world that judges from the outside. What passing means, and why it is not proof of anyone's identity. Who decides what a man or a woman should look like. How harassment, and the possibility of it, shapes ordinary decisions, and what the largest survey of trans people in the United States found. How rejection by family, at work or in healthcare can make everyday life harder. Why so many questions asked publicly about trans people are about their bodies. And why being transgender is not information that automatically belongs to everyone who meets you.

This is the fourth video in a series about transgender people. The earlier ones asked why there suddenly seem to be so many trans people, how you know you're trans, and why trans people change their bodies. The final one asks: when does society become the problem?

The full narration of the video.

0:00The first thing people notice

Imagine walking into a room. Before you say a word, people have already started making assumptions about you. They notice your face. Your hair. Your clothes. The sound of your voice. The way you move. And without even thinking about it, they make a decision. Man or woman. Most of the time, this process feels completely natural. We do it automatically, and usually, nobody gives it a second thought. But what happens when the way people see you doesn't match who you are? What happens when strangers question you, stare at you, or treat you differently because something about your appearance doesn't fit their expectations?

For some transgender people, this isn't an occasional awkward moment. It's something they may have to think about every time they leave home. Will someone notice? Will they be accepted? Will they be safe? And perhaps the most exhausting question of all: Will they be allowed to go about their day without having to explain themselves? In our previous videos, we explored what it means to be transgender, how people discover their identity, and why some choose to change their bodies. But there's another part of this experience. The world outside.

1:21What does passing actually mean?

What does passing actually mean? There's a word often used in discussions about transgender people: passing. It describes a situation in which other people perceive someone as a man or a woman without recognising that they are transgender. For some trans people, passing is very important. It can mean being addressed correctly without having to explain anything. It can make everyday interactions less stressful. And in some circumstances, it can reduce the risk of unwanted attention or harassment. But passing can also be complicated.

Some people cannot achieve the appearance they want, even after years of effort. Others cannot afford the treatments or procedures they might want. And some simply don't want to spend their lives trying to satisfy other people's expectations. Not every trans person wants to look conventionally masculine or feminine. Passing is not proof that someone is really a man or a woman. And failing to pass doesn't make someone's identity any less real. Yet society often behaves as if appearance were the final authority. If you look the way people expect, they may accept you without a question. If you don't, you may find yourself under scrutiny.

A person can be completely certain about who they are, yet still have to negotiate that identity with strangers who believe they can judge it from the outside. But why does appearance have so much power over us in the first place?

2:57Who decides what a man or woman should look like?

Who decides what a man or woman should look like? Think about the clothes we associate with men and women. Long hair. Short hair. Dresses. Trousers. Makeup. Beards. Some of these features are connected to biological differences. Others are cultural conventions. Many are a mixture of biology, individual preference, and social expectations. A man can have long hair. A woman can have a deep voice. A man can enjoy makeup. A woman can prefer traditionally masculine clothing. None of these things, by themselves, tells us everything about someone's gender identity. We already understand this when we're talking about cisgender people.

A woman doesn't stop being a woman because she has broad shoulders. A man doesn't become less of a man because his voice is higher than average. But when a transgender person doesn't fit conventional expectations, people may suddenly become much more interested in these details. A deep voice becomes evidence to investigate. A person's face becomes something to examine. Their clothes become an invitation to question who they really are. And this reveals something important. The problem isn't simply that people notice differences. It's that we often attach rules and judgements to those differences.

We create narrow ideas about what men and women should look like. Then we treat anyone outside those boundaries as a problem that needs explaining. Trans people are particularly vulnerable to this scrutiny, but they aren't the only ones affected. Cisgender people who don't fit gender stereotypes can face ridicule and suspicion, too. The underlying expectation is similar: You are allowed to be yourself, as long as you do it in a way that makes everyone else comfortable.

4:58When being different has a cost

When being different has a cost. For many people, an uncomfortable stare is just an uncomfortable stare. For someone who has experienced repeated harassment, it can carry a different meaning. A stranger looking for too long might simply be curious. Or they might be preparing to make a cruel remark. When you've experienced enough negative reactions, uncertainty itself can become exhausting. You may start thinking about which public places feel comfortable, which bathrooms you can use without confrontation, or whether a particular journey is worth the stress.

Some people become more careful about where they go, what they wear, or how they present themselves. Others decide that they won't allow other people's reactions to dictate their lives. But the possibility of harassment can influence decisions even when nothing actually happens. And sometimes, something does happen. In 2022, the largest survey of transgender people in the United States asked more than ninety thousand of them about their experiences. In the previous twelve months, about three in ten said they had been verbally harassed because of their gender identity or expression. Three in a hundred said they had been physically attacked.

It wasn't a random sample, and the risks vary between countries and circumstances. It would be wrong to suggest that every trans person is in constant danger. It would be equally wrong to pretend that harassment is merely an imaginary fear. And that is why it matters where the line lies. Disagreement is not automatically discrimination. A person can misunderstand something, ask a sincere question, or make an honest mistake. What matters is also how they respond. Do they listen when corrected? Or do they turn the encounter into an opportunity to humiliate someone?

7:00Family, work and everyday life

Family, work, and everyday life. The consequences of rejection can become much more serious when they come from people who have power over our lives. Imagine telling your family something important about yourself. You might hope for questions, confusion, or a little time to adjust. Instead, you might hear that you're embarrassing the family. That you're ruining your life. That you're no longer the person they thought they knew. Some families eventually learn, adapt, and become supportive. Others remain rejecting. For a young person who depends on their parents, the consequences can be particularly difficult.

Acceptance may determine whether home feels safe, whether they can speak honestly, and whether they have someone to turn to when life gets complicated. The same principle applies at work. A transgender employee might worry about being mocked by colleagues, treated differently by a manager, or judged by customers. In some circumstances, discrimination can affect hiring, promotion, or continued employment. And then there's healthcare. Imagine delaying a doctor's appointment because you're worried about how you'll be treated.

Or needing help with an ordinary medical problem, only to find that the conversation becomes unnecessarily focused on your transgender status. But the underlying issue is simple. Everyone depends on other people to some degree. When acceptance becomes conditional on concealing or defending a part of yourself, ordinary life can become unnecessarily difficult.

8:43Why is everything about trans people so sexualised?

Why is everything about trans people so sexualised? Consider how often public discussions about transgender people focus on their genitals. Their surgeries. Their intimate relationships. Some questions about anatomy and medical treatment are entirely legitimate. But there's a difference between discussing an issue and treating an entire group of people as if their bodies were public property. Transgender people can find themselves subjected to questions that would be deeply intrusive in almost any other context. What did you look like before? What exactly is in your underwear? What was your name before?

The last one is especially inappropriate. For many trans people, their old name is something they have deliberately left behind. Would you answer these questions from a stranger about your own body and your own past? Probably not. And you shouldn't have to. The problem is when curiosity becomes entitlement. When a person stops being seen as an individual and becomes a topic of fascination, suspicion, or political argument. A transgender person may be a programmer, a teacher, a parent, a musician, or someone who simply wants to buy groceries and get home.

Their entire existence is not a public debate. And they don't owe the world an explanation of their intimate life. Who has the right to know that someone is transgender?

10:17The right to privacy

The right to privacy. Being transgender is not a confession. It isn't wrongdoing. And it isn't a piece of information that automatically belongs to everyone who meets you. Some transgender people are open about their history. They may talk about their experiences publicly, educate others, or campaign for equal rights. Others prefer to keep that part of their lives private. Both choices deserve respect. A doctor may need to know something about a patient's medical history to provide appropriate treatment. A stranger on a bus usually doesn't.

There is also a difference between someone choosing to share their history and someone having that history exposed without their consent. Revealing that a person is transgender can have consequences they never agreed to accept. It can change how colleagues treat them. It can expose them to harassment. In some circumstances, it can create serious safety concerns. This doesn't mean transgender people must always conceal their identity. It means that they should have a choice. Privacy is not the same as shame. Perhaps the question isn't why some transgender people want privacy, but why they should have to justify wanting it.

11:41What would acceptance actually look like?

What would acceptance actually look like? Acceptance doesn't require perfect understanding. It means recognising that another person deserves dignity, even when their life doesn't fit our expectations. We tell transgender people that they should be comfortable with themselves. But then we scrutinise their appearance. We tell them that they should be honest. But sometimes we punish them for being open. Some find acceptance easily. Others struggle for years. And many simply want what most of us already take for granted. To go to work. To see their friends. To visit a doctor. To walk down the street.

To live their lives without having to prove, every single day, that they deserve to be there.

But what happens when rejection, fear, and the pressure to hide begin to affect someone's mental health? In our final video, we'll explore gender dysphoria, psychological distress, and suicide risk among transgender people. And we'll ask how much of that suffering comes from the experience itself, and how much from the way society responds to it. When does society become the problem?
