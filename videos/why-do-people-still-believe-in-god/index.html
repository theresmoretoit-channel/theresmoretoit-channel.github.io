Why Do People Still Believe in God?

11 min2026-09-28

And yet billions of people still believe in God. Why?

Maybe science didn't simply fail to replace religion. Maybe we were expecting it to answer a question it was never designed to answer. This video looks at why belief persists even in an age of science: the difference between explaining HOW something happens and WHY it exists, a pattern-seeking brain that keeps asking "who caused this?", a strange experiment where atheists' bodies reacted to daring God, death and the search for meaning, religion as a social system, what scientists actually believe — and where science and religion do, and don't, overlap.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Science explains almost everything

We can see galaxies billions of light-years away. We can edit DNA. We can build machines that talk, reason, write music and create images. We can explain lightning, disease, evolution and the history of the universe. And yet billions of people still believe in God. Why?

Did science simply fail to replace religion? Or were we expecting science to answer a question it was never designed to answer?

0:33A world full of mysteries

For most of human history, the world was full of mysteries. A storm wasn't just a physical phenomenon. It could be a god's anger. A successful harvest could be a blessing. A plague could be punishment. And if you wanted to understand why something happened, one obvious explanation was: Someone wanted it to happen.

0:56Science takes the mysteries apart

Then science started taking these mysteries apart. Lightning became electricity. Disease became biology. The diversity of life became evolution. And the universe itself turned out to have a history stretching back billions of years. One by one, things that once seemed to require supernatural explanations got natural ones. So perhaps religion should have disappeared. Except it didn't.

1:26Explaining how, not why

Because explaining how something happens doesn't necessarily answer why it exists. Science can describe the evolution of the universe. But why is there a universe at all? And science can describe what happens to the body when we die. But it can't tell us whether death has some deeper meaning. These aren't necessarily unanswered scientific questions. Some of them may simply be different kinds of questions. And that's one reason religion can survive even when its old explanations of nature disappear.

But there may be something else going on.

2:05Brains that see patterns

Human brains are extremely good at finding patterns and causes. That's usually useful. If something moves in the bushes, assuming there might be a predator is much safer than assuming it's just the wind. If something unexpected happens, we instinctively ask: What caused this? And sometimes we go one step further: Who caused this? This tendency to detect agency may be one of the ingredients that makes supernatural beliefs intuitive. The important word here is may. Human psychology doesn't prove that God is imaginary.

2:42Belief you might not notice

And here's an even stranger possibility. What if belief doesn't have to be conscious to affect us? In one small experiment, atheists were asked to read out statements daring God to harm the people they loved. They said they didn't believe a word of it. But as they spoke, their bodies showed a measurable stress response. The same response the believers had. Their conscious mind said this meant nothing. Their bodies seemed less certain. This doesn't prove atheists secretly believe. It shows something stranger: what we feel and what we say we believe don't always match.

3:24And then there is death

And then there's death. This might be the hardest problem of all. We can understand death scientifically. Cells stop functioning. The brain stops working. The body eventually breaks down. But understanding the biology doesn't make the existential problem disappear. You know that you're going to die. And, unlike almost every other problem in life, there is no conventional solution. Religion offers one. Maybe death isn't the end. Maybe consciousness continues. Maybe there is another world. Maybe someone is waiting for us. Those ideas can't simply be dismissed as an attempt to explain biology. They're also answers to one of the deepest emotional problems humans have ever faced.

4:16Religion as a social system

And religion isn't just a collection of supernatural claims. It's also a social system. Family. Community. Rituals. Traditions. Holidays. Marriage. Funerals. A shared moral framework. A place where people meet other people who believe the same things. A person can inherit a religion without ever sitting down and deciding: "After carefully examining all available evidence, I have concluded that this particular theological system is correct." They may simply grow up with it. And when millions of people around you treat something as normal, it becomes part of your culture and identity.

That's one reason religion can persist even when individual beliefs become weaker.

5:05Even in how we imagine the future

And it isn't only ancient societies — it reaches into how we imagine the future, too. Take the sci-fi series Battlestar Galactica. Gods… Prophecy… Prayer… Destiny. And you can even find viewers dismissing it as "religious crap." But that's actually what made it interesting. Here was a science-fiction universe filled with spaceships, artificial intelligence and technology far beyond ours… and religion was still there. Because having better technology didn't make them stop being human. And perhaps that's not so unrealistic. Even if our civilisation becomes vastly more advanced than it is today, we might still carry the same questions, fears and need for meaning with us.

Perhaps our descendants could travel between stars, create artificial minds and manipulate matter itself — and still wonder whether there is a God.

6:03A march away from superstition?

We often imagine the history of humanity as a march away from superstition. But reality seems more complicated. Modernization can make societies less religious. And some highly educated, technologically advanced societies are among the most secular places on Earth. But technological sophistication does not automatically produce atheism.

6:29What scientists actually believe

And here's something that doesn't fit the simple story. In 1914, about forty-two per cent of American scientists surveyed said they believed in a personal God. In a similar survey in 1997, the number had barely moved. About thirty-nine per cent. But among members of the National Academy of Sciences, the figure was far lower. About seven per cent. So the relationship between science and belief isn't nearly as simple as we might expect. The interesting question isn't whether scientists believe. It's why different groups of scientists believe so differently.

There are religious people who fully accept evolution and modern cosmology. So perhaps science and religion aren't actually fighting over exactly the same territory.

7:23When claims can be tested

Of course, this doesn't mean every religious claim is compatible with science. Some religious traditions make very specific claims about the physical world. When those claims contradict well-established evidence, science has a way of testing them. And historically, many explanations that were once supernatural have been replaced by natural ones. That's real. But it doesn't follow that every question religion asks is therefore a scientific question. And that's where the argument becomes much harder.

7:58We want stories

And there's another reason religion may survive: humans don't only want explanations. We want stories. We want meaning. We want belonging. We want rituals for the moments when ordinary life isn't enough. Birth. Marriage. Death. Grief. Hope. Even if you don't believe in God, it's easy to understand why people might find these things valuable. And that makes religion much harder to eliminate than simply eliminating an incorrect explanation of lightning.

So will religion eventually disappear? Maybe. Some societies are clearly becoming more secular. Others aren't. And we don't have a simple technological equation where: more science = less religion. Human beings aren't machines that receive new information and automatically update their worldview. We are biological, social creatures. We inherit beliefs. We fear death. We search for meaning. And we tell stories about where we came from and where we're going. Science can change what those stories contain. It doesn't necessarily remove our need for stories.

9:14What did we expect science to replace?

Maybe that's why the question isn't really: "Why do people still believe in God when we have science?" Maybe the better question is: "What exactly were we expecting science to replace?" If religion was simply a primitive explanation for thunder and disease, then science has already replaced much of it. But if religion is also an attempt to answer questions about meaning, mortality, morality and existence itself… then science was never really playing the same game. Science can tell us how the universe works. It can tell us how stars form, how life evolves, and how our brains function.

But whether the universe has a purpose… whether life has meaning… or whether there is something beyond what we can observe… those questions remain open. And perhaps that's why, even in an age of artificial intelligence, genetic engineering and galaxies photographed billions of light-years away, God hasn't disappeared.

Maybe the surprising thing isn't that humans still believe in God. Maybe the surprising thing is that, after thousands of years, we're still asking the same enormous questions.
