Why the Hell Doesn't America Use the Metric System?

11 min2026-10-12

The strange part is that, in a way, it already has. From Thomas Jefferson's decimal plan and a French scientist captured by privateers to President Ford's Metric Conversion Act, kilometre signs on an Arizona interstate and a Mars probe lost to a mix-up of units, and how Ireland, Australia and Britain went about it.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Sixty-five what?

Imagine you're driving along a perfectly normal road in the United States. And you pass a sign. Sixty-five. Sixty-five what? Kilometres per hour? Metres? Bananas? Miles per hour. About a hundred and five kilometres per hour, if you're doing the maths. Then the radio says it's eighty-six degrees. Not Celsius, obviously. That would make the road reconsider its life choices. Fahrenheit. Lunch is weighed in pounds. The recipe wants cups and tablespoons. The screws come in inches. Meanwhile, almost every other country on Earth is watching and thinking… what the hell are you doing?

So why doesn't America just switch to the metric system?

0:47The strange part

Here's the strange part. In a way, it already has. Since 1959, an American inch has been defined as exactly twenty-five point four millimetres. Not roughly. Exactly. A pound is exactly zero point four five three five nine two three seven kilograms. And the yard has been defined by the metre even longer, since 1893. So American units aren't some independent ancient system. They're the metric system in a cowboy hat. It's a bit like announcing that you don't use the internet… in a Facebook post. So the real question isn't whether America can use metric. It's why the sign still says sixty-five.

1:40Not imperial at all

Let's start with a name. People often call American units imperial. They aren't. The colonists brought English units with them. Feet. Pounds. Gallons. Miles. Then America became independent. In 1824, Britain tidied up its own units into a new system, the imperial system. America was no longer listening. So the two drifted apart. An American gallon is about three point eight litres. A British one, about four and a half. America doesn't use the imperial system. It uses something older.

2:22France builds something new

Meanwhile, in the 1790s, France was building something new. A system where everything comes in tens. A thousand metres make a kilometre. A thousand grams make a kilogram. Converting means moving a decimal point. Simple, elegant, boring. Exactly what measurement should be. And some Americans loved the idea. In 1790, Thomas Jefferson proposed a decimal system of his own. Congress shelved it. Oddly, America had already gone decimal with its money. A hundred cents to a dollar. Britain only did that in 1971.

3:05Did pirates stop it?

There's even a famous story that pirates stopped America going metric. In 1794, a French scientist sailing to America with standards of length and weight was captured by privateers. He died in prison. It's a great story. It's just not the reason. Nobody in Congress was waiting for that ship. In fact, metric became legal in the United States in 1866. And in 1875, America was one of seventeen countries to sign the Treaty of the Metre. So America was never against metric. It just never stopped using what it already had.

3:46Why units stick

Because once a unit settles in, it stops being maths. It becomes infrastructure. A carpenter buys tools in inches. A builder draws houses in feet. A factory cuts parts to fit them. A school teaches children to measure. Every one of those depends on all the others. Changing the unit means coordinating a change across the whole network. And it gets into your head. If you grew up with miles, you know a mile is about a twenty-minute walk. It doesn't feel like a unit. It's just a mile. Tell an American their house is a hundred and sixty-seven square metres, and they'll say fine… but how big is that?

One thousand eight hundred square feet. That number means something.

4:35America finally tries

By the 1970s, most of the world had switched. And America finally decided to try. In 1975, President Gerald Ford signed the Metric Conversion Act. It set up a Metric Board to coordinate the change. And there was a catch. The change was voluntary. Nobody abolished the mile. The government essentially said, metric is probably a good idea. And America said… no, thanks. Some places did try. Interstate 19 in Arizona got its distance signs in kilometres. Those signs are still there today. But to many people, it felt like being told that their mile was obsolete. Their gallon. Their pound. Their Fahrenheit.

And that's the strongest argument against switching. Not that metric is worse. It isn't. It's that the old system works well enough for the people who use it. Politicians noticed that telling voters their units were obsolete wasn't a winning strategy. In 1982, the Reagan administration shut the Metric Board down.

5:47Metric where it's useful

That was the end of the big plan. But not the end of metric. American scientists use kilograms. Doctors dose in milligrams. Soldiers measure distance in kilometres, and call them klicks. Fizzy drinks come in two-litre bottles. Because international trade doesn't care about tradition. If you build cars or computers for the global market, metric units are hard to avoid. So America runs two systems at once. Metric where it's useful. Traditional units where people are attached to them.

6:23The price of two systems

And running two systems has a price. In 1999, NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter. Software written by the contractor reported thruster impulses in pound-force seconds. NASA's navigators expected newton seconds. So every thruster firing looked more than four times weaker than it really was. For months, the error piled up. The spacecraft came in far too low, and was lost. The units were the cause. People did notice something was off. In nine months of flight, nobody tracked it down. The irony is that NASA had made metric its preferred system back in 1991. The spacecraft's own specification called for newton seconds.

A computer doesn't care about tradition. It won't stop and ask which units you meant.

7:18Are Americans being stupid?

So are Americans being stupid? No. Nobody thinks in tens naturally. We think in whatever we grew up with. A French child learns a hundred centimetres to a metre. An American child learns twelve inches to a foot. Neither finds it strange. Until they travel. Imagine being told that from tomorrow, kilometres are illegal. Distances are now in furlongs. Furlongs aren't hard to learn. You'd still be furious, because you'd have to relearn everything you already know. That's the real answer. Not that the American system is better. It's that changing an established system has an enormous switching cost.

8:05How did other countries do it?

But other countries paid it. So how did they do it? At midnight on the twentieth of January 2005, every speed limit in Ireland switched to kilometres per hour. Nearly sixty thousand new signs, put up in advance, took effect at once. Australia went metric in the 1970s, industry by industry, with deadlines. Its road signs changed in 1974. Within about a decade, it was essentially done.

8:36And then there's Britain

And then there's Britain. Britain decided to go metric in 1965. Schools switched. Most packaged food switched. Then in 1980, the government abolished its Metrication Board. And Britain stopped halfway. Petrol by the litre. Road signs in miles. Beer by the pint. So before anyone in Britain laughs at Americans… British speed limits are in miles per hour too. In each of these stories, the countries that finished set a date and kept to it. The ones that left it to choice ended up with a mixture. And America's one serious attempt was voluntary from the start.

So the signs aren't the hard part. Ireland switched over in a single night. Australia changed a whole country in about ten years. The hard part is someone naming the day the mile ends. And no American president has ever set that date.

9:36So why doesn't America use metric?

So why doesn't America use the metric system? In many ways, it already does. In its labs, its hospitals, its army, and in the definition of every inch. What it never really changed is everyday life. The road signs. The recipes. The weather forecast. Changing those would take a date on the calendar, and three hundred and forty million people keeping to it. So the radio still says eighty-six degrees. And somehow, everyone there knows exactly what that means.
