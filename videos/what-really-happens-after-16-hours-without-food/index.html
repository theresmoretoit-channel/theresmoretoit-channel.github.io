Your Body, and How Long It Lasts

What Really Happens After 16 Hours Without Food?

9 min2026-10-14

From a 16:8 meal schedule and a fasting guide that cites a study on mice, to Christian de Duve's lysosome, Yoshinori Ohsumi's starving yeast, mice that went two days without food and the first attempts to measure autophagy in human blood: what the evidence behind the sixteen-hour number actually shows.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00The sixteen-hour promise

Stop eating for sixteen hours, says the internet, and something switches on inside you. Your cells start taking out the trash. Old, damaged parts get broken down and recycled. A kind of built-in repair mode. It even has a scientific name. Autophagy. Greek for "self-eating". And in 2016, the scientist who worked out how it happens won a Nobel Prize. But where does that sixteen actually come from? And has anyone ever measured it in a human being?

0:33Following the number back

Follow the number back, and the trail gets thin very quickly. Sixteen hours is familiar from a meal schedule. Sixteen hours without food, then eight hours to eat. One guide to it, from 2010, promoted it for training and fat loss. The word autophagy isn't in it. The link came later. One popular fasting guide says autophagy "typically begins around the sixteen-hour mark". As its source, it links to a study. A study on mice, which went without food for twenty-four or forty-eight hours. That study never measured sixteen hours. And it wasn't done on people.

Autophagy doesn't start when you stop eating. It's running right now in your cells, at a low background level. Fasting can turn it up. But up from what, by how much, and after how long in a human? That's the real question.

1:39How a cell takes out the trash

Your cells are full of machinery that wears out. Proteins fold the wrong way. Clumps build up. Mitochondria, the cell's power plants, get damaged. Small things are handled by the proteasome, a molecular shredder that destroys proteins one by one. Autophagy can take apart an entire mitochondrion. The proteasome can't do that on its own. It starts with a membrane. A thin sheet forms around the cargo and keeps growing until it closes. The result is a bag with a double wall, called an autophagosome. Next, it fuses with a lysosome.

A lysosome is the cell's stomach. A sealed, acidic sac full of digestive enzymes. The cargo is broken down into its building blocks, and those go back out into the cell to be used again. The lysosome's discoverer, Christian de Duve, gave this process its name in 1963.

2:38A body that can't do without it

How much does a body depend on it? Newborn mice show it clearly. At birth, the food supply from the mother is cut off, and autophagy surges in tissues across the body. Mice born without one key autophagy gene look normal. They die within a day. So this isn't an emergency mode waiting for you to skip a meal. It's maintenance, and it never stops.

3:05Ohsumi's starving yeast

Then where does the fasting idea come from? Partly from yeast. In the early 1990s, Yoshinori Ohsumi took yeast cells that couldn't digest anything in their recycling chamber, and starved them. Within an hour, little bubbles of cell contents began to pile up inside. After three hours, they almost filled the chamber. Starvation had turned the recycling up. And because digestion was blocked, the bubbles had nowhere to go. Remember that trick. From those yeast, he found fifteen genes the process depends on. That's what the Nobel Prize was for. Genes, in yeast. Not a fasting schedule.

3:49Two days without food, for a mouse

The famous fasting results came later, from mice. In a 2010 study, young male mice went without food for one or two days. In their brain cells, the researchers counted far more autophagosomes. After two days, in one type of neuron, about four times as many. That's real data. But a mouse burns about seven times more energy per gram of body than a human does. In one lab study, adult mice lost about fourteen percent of their weight in a single day without food. So for a mouse, a day or two without food isn't a long fast. It's an extreme one.

And results from different organs and methods don't always agree. One mouse study found no response in the brain at all. Another, six years later, found a strong one. Even in mice, the answer depends on where you look, and how. Nobody can turn those days into a human countdown.

4:50Looking inside a human

So what about people? The first problem is simple. You can't look inside a living person's organs and watch. Researchers can take blood, or a small piece of muscle. That's about it. And counting isn't enough. More autophagosomes could mean the cell is recycling faster… or that the exit is blocked and they're piling up, just like in Ohsumi's yeast. What matters is the whole flow, from bag to breakdown. Scientists call it flux. Measuring it in people is new, and so far mostly limited to blood.

5:27What the human studies found

The results so far are small and mixed. After a twelve-hour overnight fast, blood cells recycled no faster than an hour after a protein meal. Eighteen-hour fasts for four days raised the activity of one autophagy gene in blood by about a fifth. That's a signal from a gene, not a measurement of the recycling. The largest study followed a hundred and twenty-one people for six months, fasting for twenty hours, three times a week. Within the fasting group, the change wasn't significant. A comparison added after the fact did find a difference at six months, partly because autophagy drifted down in the standard-care group.

6:10Asking an expert directly

One of the field's leading researchers, Guido Kroemer, was asked directly. Does a sixteen-hour fast induce autophagy in any human tissue? His answer was three words. "We don't know." That's the honest state of things. Not "it doesn't happen". We simply don't know yet.

6:31Longer life, and cancer

So should we even want more of it? In animals, the case looks strong. Mice that lose autophagy in the brain develop movement problems, and their neurons fill with clumps of protein. Turn it up, and some animals live longer. Mice engineered to overproduce Atg5 lived about seventeen percent longer. In humans, there's no evidence yet that boosting autophagy makes anyone live longer. And then there's cancer. Mice with only one working copy of a key autophagy gene develop tumours more often. Autophagy helps keep cancer from starting.

But once a tumour is established, it can use autophagy to survive. Pancreatic tumours rely on it heavily, and in mice, blocking it made them shrink. Autophagy isn't good or bad. It's a tool, and what it does depends on whose side it's working for.

7:30So what really happens?

So what really happens after sixteen hours without food? Fasting does change the fuel your body uses. Over time, it relies more on fat and ketones. But that's a gradual shift, not a universal countdown. And it doesn't tell us when autophagy changes. Inside your cells, the recycling that never stopped keeps going. Maybe a little faster, in some tissues. By how much, nobody can tell you yet. So when a timeline promises that repair begins at a precise hour, it knows more than the science does. Your cells don't stay alive by keeping everything forever.

Sometimes, staying alive means knowing what to throw away.
