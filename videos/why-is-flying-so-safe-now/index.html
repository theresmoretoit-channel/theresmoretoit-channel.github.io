Ordinary Things, Awkward Questions

Why Is Flying So Safe Now?

12 min2026-11-15

This video goes through the Boeing bomber that crashed because of a forgotten lock, the de Havilland Comets that broke apart in mid-air and a fuselage tested in a tank of water, the young Australian scientist behind the black box, the radio call that was lost at Tenerife and the single word that changed after it, the DC-10 that lost all three hydraulic systems over Iowa, Air France 447 and what happens when the autopilot switches off, a warning that never reached TWA, Swiss cheese, and the Boeing 737 MAX.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Forty times safer

In the early 1970s, every time you boarded a passenger plane, your chance of dying on that flight was about one in three hundred and fifty thousand. Today, it's about one in fourteen million. In a single lifetime, flying has become roughly forty times safer. And it's not because planes stopped breaking. Engines still fail. Sensors still freeze. Pilots still make mistakes. So what actually changed?

Almost every rule on a modern flight exists for the same reason. Somebody, somewhere, died. But that alone doesn't explain it. People die on roads too, and roads haven't become anything like forty times safer. So the real question isn't whether aviation learns from its crashes. It's how it manages to learn so well.

0:55"Too much airplane"

On the thirtieth of October 1935, Boeing showed the US Army its new bomber. The Model 299. Four engines, retractable landing gear, and far more switches and settings than any pilot was used to. Seconds after takeoff, it climbed steeply, stalled, and crashed. Two of the five men on board died. The pilots weren't careless. They had simply forgotten to release a lock that held the controls in place on the ground. A newspaper called it "too much airplane for one man to fly". The answer wasn't more training. It was a card. A short list of things to check before takeoff, and before landing.

The Army bought the bomber anyway. It became the B-17, and its crews flew it with checklists. Today, a captain with twenty thousand hours still reads a list to make sure the landing gear is down. Not because they don't know how to fly. But because knowing what to do isn't the same as doing it, every single time.

2:02Why did the Comets break apart?

In 1952, Britain's de Havilland Comet became the first jet airliner to carry paying passengers. It flew higher and faster than any airliner before it, above most of the weather. Then, in early 1954, two Comets broke apart in mid-air, within three months of each other. No storm. No collision. No warning. Every Comet was grounded. Investigators put a complete one into a giant tank of water, and pressurised the cabin and released it, over and over. After the equivalent of about three thousand flights, the fuselage tore open.

The crack had started at the corner of an opening in the fuselage. Every flight stretched the metal there, a little, until it gave way. Metal fatigue. From then on, every new airliner had to be designed against metal fatigue, and its makers had to show that it would hold.

3:00The black box

There was a second lesson in the Comets, and it came from a young Australian scientist who'd been asked to help work out what had gone wrong. David Warren noticed that the investigators could only guess. Nobody knew what the crew had seen or said in their last moments. So he built a device that recorded the instruments and the voices in the cockpit, and would survive the crash. We still call it the black box. It's actually bright orange, so it can be found in the wreckage. And once investigators could hear what went on inside a cockpit, they found problems that weren't in the machine at all.

3:39Tenerife

On the twenty-seventh of March 1977, two Boeing 747s were on the same runway at Tenerife, in the Canary Islands. The fog was so thick that neither crew could see the other. The KLM jet began its takeoff roll. The Pan Am jet was still on the runway, taxiing towards it. The KLM captain believed he'd been cleared to take off. He hadn't. The tower had said "OK", and then "stand by for takeoff". But the second half was lost, because another radio call came in on top of it. In the cockpit, the flight engineer asked, "Is he not clear, that Pan American?" The captain answered, "Oh, yes." And kept going.

Five hundred and eighty-three people died. It's still the deadliest accident in the history of aviation.

4:33A single word

Two things changed. The first was a single word. Today, pilots and controllers only say "takeoff" when an aircraft is actually cleared to take off. Before that, it's a "departure".

4:47Speaking up in the cockpit

The second change took longer. The KLM captain was the head of the airline's training for the 747. His engineer had doubts, and voiced them. Once. Out of Tenerife, other crashes like it, and years of research at NASA, came a new kind of training, known as Crew Resource Management. The captain is still in charge. But a first officer saying, "I think that's wrong," isn't challenging authority. They're doing their job. And sometimes, that teamwork is all a crew has left.

5:22United 232: no controls left

On the nineteenth of July 1989, United Flight 232, a DC-10, was cruising over Iowa when its tail engine blew apart. Fragments tore through the tail and drained all three hydraulic systems. The pilots could no longer move a single control surface. Three separate systems were meant to make that impossible. But all three ran through the same small part of the tail. The crew found one way left to steer. More power on one wing engine, less on the other. A DC-10 instructor, travelling as a passenger, came up to the cockpit and worked the throttles for them.

They reached the airport at Sioux City. The landing was a disaster. The plane cartwheeled and broke apart. And yet a hundred and eighty-four of the two hundred and ninety-six people on board survived. Captain Al Haynes later said a big part of the reason was the way the four of them worked together. The training that came out of crashes like Tenerife.

6:28Air France 447

By then, computers could fly a plane more smoothly than any pilot. That made flying safer. But it created a new kind of problem. On the first of June 2009, Air France Flight 447 was flying from Rio de Janeiro to Paris, at night, over the Atlantic. Ice crystals blocked the sensors that measure airspeed. The autopilot switched off, and suddenly the pilots were flying by hand, in the dark, in turbulence. The pilot at the controls pulled the nose up. The plane climbed, slowed, and stalled. The stall warning sounded again and again. For more than three minutes, the plane fell towards the sea with its nose still pointing up.

Two hundred and twenty-eight people died. The recorders were found almost two years later, almost four kilometres under the sea. The lesson was uncomfortable. Automation makes flying safer… but pilots still have to understand what it's doing, and be ready when it stops.

7:34Lessons that cost lives

So far, every lesson in this story has cost lives. But the biggest reason flying is so safe is that most of its lessons don't.

7:45A warning that never arrived

On the first of December 1974, a TWA jet descended too early on its approach to Washington Dulles and flew into a ridge. Ninety-two people died. Six weeks earlier, a United crew had made the same mistake, on the same approach. They caught it just in time, and reported it. But only United's own pilots were told. The warning never reached TWA.

8:12A system run by NASA

So in 1976, America set up a reporting system run by NASA, not by the regulator. Pilots, controllers and mechanics could report their own mistakes, and in most cases, the report couldn't be used to punish them. It has received more than two million reports. Close calls, confusing charts, a checklist that was easy to misread… each one a lesson, learned without anyone getting hurt. And when something does go wrong, the investigation has one purpose, written into international rules. To prevent the next accident. Not to decide who's to blame.

That's the part that's easy to miss. People only tell you about their mistakes if they're not afraid of what happens next.

9:01Swiss cheese

Safety experts like to picture this as slices of Swiss cheese. The design, the checklist, the crew, the training. Every slice has holes in it. An accident only happens when the holes in every slice line up. And every crash, and every report, adds a slice, or makes a hole smaller.

9:22The Boeing 737 MAX

But that system is run by people too. And it can be weakened from the inside. In October 2018, a brand-new Boeing 737 MAX crashed into the sea off Indonesia, minutes after takeoff. Less than five months later, a second one crashed in Ethiopia. Three hundred and forty-six people died in the two crashes. In both, new software kept pushing the nose down, because of a single faulty sensor. Many of the pilots flying the MAX hadn't been told the software was there. The evening before the first crash, another crew had faced the same problem on the same aircraft. They switched the system off. The plane flew again the next morning.

Investigators found failures far from the cockpit. In how the aircraft was designed, how it was certified, and how much of that checking the regulator had left to the manufacturer. The slices of cheese were all still there. Some had just been made thinner.

The MAX was grounded for around twenty months. And the lesson went back into the system, like every lesson before it.

10:39Is flying getting more dangerous?

When several crashes make the headlines, it can feel as if flying is getting more dangerous. But a single year is too short to reveal a trend. Aviation safety is measured in decades.

10:54So why?

So why is flying so safe now? Not because planes stopped failing, or pilots stopped making mistakes. But because every failure, and every near miss, is taken apart, written down, and shared with everyone who flies. None of it is there by accident. It's a collection of lessons. And some of them are written in the names of people who never made it home.

We didn't learn to fly safely by never making mistakes. We learned by refusing to make the same mistake twice.

So here's a question for you. Aviation learned to treat mistakes as information, not as something to hide. Where else do you think we need that most? Let me know in the comments.
