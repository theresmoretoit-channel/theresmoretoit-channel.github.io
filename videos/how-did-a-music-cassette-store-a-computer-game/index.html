How Did a Music Cassette Store a Computer Game?

10 min2026-10-24

This video follows one program from the tape into a ZX Spectrum: how a zero and a one became two notes an octave apart, what the steady hum before every game was for, why loading screens appeared in such a strange order, and how a single byte at the end could throw away five minutes of waiting. Along the way: why American kids mostly never heard the noise, how the Commodore 64 and Atari did it differently, the tiny screwdriver everyone used on their tape head, and the radio and TV stations that broadcast software over the air.

The loading signal in this video was generated from the Spectrum's own ROM timing, and a real Spectrum program is inside. It plays in full under the closing credits. Record it, and a Spectrum (or an emulator) will load it.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Not music, a program

You press play on a cassette recorder. And this comes out of the speaker.

It isn't music. And it isn't a game's soundtrack. It is the game. Every squeal is part of a program, and the computer is listening to it. The cassette player has no idea. As far as it knows, it's playing a song. So how do you turn a song into a game? And why could one wrong note throw away five minutes of waiting?

0:33Zero and one as two notes

Here's the trick, on one particular machine: the ZX Spectrum, a British home computer from 1982. It uses two kinds of pulse. A short pair of pulses means zero.

A one is the same pair, with each pulse twice as long, so its tone sits an octave lower.

Send around fourteen hundred bits every second, and you get the noise you just heard. It also means a program full of zeros loads faster than one full of ones. Every one takes twice as long to send.

1:11An American idea

Now, if you grew up in America, you may never have heard it at all. Which is odd, because the idea itself was American. In 1975, at a meeting in Kansas City, computer hobbyists agreed on a way to store data as sound on ordinary audio cassettes. A one was eight quick cycles of a high tone, a zero four slower cycles of a low one. Three hundred bits a second. Early machines like the Apple II and the TRS-80 saved their programs on everyday tape recorders. But American home computing moved on quickly. In 1983, one magazine reported a survey: nine in ten new Commodore 64 owners were buying a disk drive together with the computer.

2:00Why Britain stayed on tape

In Britain, the story went the other way. The Spectrum cost a hundred and twenty-five pounds, and it came with no storage at all. You used whatever cassette player you already had. So for millions of kids across Europe, this noise was the sound of a game about to start.

After enough loads, you could even tell which game it was just from the screech.

2:27Following one game into the computer

So let's follow one game from the tape into the computer. You type LOAD, press enter, and press play. First comes a steady hum, eight hundred cycles a second, for about five seconds. It carries no data at all. It's there so the computer can find the signal and lock onto its rhythm. Then two quick pulses, the sync. They mean: from here on, it's data. The first thing to arrive is a short header. Nineteen bytes: a name of up to ten letters, what kind of file it is, and how long it's going to be. Then a second, shorter hum, two seconds this time, and then the game itself.

Every byte is eight of those notes, one after another, with no gaps between bytes at all. And the computer doesn't hear notes. It times the gap between one edge of the signal and the next. A short pair is a zero. A long pair is a one. That's the whole decoder. And the noise at the start of this video isn't a sound effect. We generated it from exactly these rules, and a real Spectrum program is inside. We'll play all of it at the very end. Record it, and a Spectrum will load it.

3:43The strange loading screen

Most games showed a picture first, and it loaded in a strange order. Thin stripes appeared, eight lines apart, then filled in, line by line, one third of the screen at a time. And the colours came last, sweeping down over everything. That picture alone took about forty seconds. The whole game, around five minutes.

4:06One byte decides

And at the end of each block comes one more byte. While saving, the Spectrum runs every byte of the block through a simple rule called XOR, and records the result as one more byte. While loading, it repeats the calculation. If the results agree, the block arrived intact. If they don't, the Spectrum says this. Five minutes of waiting, and one byte decides it was all for nothing. Rewind, and start again.

Oddly, a broken header gets no error at all. The Spectrum simply ignores it, and keeps listening for the next one.

4:45Other computers, other bets

Other computers made other bets. The Commodore 64 recorded every block twice, so a damaged spot in one copy could be rescued from the other. Safer, but slow. With Commodore's built-in routine, a forty-kilobyte program took about twelve and a half minutes. So game publishers wrote their own faster loaders, some of them up to about ten times faster.

5:12The cassette player problem

And then there was the cassette player. Too quiet, and the edges sink into the hiss. Too loud, and they smear into each other. A tape running slightly fast or slow stretches every pulse, and the computer is measuring time. Worst of all was the azimuth, the angle of the tape head. A tape recorded on one machine might not line up with another. So people pushed a tiny screwdriver into a hole next to the head, and turned it a fraction at a time, listening. It looked like superstition. It was signal tuning.

5:49Anything that carries sound

But notice what the cassette player never had to do. It never understood a single bit. It recorded sound, and played it back. Which means a program didn't really need a cassette. Anything that could carry sound could carry software. It also meant that copying a game was as easy as copying an album. Two cassette decks and a cable.

Atari used both stereo tracks. The program on one, and a voice on the other, talking to you through the TV while the game loaded. In Yugoslavia, a show on Radio Belgrade broadcast programs over the air. Listeners recorded them on tape, around a hundred and fifty programs between 1983 and 1986. In Czechoslovakia, it was television. A program played as sound under the closing credits. And in 1983, the singer Pete Shelley hid a Spectrum program at the end of a vinyl album. Copy it to tape, load it, and the lyrics appeared on screen in time with the songs.

6:56The catch

But there was a catch. The radio could carry the sound to every computer in the country. Only the right one could understand it. Play a Spectrum game to a Commodore, and the Commodore hears noise. The bits only mean something to a machine that knows the format. So Dutch radio tried to fix that. In 1982, it created BASICODE: one sound format, with a small translator program for each kind of computer. And that problem never went away. A file is only useful if something can still read it.

7:34Tape never left

Cassettes lost, of course. Floppy disks were faster, then hard drives, then flash. Except that tape never actually left. Data centres still keep their archives on magnetic tape. One modern LTO-9 cartridge holds eighteen terabytes. Converted to Spectrum speed, that capacity would fill roughly twenty-nine million cassettes, and take more than three thousand years to load.

Still the same idea, though. Patterns on a moving strip of tape, turned back into numbers when you need them.

So next time you hear that screech, remember what it is. Not noise. A program, travelling as sound. And somewhere in 1985, a kid is sitting in front of a television, waiting for it to stop. Because when it does, the game begins.

So here's a question for you. If you ever loaded a game from a cassette, what do you remember most about it? Let me know in the comments.

8:40The program, as promised

And as promised, here's that program. Just like Czechoslovak television, we've put it under the closing credits.
