What GDP Really Measures — And What It Doesn't

10 min2026-09-10

That isn't a glitch. It is what the number was built to do: GDP measures the market value of what gets produced, and rebuilding a town is production. The destruction itself is recorded somewhere else entirely, in an account that never touches GDP. All of the production is counted. None of the destruction is subtracted.

This video works through what GDP actually measures and what it quietly leaves out. Why nominal and real growth are not the same thing. Why an average per person can hide almost everything. Why cooking dinner for your family is worth nothing to the statistics, while paying a restaurant fifty dollars for the same meal is worth fifty dollars.

The full narration of the video.

0:00How a hurricane can raise GDP

Imagine a hurricane destroys thousands of houses. People lose their homes. Roads are damaged. Businesses are destroyed. And yet… GDP can go up. Wait. How can destroying things make an economy look better?

To understand that, we need to talk about one of the most important numbers in economics. GDP.

0:27What GDP actually measures

GDP stands for gross domestic product. In simple terms, it measures the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders, during a certain period of time. Usually, we talk about a year or a quarter. So if factories produce cars, companies provide software services, restaurants serve food, builders construct houses… All of that contributes to GDP. GDP is basically an attempt to answer one question. How much economic production is happening? And that's actually a very useful question.

1:08Why anyone cares

So why does everybody care about GDP? Because it gives us a reasonably standardised way to measure the size and growth of an economy. If GDP is growing, the economy is generally producing more. If GDP is shrinking, economic activity is generally declining. And because we can calculate it repeatedly, we can compare… this year with last year, one country with another, or one period of economic growth with another.

1:39Back to the hurricane

So now we can go back to the hurricane. The hurricane destroys houses. That's terrible. But then people have to rebuild them. Construction companies get paid. Workers get paid. Materials are purchased. Businesses provide services. The government may spend enormous amounts of money repairing infrastructure. All of that is economic activity. And economic activity contributes to GDP. So GDP can increase after a disaster. But nobody would seriously argue that destroying a city made its inhabitants richer. This is an important distinction. GDP measures activity.

It doesn't automatically measure whether that activity was good or bad.

2:31Nominal and real

And that's not the only weird thing about GDP. Imagine that a country produces exactly the same amount of stuff this year as last year. But everything becomes ten percent more expensive. The country's GDP, measured in current prices, can still increase by roughly ten percent. Did the economy actually produce more? No. Prices simply went up. That's why economists distinguish between nominal GDP and real GDP. Nominal GDP uses current prices. Real GDP tries to remove the effect of changing prices. So when you hear that an economy grew by, say, three percent, you usually want to know whether that's real economic growth, rather than simply higher prices.

3:22Per capita, and PPP

Another number you'll often see is… GDP per capita. Total GDP tells you how big an economy is. GDP per capita is more useful when comparing people in different countries. And purchasing power parity, or PPP, tries to account for the fact that a dollar doesn't buy the same amount everywhere. But even GDP per capita adjusted for PPP has a major limitation. It is an average. And averages can hide enormous differences.

3:58A flow, not a stock

GDP is also not the same thing as wealth. This is an easy mistake to make. GDP measures economic production over a period of time. Wealth is about what has accumulated over time. Think of GDP as a flow. And wealth as a stock. A country can produce a huge amount every year without everybody becoming equally wealthy. And a country can have enormous accumulated wealth, even if its current economic growth is relatively slow. So… GDP is not a country's bank account.

4:37The dinner nobody counts

And this is where things start getting weird. Imagine you cook dinner for your family. You spend an hour preparing the food. Your family eats it. You've created something valuable. But as far as GDP is concerned… that hour didn't happen. Now imagine you pay a restaurant fifty dollars to prepare exactly the same meal. Suddenly there is a market transaction. And GDP increases. The meal didn't magically become more valuable. The economic activity simply became easier for the statistical system to measure. The same thing happens with many forms of unpaid work.

Childcare. Cleaning. Cooking. Taking care of relatives. These things can be extremely valuable. But a lot of them don't appear in GDP.

5:32Kuznets warned in 1934

And this isn't a modern criticism of GDP. Simon Kuznets, one of the people who developed national income accounting, warned about this problem almost a century ago. In a nineteen thirty-four report to the US Senate, he explicitly noted that the welfare of a nation could not simply be inferred from a measure of national income. And unpaid household work was among the things left outside the measurement.

6:02What GDP cannot see

And GDP doesn't tell us whether people are happy. It doesn't directly tell us whether people are healthy. Whether they feel safe. Whether they have free time. Whether the environment is clean. Or whether people actually like the way they live. Two countries can have very similar GDP per capita… and radically different quality of life. GDP simply wasn't designed to answer those questions.

6:33War, pollution, crime

And the hurricane wasn't the strangest example. A country at war may produce enormous quantities of weapons, while destroying enormous amounts of wealth. All of the production is counted. None of the destruction is subtracted. There's a whole video about that one.

Pollution, crime, accidents and disease can all generate economic activity too. None of this means these things are good. It simply means that GDP isn't a moral score. It doesn't give you points for doing good things. And it doesn't subtract points for doing bad things.

7:14So is GDP useless?

So… is GDP a terrible economic indicator? No. Quite the opposite. GDP is incredibly useful. If we want to know whether an economy is producing more or less, GDP gives us valuable information. If we want to understand recessions, recoveries and economic growth, it's extremely useful. It is not perfect. But having a reasonably consistent thermometer is much better than having no thermometer at all. The problem isn't GDP. The problem is asking GDP to answer questions it was never designed to answer.

7:55GDP is a thermometer

Think of GDP as a thermometer. A thermometer is great at telling you the temperature. But you would not use it to measure your blood pressure. And you certainly would not use it to determine whether you're happy with your life. GDP works the same way. It tells us something important about the economy. Just not everything that's important about a society.

8:24What three percent means

So the next time you hear… "GDP increased by three percent." Don't immediately translate that into… "People are three percent better off." That's not what it means. It means that, according to this particular measure, the economy produced more economic value than before. And that's useful information. But it's only one piece of the puzzle. Because a country is more than its economy. And economic growth is not automatically the same thing as human progress. GDP is a thermometer. It was never meant to be a complete medical examination.
