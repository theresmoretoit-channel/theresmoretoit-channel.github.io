Why Can't We Compress Everything?

11 min2026-11-16

This video goes through the two tricks inside ZIP and gzip: Jacob Ziv and Abraham Lempel's pointers back to earlier text, and David Huffman's code from a term paper he had almost given up on. Then a little counting puzzle with three-bit files, a 1992 product that claimed to be exempt from the laws of information theory, why JPEG plays a different game, Claude Shannon's guessing game and the limit no lossless code can beat, the Hutter Prize and its half a million euros for squeezing Wikipedia, how Dropbox saved forty-six petabytes on photos it had already stored, a large language model used as a compressor, and the strange case of a million digits of pi.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Every script on this channel, compressed

Here's every script ever written for this channel. A hundred and fifty-eight videos, almost one and a half million bytes of text. Run them through an ordinary compressor called gzip. They come out at just over half a megabyte. Almost two thirds gone. And nothing is lost. Unpack the file, and every script comes back, letter for letter.

0:24Compress it again

Now compress it again. It gets bigger. Ninety-eight bytes bigger. A third time. Ninety-eight bytes bigger. A fourth. Ninety-eight again. The first round throws away two thirds. The second finds nothing at all. So why can't we just keep compressing?

0:46Inside the second file

Let's look inside that second file. gzip didn't compress anything. It copied the data across, unchanged, in sixteen blocks. Each block got a five-byte label. Add a small header and a footer, and you get exactly ninety-eight. When a compressor finds nothing to remove, it stores the data as it is, and adds only what it needs to undo it. So why did the second round find nothing? And would a cleverer program?

1:17Pointing back at repeats

Text like this is full of repetition. The same words, the same phrases, the same little scene markers at the top of every scene. In 1977, Jacob Ziv and Abraham Lempel published a simple trick. Don't write a repeat again. Point back to where it was. Go back three hundred characters, and copy the next twelve. A few bytes instead of twelve.

1:44Huffman's term paper

The second trick: give common letters short codes, and rare ones long codes. In 1951, David Huffman, a graduate student at MIT, could take the final exam, or write a term paper instead. The paper, set by Robert Fano, was to find the most efficient code of exactly this kind. For months, Huffman got nowhere. He gave up, and started studying for the exam. Then it came to him. Take the two rarest symbols, join them into one, and repeat until a single tree is left. He later called it "the most singular moment of my life." Fano hadn't told the class that he'd struggled with it himself, and so had Claude Shannon.

Huffman's code beat both their methods. And it can be proven that no code of its kind does better.

2:38ZIP and gzip

In the early 1990s, Phil Katz put both tricks together for ZIP files. gzip uses the same method. The first round already used up the patterns gzip knows how to find. What's left gives it nothing to work with. To gzip, it looks like noise.

2:58Could a cleverer program do it?

But a cleverer program might see patterns gzip missed. Could some program, someday, shrink anything you give it? No. And you don't need to know how it works to be sure of that. Take every file that's exactly three bits long. There are eight of them. Now count everything shorter. Four files of two bits, two of one bit, and one empty file. Seven. Eight files, and seven shorter places to put them. If every one got shorter, two would have to share a place. And when you unpack it, which one did you mean? It's called the pigeonhole principle, and it holds at any length. There's always one fewer shorter file to go around.

So any compressor that takes every file and shrinks some of them has to make others bigger. Not because it's badly written. There's nowhere else for them to go. Those ninety-eight bytes are how gzip pays that price. Pick any compressor you like. Of all files of a given size, it can shrink fewer than one in two hundred and fifty by a single byte. Compression works anyway, because the files we make aren't random. They're text, pictures and programs, full of patterns.

4:16Software that promised too much

That hasn't stopped people from promising more. In 1992, WEB Technologies announced software that could squeeze almost any data under a kilobyte. Even its own output, again and again. Its vice president said the method was "not subject to the laws of information theory." The product never shipped.

4:39How do photos get so small?

So how do photos and music get so much smaller? They don't play by this rule at all. A JPEG doesn't come back identical. It comes back close enough. Detail your eye won't miss is thrown away for good. Zoom in, and you can see where it went. That's lossy compression, and it's a different game. This video is about the other kind. Every bit back, exactly.

5:08Is there a bottom?

So for files that do have patterns, how small can they go? Is there a bottom? There is. It was found in 1948, in the same paper behind our video on error-correcting codes. There, Claude Shannon showed how adding redundancy protects a message. Here, he worked out how much of it you can take away. His answer was a number he called entropy: how hard it is to guess what comes next. No lossless code can beat that number on average. But you can get as close to it as you like.

5:45Shannon's guessing game

So how unpredictable is English? In 1951, Shannon measured it with a game. He gave someone a passage they'd never seen, and asked them to guess it, letter by letter. If a guess was wrong, they guessed again, until they got it right. People turned out to be very good at it. With a hundred letters of context, English came out at about one bit per letter. A computer stores each letter in eight bits. A human reader needs about one. That gap is the room compression works in. gzip got the channel's scripts down to under three bits a character.

So how close to one bit can a machine get?

6:28The Hutter Prize

There's a prize for exactly that. It's called the Hutter Prize, and since 2020, the task is one file: a gigabyte of English Wikipedia. gzip shrinks it to a third. The best everyday compressors, to about a fifth. The record, set in July 2026, is just over a hundred million bytes. A tenth. And the decompressor counts too. Its size goes into the total, so you can't hide Wikipedia inside the program. The fund is five hundred thousand euros. Every one percent of improvement pays five thousand. Marcus Hutter, who funds it, explains why: "being able to compress well is closely related to acting intelligently."

7:14Programs that predict

Because the programs at the top don't hunt for repeats. They predict. Before each character, they estimate how likely every possible next one is. A good guess costs a fraction of a bit. It's Shannon's guessing game, played by a machine. The best players now use neural networks, trained to predict what comes next. It works even on finished files. In 2016, Dropbox began re-packing its users' JPEGs with a better predictor. On average, over a fifth smaller. Every file came back bit for bit, and by 2017, it had saved over forty-six petabytes. That's over forty-six thousand terabytes.

7:59A language model as a compressor

Then there are the giants. In 2023, researchers at DeepMind used a large language model as a compressor. On small greyscale patches of photos, it beat PNG: forty-eight percent of the original size against fifty-eight. On recordings of read speech, it beat FLAC: twenty-one percent against thirty. But that's without counting the model. Add its size, and the result comes out a hundred and forty times bigger than the data. A compressor bigger than its data hasn't saved anything. The program is part of the description.

8:40A million digits of pi

And that brings us to the strangest part. Here are the first million digits of pi. Feed them to four compressors. The best gets them down to about four hundred and twenty-six thousand bytes. Now give the same compressors a million random digits. The same result, within a tenth of a percent. But pi isn't made at random. It follows a mathematical rule. Here's a program that prints every one of those million digits. Three hundred and sixty-two bytes. Over two thousand times shorter than the file. The compressors couldn't see the mathematical rule behind pi. They could only see the digits.

9:24The shortest program

One way to measure the information in data is to ask: how short can a program be, and still print it? That length is called Kolmogorov complexity, after one of the men who described it. Most data has no short program. It's the pigeonhole principle again: there aren't enough short programs to go around. A million random digits very likely has nothing shorter. Pi does. So why not just find the shortest program for any file? Because it's been proven that no algorithm can do that for every file. Every program gives you an upper limit. But nothing can always tell you whether a shorter one exists. You never know you've reached the bottom.

10:11What gzip can't see

gzip found the repeats in the scripts, and nothing more. It sees bytes, not what the words mean. To go further, a program has to predict them better. And predicting text well starts to look a lot like understanding it. So here's a question for you. If a program could squeeze Wikipedia smaller than anything else, would you say it understands it?
