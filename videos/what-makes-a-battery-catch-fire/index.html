Are Lithium-Ion Batteries Dangerous?

10 min2026-09-17

This video is about what actually has to go wrong. A lithium cell carries its own fuel, sealed inside it. Between the two electrodes sits a plastic film thinner than twenty micrometres — and plastic does not do well when heated. Once a short circuit makes one spot hot, the hole in that film grows, a bigger hole carries more current, more current makes more heat, and nothing in that loop slows it down. That is thermal runaway.

Then the harder half: how often it happens. When Samsung recalled two and a half million phones in 2016, the number of incidents reported in America was ninety-two, out of roughly a million sold there. One in ten thousand was enough to end a flagship phone and get it banned from aeroplanes. We see the failures. We never see the denominator — and in New York, where these fires killed eighteen people in 2023, what changed afterwards was not the chemistry but which batteries were allowed on the market.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Another battery caught fire

Another battery caught fire this week. A power bank on a plane. A scooter left charging in a stairwell. Sometimes an entire building. And the headline is always the same. Lithium-ion batteries are dangerous. There is something odd about that. Because there is one in your pocket. One in your laptop. Probably one parked outside. Billions of them, sitting in drawers and bags and walls, doing nothing dramatic whatsoever. So which is it? Did we quietly hand a dangerous technology to almost everybody on Earth?

0:40It carries its own fuel

Here is the short answer. A lithium cell carries its own fuel. The flammable part isn't in the room. It is sealed inside the battery. That is why a battery fire is so hard to smother, and why it can start again an hour after you think it is out. But the short answer is the boring one. Almost every one of those billions never does anything of the sort.

1:05What has to go wrong first

So the question is narrower than the headline. What has to go wrong first?

It helps to remember how new this is. Sony sold the first commercial lithium-ion cell in 1991. Lithium is the lightest metal there is, so the chemistry stores a great deal of energy for very little weight. And then it got cheap. Between 2010 and 2023, the average price of a battery pack fell by almost ninety percent. Light and cheap is a powerful combination. It is why one technology can sit inside a phone and underneath a car.

1:46Inside a phone battery

From the outside, a phone battery is deeply boring. A flat silver rectangle. Inside, it is not boring at all. There is a positive electrode. A negative electrode. And a liquid electrolyte between them. Charging pushes lithium ions through that liquid, from one electrode to the other. Using the phone lets them drift back. The electrons cannot take that route. So they go the long way round… through your phone. That is the part we actually wanted. And in the middle sits the component nobody ever thinks about.

2:25The separator

The separator. A plastic film that holds the two electrodes apart. In a modern cell it is typically under twenty micrometres thick. A human hair is around seventy. So that is the design. A great deal of energy. A flammable liquid. And a film keeping the halves apart. Enormously useful. Not very forgiving.

2:51Four ways in

Batteries do not usually burn of their own accord. Something has to go wrong first. Crushed. Overheated. Overcharged. Or simply built badly on a Tuesday afternoon.

3:04An internal short

The worst case is an internal short. The two sides meet somewhere they were never meant to meet. And now the energy of the whole cell pours through one tiny spot. That spot gets hot. And here is the awkward part. The separator is plastic. Plastic does not do well when heated. Heat it enough and it softens, shrinks away from the hot spot, and gives way. So the hole gets bigger.

3:32The loop

A bigger hole carries more current. More current makes more heat. More heat opens a bigger hole. Meanwhile the heat is starting chemical reactions in the cell, and those reactions give off heat of their own. Heat makes reactions. Reactions make heat. Nothing in that loop slows it down.

3:52Thermal runaway

It has a name. Thermal runaway.

The battery stops being a power supply and becomes a heater that is feeding itself.

4:02What comes out

The electrolyte boils. Pressure builds. The cell splits open, and what comes out is hot and flammable. And in some chemistries, the breaking electrode gives off oxygen as it goes. Which is the part people find genuinely unnerving. In those cells, the fire has brought some of its own air. Smothering it does very little. Firefighters mostly use water to cool what is around it, and wait for it to finish.

4:31Cells are never alone

Because cells are rarely alone. In a bike or a car they sit shoulder to shoulder, in packs. And a cell in thermal runaway is a very hot lump of metal pressed against the next one along. That is the difference between a phone that has a bad afternoon and a fire that takes the stairwell with it.

5:01So, are they dangerous?

So. Are they dangerous? Yes. And that answer is almost useless. A chainsaw is dangerous. A gas hob is dangerous. The question was never whether a thing can hurt you. It is how often it does. And that is where headlines quietly mislead us.

5:21What rare actually looks like

Here is what rare actually looks like. In 2016, Samsung recalled two and a half million phones because they were catching fire. The recall cost Samsung more than five billion dollars. The number of incidents reported in America was ninety-two, out of roughly a million phones sold there. That is about one in ten thousand. One in ten thousand was enough to end a flagship phone and get it banned from aeroplanes.

5:52Count the batteries around you

Now hold that number in your head and count the batteries around you. When one fails on an aeroplane, or in a block of flats, everybody hears about it. The ones that charged quietly on a bedside table last night are not news.

6:07The denominator

We see the failures. We never see the denominator.

Which turns the question into a better one. Not are lithium batteries safe. Safe enough for what? A phone battery holds about a dozen watt-hours. An electric bike, around five hundred. An electric car carries tens of thousands. The same family of chemistry, entirely different consequences.

6:36A battery is a system

And the cell is not the whole battery. A proper pack has electronics watching it the entire time. Voltage, current, temperature. And the authority to cut the battery off when the numbers stop making sense. So a battery is really a system. Cells, sensors, software, a case, and the charger you plug it into. Which means any one of them can ruin it. A flaw in the separator. A speck of metal in an electrode. A bad weld. A protection circuit left out to save a few pence. And none of that is visible. The cheap pack and the careful pack look identical, right up until one of them doesn't.

7:20What New York did

Which is where New York becomes the interesting example. In 2023, the city recorded two hundred and sixty-eight fires started by these batteries. Eighteen people died. Most of it traced back to one place. Cheap replacement packs for electric bikes and scooters, often rebuilt from salvaged cells. So the city did something distinctly unglamorous. From that September, selling a pack that had not been certified became illegal. The timing is striking. By 2025 the annual death toll had fallen from eighteen to one. The chemistry had not changed. What had changed was which batteries were reaching the market.

8:07Why not just make them safe?

So why not simply make them safe? Because we are asking for everything at once. Small, light, cheap, powerful, quick to charge and long-lived. And the more energy we pack into a small space, the more there is to release when something finally goes wrong. That trade-off is not an engineering failure. It is the entire point of the technology.

8:33What comes next

So what comes next is mostly more of the same, done better. Better separators, better cooling, better ways to catch a flaw before it leaves the factory. Some chemistries already trade energy for calm. Lithium iron phosphate stores less per kilogram and is much harder to push into runaway. Which is why it keeps turning up where weight matters least. A battery bolted to the ground does not care what it weighs. Solid-state batteries get the headlines. Swap the flammable liquid for a solid, and the part that burns is simply not there.

In principle. Those two words are doing an enormous amount of work.

9:16Back to the paradox

So we end up back at the paradox we started with. Batteries did not become dangerous. They became everywhere.

We found a cheap, portable way to keep an extraordinary amount of energy sitting perfectly still.

The hard part was never storing it. It is persuading it to stay put.
