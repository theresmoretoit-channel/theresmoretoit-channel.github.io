Who Runs the Earth? Why There Is No Government Above Governments

13 min2026-09-03

So who is actually above the countries? Nobody.

This video is about what that absence actually means — why international politics is anarchic in the technical sense (no higher authority), why even a hegemon is not a world government, what a Federation of Earth would really need, and why the thing most likely to force our hand isn't an agreement but a problem bigger than the units trying to solve it.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Nobody is above the countries

Imagine that tomorrow, something unexpected happens. One country invades another. And we say: "Enough. This is against the rules. Stop." But… who gets to order them to stop? Who is actually above the countries? The answer is surprisingly simple. Nobody.

There is the United Nations. There are international courts, treaties, and countless international organisations. But there is no world government with its own police and military that can simply say: "This country has broken the rules. And now we are going to stop it." And that is why international politics is, in a sense, anarchic. Not because the world is necessarily chaotic. But because there is no higher authority above sovereign states, with a monopoly on the use of force. And that may be one of the biggest problems in the world today.

1:01How it works inside a country

Imagine an ordinary person. They can't simply say: "I don't like this law, so I won't follow it." If they break the law, there is a police force. If they have a dispute, there is a court. And if someone attacks another person, the state can use force to stop them. Why? Because the state has a monopoly on the legitimate use of force. We don't have to agree with every law. But we all know that there is someone who ultimately has the authority to enforce it. And that is what makes it possible for millions of people to live together under one system.

1:39The same question, one level up

Now let's ask the same question at the international level. Imagine a small country attacks its neighbour. Who comes and arrests its prime minister? Nobody. China invades another country. Who sends the police? Nobody. The United States violates an international treaty. Who can order the United States: "Stop. Fix this immediately." Again, there is no world police force with authority over the United States. Of course, there are international institutions. There are courts. There is the United Nations. There are sanctions. There are diplomatic and military coalitions.

But ultimately, these mechanisms depend on the countries themselves. And that creates a strange situation. Countries create the rules, but countries are also the main enforcers of those rules.

2:37So why not just build one?

At first, it sounds simple. Let's create a world government. Every country gives it some of its powers. We create a world parliament. A world court. A world police force. And a world military. When one country causes trouble, the world government intervenes. Problem solved. Except… which country would agree to that? Imagine the world government tells the United States: "Your military cannot cross this border." And the United States says: "No."

What happens next? If the world government has no force of its own, the order is just a suggestion. If it does have force, then it has to be capable of using that force even against the most powerful countries on Earth. And that is the real problem. For a world government to work, it would actually have to be above the countries.

3:37The United States was not a world government

And here we get to an interesting historical detour. After the collapse of the Soviet Union, the world entered a period when there was one clearly dominant power. The United States. It had an enormous economy. An enormous military. Military bases around the world. Powerful allies. And the ability to project force almost anywhere on the planet. Some people therefore started talking about an American unipolar world. But there is an important difference. The United States was not a world government. It was a hegemon. It had an extraordinary ability to influence other countries. But it did not have universal legal authority over them.

And more importantly: Who had the authority to control the United States? Nobody above it. And that reveals a fundamental problem with every hegemon. If we create one power strong enough to stop everyone else… who stops that power?

4:40What a Federation of Earth would need

Let's imagine that we really wanted to build one. We could create something like a Federation of Earth. Countries would still exist. Brazil would still be Brazil. France would still be France. Japan would still be Japan. But some powers would be transferred to a higher level. For example: wars between countries, weapons of mass destruction, international aggression, certain global environmental problems, and perhaps the protection of global commons. The Federation would have its own courts. Its own police. And most importantly: its own armed forces.

Not an army that countries lend to it when they feel like it. An actual military belonging to the Federation of Earth.

5:29Forty-eight hours instead of sanctions

Imagine that one country invades its neighbour. The world government declares: "This is aggression." And instead of: "We are considering another round of sanctions…" the response is: "Withdraw your forces within forty-eight hours." And if they don't? World forces begin pushing the aggressor back. No months of negotiations. No waiting to see which countries will join. No: "We couldn't reach an agreement in the Security Council." There would simply be a higher authority. Just as a state can tell an individual: "You cannot do this." And if they don't, the police intervene.

6:11And what if it goes wrong?

Because a world government would have enormous power. And that immediately raises another question: What happens if it goes wrong? Today, one government can become authoritarian. People can leave. They can overthrow it. Other countries can provide a counterbalance. There can be democratic allies. But if there is only one world government… where do you escape from it?

Do we all move to Mars? So a world government would need something that may be even more important than its military. Extremely strong checks and balances.

6:51A thin layer, not a ceiling

The ideal system might therefore look more like a federation. The global level would have only clearly defined powers. Countries would keep most of their existing responsibilities. Brazil would still decide how its schools work. France would still decide how its roads are built. Japan would still run its healthcare system. But if one country wanted to start a war against its neighbour, that would no longer be entirely its own business. Because a war between two countries can threaten the entire planet. And that could be the boundary between national and global authority.

7:32What would actually force us

And this is, I think, the most interesting part. Maybe a world government will not appear because one day everyone decides: "A world government would be nice." History suggests something different. Major political changes often happen because a problem appears that is larger than the political units dealing with it.

7:54An artificial signal

Imagine that one day we detect something strange. There is no doubt. It is an artificial signal. And it is coming from another star system. It isn't noise. It isn't a natural phenomenon. Someone is out there. And they send us a message. What do we do? Who answers? The President of the United States? The President of China? The Secretary-General of the United Nations? The European Union? India? Or everyone at once? They probably won't ask who runs one country. They might ask: "Who represents planet Earth?" And suddenly, we have a problem. We don't have anyone.

8:39Who speaks for Earth?

Maybe first contact wouldn't create a world government overnight. But it could create enormous pressure for coordination. We would need a common diplomatic protocol. Joint scientific teams. A common defence strategy. And above all, someone who could say: "This is the official position of humanity." Suddenly, the question: "Why should we have some kind of world authority?" would have a very practical answer. Because someone has to speak for Earth.

9:12It doesn't have to be aliens

We don't have to wait for an extraterrestrial civilisation. The same pressure could come from another global threat. An asteroid on a collision course with Earth. A global pandemic. A technological catastrophe. Or something we cannot even predict today. The more interconnected the world becomes, the more problems there will be that no individual country can solve on its own. And maybe one day we will realise that we have two choices. Either we have two hundred countries trying to negotiate every time something happens.

Or we create a higher level of government for problems that affect everyone.

9:55Add another layer

Thousands of years ago, humans mostly lived in small communities. Then came cities. Then kingdoms. Then nation-states. Then international organisations. And today, we are dealing with problems that are genuinely planetary. Maybe the next step isn't: "Get rid of countries." Maybe it is: "Add another layer above them."

Just as towns didn't disappear when countries emerged. There would simply be another level above them.

10:29But would it really be better?

That is far from certain. A world government could potentially end wars between countries. It could be much more effective at dealing with global problems. But it would also create the most powerful political institution in human history. And if we designed it badly… we could create something much worse than the world we have today. So perhaps the real question isn't: "Do we need a world government?" It is: "Can we ever build a world government that is powerful enough to stop countries, but weak enough that it cannot enslave humanity?"

And that is a hell of a problem.

11:10Nobody to say enough

Today we live in a strange situation. Inside countries, we have laws, courts, and police. But between countries, there is still something resembling anarchy. Countries can negotiate. They can sign treaties. They can create organisations. They can impose sanctions. But there is still nobody above them with the ultimate authority to say: "Enough." Maybe it will stay that way for hundreds of years. Or maybe something will happen that changes it within a single decade. Maybe a catastrophe. Maybe a technological breakthrough. Maybe a common threat. Or maybe…

one day, someone will answer our message.

And humanity will realise that to the rest of the universe, we are not the United States, or China, or Europe, or any of the countries we spend our lives arguing about. We are simply… Earth.

And for the first time, we may have to answer the question: "Who speaks for humanity?"
