Legacy Tech

It Still Works. So Why Upgrade It?

10 min2026-09-03

It's the most popular argument in IT, and it isn't wrong. Windows XP worked. A phone from 2008 still works. So why replace anything?

Because "it works" was never a property of your software. It's a property of your software and everything around it — today. This video follows that idea from a 2G device on a wall, through a water company switching to Water 2.0, to a chair manufacturer that will happily sell you Enterprise Extended Chair Support.

The full narration of the video.

0:00They already know

Imagine a company that has been building software for twenty years. Its programmers know what technical debt is. They know what outdated technology means. They know that support eventually ends. They know that one day the system will have to be upgraded. And yet… …every so often, someone still has to explain why we should upgrade. Welcome to IT.

0:26"But it works"

The most popular argument is: "But it works." And yes. It does. Windows XP worked. A phone from 2008 can still work. A twenty-year-old car can still work. That doesn't necessarily mean you should build the next ten years of your business around it.

0:45It's a property of everything around it

Because "it works" is not a property of your software. It's a property of your software and everything around it. Today.

Software doesn't exist in isolation. Every application depends on languages, frameworks, libraries, operating systems, databases and servers. And all of those things keep changing.

1:09A real example: 2G

Here's a real example. Imagine a device that uses a 2G mobile network. It works perfectly. Then the operator announces: "2G is going away." And you say: "But my device still works!" Yes. But it needs the world around it. And the world has moved on.

1:30Water 2.0

Now let's make this ridiculous. Imagine your water company announces: "Starting next year, we're switching to Water 2.0." Your pipes still work. Your sink still works. Your toilet still works. Everything is fine. Except your plumbing only supports Water 1.0. So you have two choices. Replace your plumbing… or pay extra to keep receiving the old water. Forever. Sounds ridiculous. And yet that is roughly what happens with legacy technology. Old versions stop being supported. New software stops working with them. And eventually you pay extra just to keep something obsolete alive.

2:15Chair 6

Let's make it even more ridiculous. Imagine a company that makes office chairs. They release Chair 8. Your company has Chair 6. It works perfectly. It has four legs. It has a backrest. Everything is fine. Then the manufacturer announces: "Chair 6 is now out of support." No more replacement parts. No more safety fixes. You say: "Why should we buy new chairs? These ones work!" Fair enough. You can keep using Chair 6. But eventually you discover that the new desks don't fit. The replacement legs aren't available. And the only person who knew how to repair Chair 6 retired five years ago.

2:59Enterprise Extended Chair Support

Fortunately, there is a solution. Enterprise Extended Chair Support. For several million a year, the manufacturer will keep making replacement legs for your obsolete chairs. Completely absurd. But replace "chair" with "software"… and suddenly nobody finds it strange.

3:19Except this part is not a joke

Except this part is not a joke. When Windows XP ran out of support in 2014, Microsoft sold exactly that deal. Custom support. Around two hundred dollars per machine for the first year. Double that for the second. The British public sector signed it. Five and a half million pounds for one more year of security updates. A year later they decided not to renew. And the old machines were still there. That is Enterprise Extended Chair Support. It just stops being funny when the invoice is real.

3:59What will the upgrade give us?

This is also why the question: "What will the upgrade actually give us?" can be misleading. A new feature? Users see it. A new button? Users see it. A technology upgrade? Nothing. And that is exactly the point. A successful upgrade has one very boring result: Nothing happens. The application keeps working, only now on supported technology.

4:25Old technology rarely fails by crashing

Although that is not quite the whole truth. Old technology rarely fails by crashing. It fails when the security audit comes back with a finding. When a partner's new interface needs something your platform cannot speak. When the hosting provider retires the image you run on. Or when you try to hire someone and nobody wants the job. None of that is a bug. There is nothing to fix, because nothing is broken. The world simply stopped waiting for you.

4:58Changing the oil

It's like changing the oil in your car. Nobody gets excited about new oil. But skip the maintenance for long enough… and eventually you get a much more exciting problem. Technical debt works the same way. A small upgrade today might mean: Update a few dependencies. Fix a few incompatibilities. Run the tests. Done.

5:21Five years later: archaeology

Wait five years. Now it is: Update the platform. Replace half the libraries. Upgrade the framework. Rewrite that ancient component. And whatever you do… don't touch that file. At that point we are no longer doing an upgrade. We are doing archaeology. "Look! I found a dependency from 2014!" "Don't touch it." "Why?" "We don't know what it does."

5:48Technical debt is also fear

Because technical debt isn't only old software. It's also fear. Fear of changing something nobody fully understands any more.

5:59The blacksmith's horse

And this is where an old saying comes in. The blacksmith's horse goes barefoot. A blacksmith spends his life making shoes for everyone else's horses… while his own horse has none. English usually tells it about the cobbler's children, who have nothing on their feet. Different trade. Same blind spot. Software companies are remarkably good at this. We spend decades explaining to customers why their old systems need to be upgraded… while our own systems keep getting older.

6:32"We don't have time right now"

Because: "We don't have time right now." Next year: "Still not a priority." A few years later: "That's going to be a pretty big upgrade." And eventually: "It's too big to upgrade."

6:47The trap

That is the trap. The longer you postpone, the further your system drifts from the technology around it. And closing that distance gets more expensive every year. And none of this is a misunderstanding. They know it is old. They know support is ending. They know the upgrade will eventually be necessary. It is a business decision.

7:10Two costs, two budgets

The cost of upgrading is visible today. The cost of not upgrading is somewhere in the future. And those two costs don't land on the same person. The upgrade comes out of this year's budget, approved by someone who gets nothing visible in return. The outage comes out of a budget that doesn't exist yet, in a year when somebody else will be sitting in that chair. Humans are remarkably good at sending problems into the future. Unfortunately… the future eventually arrives. Technical debt doesn't disappear when we ignore it. It grows.

7:50When not to upgrade

To be fair, not everything needs upgrading. A system that gets switched off next year can stay exactly as it is. And "there is a newer version" is not an argument on its own. The question was never "is this the latest?" The question is: "when this breaks, will anyone still be able to fix it?"

8:10The healthy version is boring

Which is why the healthy version of this looks disappointingly boring. A few dependencies every release. One framework version at a time. Not a project. Not a migration. Not a two-year programme with a steering committee. Just maintenance. The same way nobody runs a project to change the oil.

8:34Remember 2G, Water 2.0 and Chair 6

So the next time someone asks: "Why should we upgrade? It still works." Remember 2G. Remember Water 2.0. And remember Chair 6. Because "working today" is not the same as "still working in five years". Technology moves on. And if we don't move with it, eventually we are not maintaining our software. We are maintaining a museum.

Which is exactly what the blacksmith's horse was doing. Walking around barefoot… while the blacksmith was busy making shoes for everyone else.
