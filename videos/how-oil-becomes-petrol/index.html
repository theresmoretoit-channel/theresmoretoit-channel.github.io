How Do They Turn Crude Oil Into Petrol?

The full narration of the video.

0:00How petrol appears

You put a nozzle into your car. You press a button. A few seconds later, you've got petrol. Simple. But where did that petrol come from? Was it already hiding inside the crude oil? Did someone just boil the oil until petrol came out? And what about diesel? What happens if the crude oil is really heavy? And perhaps the most important question. If I somehow got my hands on a barrel of crude oil, could I make my own petrol at home?

0:35What a refinery does first

To answer that, we first need to see what a refinery actually does to crude oil. So the crude oil arrives. First, it has to be cleaned up. Water, salts and other unwanted material are removed. Then it's heated. And this is where the classic refinery image appears. The enormous distillation column. It looks like a giant industrial tower. And essentially, it is a giant separation machine.

1:04159 litres in, 170 out

But before we climb into it, one number. A barrel of crude oil is a hundred and fifty-nine litres. American refineries pour in a hundred and fifty-nine litres… and get out about a hundred and seventy. More comes out than went in.

Nobody is cheating. The products are simply less dense than the crude they came from. A refinery is not a machine that takes petrol out of oil. It's a machine that makes new things out of it.

1:36What is inside the barrel

And to see how, we have to look at what is actually inside that barrel. Crude oil is not a liquid version of petrol. And it isn't one chemical compound. It's a mixture of thousands of different hydrocarbons, along with sulphur, nitrogen, oxygen and metals. And they come in wildly different sizes. Some are small. Some are so large and heavy that they behave more like tar. So when we say oil, we're talking about an enormous molecular soup. And not all of that soup is the same.

2:10Light, heavy, sweet, sour

Crude oil is described using two characteristics. One is light versus heavy. Light crude contains more small hydrocarbon molecules. Heavy crude contains more large, complex ones. The second is sweet versus sour. That has nothing to do with taste. Sweet crude contains little sulphur. Sour crude contains more. So two barrels can look similar… while being very different raw materials. Which is one reason why refineries are not all built the same way.

2:45Inside the column

Back to the column. The refinery isn't separating molecules one by one. It's separating groups of hydrocarbons according to their boiling behaviour. The crude is heated to around three hundred and fifty degrees Celsius. Hot vapour rises up the column while cooler liquid flows downward. Different parts of the mixture condense at different levels. Near the top, the lightest hydrocarbons. Gases. Below them, naphtha, boiling up to about a hundred and ninety degrees. Then kerosene, roughly a hundred and ninety to two hundred and fifty. Then the diesel range, very roughly two hundred and fifty to three hundred and fifty.

And at the bottom, the stuff that really doesn't want to evaporate. Heavy oil and residue. Those numbers are deliberately vague, and that isn't me being careless. The fractions overlap, and where one ends and the next begins is something the refinery chooses. It's a setting, not a law of nature.

3:50Why distillation is not enough

And at this point, you might think. Great. We've separated the petrol from the diesel. But… not really.

Here's the big secret of modern refining. A refinery doesn't just separate hydrocarbons. It changes them. Here's why it has to. Take a good, light crude and simply distil it. About twenty per cent of the barrel comes off in the petrol range. Twenty per cent.

Meanwhile, petrol is close to half of everything a refinery sells. And it gets worse. That straight-run petrol has an octane rating of about fifty. Your car wants ninety-five. Too little petrol. And what there is, knocks too easily. Both gaps have to be closed by chemistry.

4:44Cracking

First, quantity. We can break large molecules into smaller ones. This is called cracking. And the name is wonderfully literal. Large hydrocarbon molecules are chemically broken into smaller molecules. One important method is catalytic cracking. Another is hydrocracking, which uses hydrogen under high pressure together with catalysts. This matters most when the refinery is working with heavy crude. Suddenly those enormous molecules aren't something to be sold off cheaply. They're raw material for petrol. So the refinery isn't just asking… what's already in this crude oil?

It's asking… what can we turn it into?

5:30Octane, and what it means

Now for the second gap. Quality. Petrol isn't one chemical. It's blended from several different refinery streams. Some hydrocarbons are reformed into molecules with better properties for petrol engines. Others are rearranged by processes such as isomerisation. And cracked products contribute as well. The result is a carefully controlled mixture. And that octane number measures its resistance to knocking. The scale is beautifully arbitrary. One particular molecule, heptane, was defined as zero. Another, iso-octane, was defined as a hundred. Everything else is measured against those two.

So when you see ninety-five at a filling station, that number isn't telling you how much energy is in the fuel. It's telling you how well it resists igniting before it is supposed to. The petrol in your tank is therefore the result of a whole chain of physical separation, chemical transformation and precise blending.

6:35Diesel

Diesel follows the same story. Some of it comes straight from distillation. The rest is built by the same kind of chemistry. And diesel has its own requirements. Its ignition quality is measured by the cetane number, not the octane number. So diesel isn't simply the stuff that's heavier than petrol. It's a deliberately manufactured fuel.

7:00Where the sulphur goes

There's another problem. Crude oil contains sulphur. And we don't want large amounts of it ending up in modern fuels. So refineries run processes such as hydrotreating. Hydrogen reacts with the sulphur-containing molecules. And here's a nice refinery trick. The sulphur doesn't get thrown away. It comes out the other end as solid, elemental sulphur. Something that started as an undesirable contaminant gets sold as a product.

7:33One barrel, many products

So what actually comes out of that hundred and fifty-nine litres? In American refineries, roughly seventy-four litres of petrol. Forty-seven of diesel and heating oil. Seventeen of jet fuel. And then the rest. Petroleum coke, refinery gas, bitumen, lubricants, feedstocks for the chemical industry, and sulphur. Which is why saying… one barrel gives you so many litres of petrol… is misleading. A refinery isn't a petrol factory. It's a chemical factory making many things at once.

8:11Could you do it at home?

And now we arrive at the question we started with. Suppose you had a barrel of crude oil. Could you make your own petrol? In principle… yes. Nothing about it violates the laws of physics. You can heat crude oil. You can separate fractions by boiling point. You can transform hydrocarbons. But there's a rather large problem. Actually… There are several. You might think. I'll just build a still. Heat the crude oil. Collect whatever boils off in the petrol range. Done. But you already know what you'd get. About a fifth of the barrel, at octane fifty.

You haven't removed the sulphur. You haven't produced a fuel that behaves correctly in a modern engine. So simple distillation gets you part of the way. But only part of the way.

9:04Building a real refinery

Fine. I'll build a real refinery. Okay. Let's add catalytic cracking. And hydrocracking. And reforming. And isomerisation. And hydrotreating. Let's add hydrogen production. High-pressure equipment. Catalysts. Heat exchangers. Distillation columns. Compressors. Pumps. Control systems. Storage tanks. Safety systems. Waste treatment. And laboratories to continuously analyse the products. Congratulations. You have just started building… a refinery.

And suddenly the reason refineries are enormous becomes obvious. They aren't enormous because someone couldn't find a sufficiently large cooking pot. They're enormous because that is what modern fuel takes.

9:55What is really in your tank

And here's the part I find most fascinating. When you put petrol into your car, you aren't putting crude oil into it. You aren't even putting one particular substance into it. You're putting a carefully engineered mixture of hydrocarbons. Some molecules may have started as light components of the original crude. Others were created by breaking larger molecules apart. Others were chemically rearranged. So the petrol in your tank is not something that was found inside the Earth. It is something that was manufactured from crude oil.

Not liquid dinosaur juice. Not simply distilled crude oil. But the end product of one of the most complicated industrial processes humans have developed. All so that, eventually, you can put a nozzle into your car, press a button, and drive away.

Not bad for a barrel of black goo.
