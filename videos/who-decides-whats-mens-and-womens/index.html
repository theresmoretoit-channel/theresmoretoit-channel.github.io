Ordinary Things, Awkward Questions

Who Decides What's Men's and What's Women's?

15 min2026-09-25

So where do the rules come from? Not from anyone in particular — from a loop that runs between manufacturers, retailers, advertisers and us, and that keeps going because everyone keeps taking part in it.

Along the way: a trade magazine that told shops pink was the stronger, more decided colour and therefore better for boys; a Roman law that banned trousers inside the city; and a Paris ordinance that made a woman ask the police for permission to wear them. Same garment, fourteen centuries apart, two laws pointing opposite ways.

The full narration of the video.

0:00An invisible line in every clothing shop

Somewhere in every clothing shop there is an invisible line. On one side of it, a sign says MEN. On the other side, WOMEN. Nobody guards that line. Nobody asks to see your papers when you step across it. And yet almost everybody knows exactly where it runs. This is men's clothing. That is women's clothing. This colour is masculine. That one is feminine. So somebody must have drawn it. Some committee. Some international body. The Ministry of Trousers, perhaps. There isn't one. There is no authority anywhere that decides what counts as men's clothing and women's.

No committee. No register. No standard. No inspector. The rules are real enough that breaking one gets you stared at. And nobody ever voted on them. So where exactly do they come from?

Start with the honest part. Some of the difference is practical.

1:01The honest part: some of the difference is practical

Bodies are shaped differently. Cuts follow shapes. Preferences are real. But walk back into that shop and look at what actually separates the two halves of it. A different colour. A different pattern. A different label. Or simply a different aisle. Does the fabric know which aisle it was hung in? Does a pair of trousers change when somebody carries it across the shop? Of course not. The difference is not in the cloth. It is something we added to it.

Which leaves a better question than who invented all this. Who keeps it alive?

1:45The loop: who keeps it alive

The answer is not one person. It is not even one industry. It is a loop. A manufacturer designs a product. Before anything else, they have to decide who it is for. So they look at what people buy. And people buy men's things and women's things. So the manufacturer makes men's things and women's things. A retailer has to lay out a shop. Customers expect two sections. So the shop gets two sections. Marketing has to sell whatever arrives. So it tells us who each thing is for. Men's. Women's. Masculine. Feminine. Customers see those categories everywhere they look.

And they start to expect them. Then manufacturers study those expectations, and design for them. Retailers follow the manufacturers. Marketing follows the retailers. People follow the marketing. And around we go. People create expectations. Businesses answer them. Businesses reinforce them. People learn them. And then the businesses point at the people and say: that is what customers want. And the people point at the businesses and say: that is just how things are. And the circle closes. So who started it? Possibly nobody. And that is the strange part. A system like this does not need anyone in charge.

It only needs everybody to keep taking part in it.

3:31Why the companies are not the villain

Which is where the companies come into the story. It would be easy to tell it like this. Big firms invented gendered clothing and forced it on the rest of us. That is not really how it works. Companies are not standing outside the culture and aiming at it. They are selling things to people who are already inside it. They watch what we buy. What we ignore. What we click on. What makes somebody think: that one is for people like me. And if splitting a product in two sells more of it, there is every reason to keep going. Not because of a grand plan. Sometimes it is simply good business.

If a label helps sell the thing, the label stays.

4:19The same object, two different promises

And marketing can make that split much sharper. Take a single product. A razor. A pen. A bicycle. One advertisement says: POWER. PERFORMANCE. DURABILITY. Another says: BEAUTY. ELEGANCE. SOFTNESS. The same thing. Two completely different promises. And suddenly the thing does not just have a function. It has an identity. One we are expected to recognise. Sometimes those differences are useful. Sometimes they are genuine. And sometimes the difference is mostly in our heads. But once you have seen the same message a few thousand times, it stops feeling like a message.

It starts feeling like a fact.

5:07How we learned the map

And this is where we come in. Picture a man walking past you in a skirt. Something in your head says: that is women's clothing. But why? Nobody ever sat you down and explained the rule. You learned it by watching. Your parents. Your classmates. Television. Films. Adverts. Shop windows. Strangers in the street. You saw who wore what. And you saw how people reacted when somebody crossed the line. So you learned the map. Men over here. Women over there. You do not have to think about it any more. It runs by itself. And when somebody breaks it, it can feel wrong, even when you cannot say why.

So the next time you see a man wearing a skirt, don't laugh. Don't stare. Ask yourself why it feels unusual. Maybe it's feminine only in our heads.

6:08Familiarity is not nature

Because we have a bad habit. We confuse familiarity with nature. Something can feel ancient and obvious simply because we have seen it our whole lives. People say men have always worn this. Women have always worn that. So how far back does always actually go?

In 1918, an American trade magazine for children's clothing told shops the following. Pink is the stronger, more decided colour, so it is more suitable for the boy. Blue is more delicate and dainty, so it is prettier for the girl. Exactly the wrong way round, printed as ordinary retail advice, just over a hundred years ago. And before you conclude that everybody did it that way, they didn't. Nine years later, Time magazine listed what the big American department stores were advising. Some said pink for boys. Some said exactly the opposite.

They were not following a rule. There wasn't one yet. The version we grew up with did not settle in until about the 1950s.

7:19When high heels were a man's shoe

Or take high heels. They reached Europe from Persia, where cavalry wore them to brace against the stirrups. In the 1600s in Europe, a heel was a man's shoe. A sign of status. A sign of masculinity. At the French court, a red heel meant you were close to the king. Nobody at the time thought heels were feminine.

7:43Rome: a tunic to the knee, and no trousers

Or trousers. In ancient Rome, a grown man wore a tunic down to the knee. Bare legs. No trousers. If you saw it in the street today, you would call it a dress. Rome had a line too. It just ran somewhere else. Their line was length. A man's tunic stopped at the knee. A longer one got him called effeminate. And trousers were what foreigners wore. Gauls. Germans. Persians. In 397, that became law. No trousers inside the city of Rome. The penalty was exile.

8:22Paris, 1800: permission to wear trousers

Paris had an ordinance requiring a woman to ask the police for permission to wear them. A doctor's note as well. Renewed every six months. It was issued in 1800. A French senator asked about it. The government answered in 2013. It had quietly lost all force a very long time ago. Not repealed. Just left behind by everything that had changed around it. And yes, that was a real law. You have just seen two of them. One banned trousers. The other made you ask permission. But look at what that one never had to do. It never had to explain why trousers were men's.

Everybody already agreed. The law was written on top of something that was there first. Laws like that enforce a convention. They don't invent it. And when the convention moved, the law just sat there.

9:25The boundaries move

Fashion changes constantly. Colours change. Silhouettes change. Hair changes. Shoes change. Things that were masculine become feminine. Things that were feminine become masculine. The boundaries move. Sometimes slowly. Sometimes very fast. And while we are living inside one particular version of them, they feel permanent.

9:51Invented does not mean harmless

Now, here is the part that is easy to get wrong. Saying that a convention is invented does not make it imaginary. The consequences are very real. A man in a skirt can still be laughed at. A woman in something considered masculine can still be judged for it. A child can be told that a colour is for girls. An adult can feel embarrassed buying something from the wrong half of the shop. None of that is law. No police force enforces it. The enforcement is social. Embarrassment. Ridicule. Disapproval. The raised eyebrow. The joke. The question: why are you wearing that?

And most of the time, just the quiet fear of what other people will think.

10:42The loop can also turn the other way

But if all of this is held up by a loop, then the loop can also turn the other way. Somebody does something unusual. People see it. And somebody else thinks: actually, I like that. They try it. Other people see them. The strange thing becomes slightly less strange. More people try it. Shops notice the demand. Manufacturers answer it. Marketing changes its language. The thing becomes easier to find, so more people see it. And eventually, what looked ridiculous looks completely ordinary. The convention changed. Not because anybody officially changed the rules.

Because enough people stopped treating the old ones as absolute.

11:33A better question than men's or women's

Which brings us back to the question on the wall of that shop. Is this men's clothing or women's clothing? Sometimes that is simply the wrong question. A better one might be: what is this clothing actually for? Does it fit? Is it comfortable? Does it do the job? Do you like it? Does it make you feel good? If the answer is yes, what exactly is the label adding? And now the distinction that matters most. Nobody is saying that men and women have to dress identically. People are allowed preferences. Cultures are allowed traditions. Fashion is allowed categories.

There is nothing wrong with any of that. The trouble starts when a category stops being a description and turns into a restriction. When women's clothing stops meaning clothing usually marketed to women, and starts meaning clothing you are not allowed to wear. Those are two completely different statements. One of them describes a convention. The other one builds a cage.

12:44So who decides?

So who decides what is men's and what is women's? Manufacturers have a part in it. Retailers have a part. Advertisers have a part. Designers. Our families. Our friends. And so do we. Sometimes on purpose. Usually not. Which is why there probably isn't a single person to blame. There doesn't have to be. The system keeps going because it copies itself. We learn the categories. We expect them. Companies answer the expectation. Their products and their advertising teach the categories again. And the next generation learns them from us.

Round and round.

13:31The one weakness every convention has

But a convention has one enormous weakness. It only works as long as people believe that it matters. Which means you don't have to bring the whole thing down. You don't have to convince anybody. You don't even have to stop wearing men's or women's clothing. You only have to remember what these categories actually are. They are conventions. Not laws. Not physics. Not biology. Not morality. Just conventions.

14:04Who was holding the pencil

And if one day you wear something that convention says belongs to the other side, you haven't committed a crime. You haven't broken reality. You haven't hurt anybody. You crossed an invisible line. And maybe the line was never really there. Maybe we just kept drawing it. Over and over again. Until we forgot that we were the ones holding the pencil.

And if you ever see a skirt you actually like, why not dare to wear one yourself?
