Physics That Sounds Simple Until You Ask

What If Space Had a Fourth Dimension?

10 min2026-10-18

In 1884 a London headmaster, Edwin Abbott, worked out how to think about it in Flatland, a book narrated by a square who has no idea that "up" exists. Using the same trick, we look at what a four-dimensional being could do with a locked safe, what a four-dimensional ball or cube would look like passing through your room, and why August Möbius noticed in 1827 that a fourth dimension could turn a left shoe into a right one. H. G. Wells and Liu Cixin turned it into fiction. But there's a catch, and it says a lot about whether such a direction could exist, and where it might be hiding.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Someone inside a locked room

Imagine you're sitting at home. The door is locked. The windows are shut. And suddenly, someone is standing in your living room. They didn't pick the lock. They didn't break through the wall. From where they stand, nothing strange happened at all. They just took a route you can't take. Because suppose space had one more direction. Not time. Not another universe. A real direction, like left, forward, or up.

0:27Flatland, 1884

It sounds like fantasy. But the trick behind it was worked out in 1884, by a London headmaster. His name was Edwin Abbott, and his book was called Flatland. Its narrator is a square. He lives on a flat plane, and he has no idea that "up" exists. In Flatland, a house is a closed outline. A line drawn around you is a wall. Inside it, you are sealed off from everyone. Then, one evening, a stranger appears inside his closed house. His wife can't understand how it got in. It's a sphere, from our world. The square can only see the slice that cuts through his plane. So the sphere looks like a circle that shrinks to a dot, vanishes, and grows back out of nothing.

And the sphere sees things no one in Flatland can. Every house. Every locked chest. Even the square's own insides. All of it lying open to view. To prove it, the sphere touches him in the stomach. From the inside. Without crossing a single line. Then it lifts the square out of the plane. He doesn't pass through any wall. He goes up, in a direction his world doesn't have. Now turn the book around. We are the flat ones. A fourth direction would be one we can't point to, or even picture. And to anyone who could move in it, our walls would be like lines on paper.

2:03What could a 4D being do?

So what could such a being do? Think of a safe. Steel on every side. To a four-dimensional being, it would be open, the way a square drawn on paper is open to you, from above. It could see the money inside, take it out, and leave the safe locked.

2:20Liu Cixin's Death's End

Liu Cixin takes this idea about as far as it goes. In Death's End, the last book of the trilogy that begins with The Three-Body Problem, a spaceship crew drifts into a region where space has four dimensions. Suddenly, they can see inside objects that were completely sealed off before. It's fiction, of course. But the geometry underneath is real.

2:45A 4D object passing through our world

Now picture a four-dimensional object passing through our world. We could never see all of it. Only the slice that's in our space, the way the square only saw a slice of the sphere. A four-dimensional ball would appear in your room as a dot. It would swell into a ball. Then shrink. And vanish. A four-dimensional cube, pushed through corner first, would be stranger still. A tiny pyramid that grows, cuts off its own corners, and turns into an eight-sided diamond, before shrinking away again. To us, it would look like a shape-shifting object appearing out of thin air. To the object, it's just moving in a straight line.

3:29Turning a left shoe into a right shoe

And a fourth dimension allows one more trick. Take your left shoe. However you turn it, it never becomes a right shoe. They're mirror images, and in three dimensions, no rotation gets you from one to the other. But think of a paper cutout of the letter R. Slide it around the table all you like, it stays an R. Lift it off the table, flip it over, and it's backwards. In 1827, the mathematician August Möbius noticed the same thing one level up. In a space with four dimensions, an object could be turned into its own mirror image.

Lift your left shoe into the fourth direction, turn it, set it back down, and it's a right shoe.

4:13H. G. Wells and The Plattner Story

H. G. Wells turned that into a story. In The Plattner Story, from 1896, a schoolteacher is blown out of our space by an explosion. When he comes back, his heart beats on the right side of his body. And he can only write from right to left, with his left hand.

4:32The catch

So far, a fourth dimension sounds like a superpower. But there's a catch. A fourth direction wouldn't just be somewhere for us to go. Everything else could spread into it too. Including gravity. In our world, gravity follows a simple rule. Twice as far away, and it's four times weaker. Here's why. Picture the Sun's pull spreading out in every direction, over the surface of a growing sphere. Double the distance, and that surface is four times larger. The same pull is spread four times thinner. Now give space a fourth direction. The pull spreads over the surface of a four-dimensional sphere. And that surface grows faster. With the cube of the distance.

Twice as far, eight times weaker. Ten times as far, a thousand times weaker. That sounds like a detail. It isn't. In 1917, the physicist Paul Ehrenfest asked what this would do to the planets. Working with Newton's laws, he found there would be no stable orbits.

5:30Ehrenfest and the planets

A planet stays in orbit because two things balance. Gravity pulls it in. Its own motion carries it outward. In our space, if a planet drifts a little farther out, that outward push fades faster than gravity does. So the planet is pulled back. The orbit corrects itself. With a fourth dimension, in that simple picture, both fade at exactly the same rate. Nothing corrects anything. A little too slow, and the planet spirals into its star. A little too fast, and it drifts away for ever. Ehrenfest argued that atoms would have the same problem, because the electric force that holds them together would spread out the same way. Later physicists, using quantum mechanics, came to the same conclusion, at least with electricity as we know it.

So a big fourth dimension wouldn't just give us a new direction. It could take away the solar system. And maybe the atoms we're made of.

6:42Why it's a clue

Which means this is no longer a story about magic. It's a clue.

Earth has been circling the Sun for four and a half billion years. It hasn't spiralled in. It hasn't drifted off. So a large fourth dimension, one that ordinary matter can move through and gravity can freely spread into, is in sharp conflict with the universe we actually see.

But that's not the same as saying there's no fourth dimension at all. Physicists have found at least two places it could hide.

7:17Curled up too small to see

The first idea is a century old. In 1921, Theodor Kaluza added a fourth direction of space to Einstein's equations. And in 1926, Oskar Klein suggested why we don't notice it. It's curled up into a circle, far too small to see. Think of a garden hose seen from across a field. It looks like a line. Only up close do you see that it's round.

7:45Stuck to our three dimensions

The second idea is newer. What if the extra direction is large, but we're stuck to our three dimensions, like ink on a page? That answers a question Flatland never asks. A direction can exist, and you still might not be able to step into it. Atoms, light, people, all of it could be stuck. And maybe only gravity leaks out.

8:08Testing it

In 1998, three physicists pointed out that extra dimensions like that could be as large as a millimetre, and nobody would have noticed. Because nobody had measured gravity at such small distances. So people went and measured it. Today, gravity's simple rule has been checked at distances thinner than a human hair. So far, it still holds. At the Large Hadron Collider, physicists look for energy that goes missing from a collision, as if something had slipped out of our three dimensions. So far, no convincing evidence of extra dimensions has emerged.

8:48Where we are now

So here's where we are. A big fourth dimension, the kind you could walk into, doesn't fit the universe we see. A small one, or a hidden one, is still an open question. And it's one we can test.

Remember the square. He spent his whole life in a world with a missing direction, and never noticed, until something came to visit. If our world has a missing direction, nothing has come to visit. Not that we've noticed. So the real question isn't only what the world would look like from there. It's why, if it's there, we still haven't found it. That's where we're going next.
