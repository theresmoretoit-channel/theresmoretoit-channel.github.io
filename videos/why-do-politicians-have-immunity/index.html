Why Do Politicians Have Immunity?

9 min2026-10-02

That day shows why parliaments need protection from those in power. But how do you protect politicians from persecution without putting them above the law?

This video separates two very different kinds of immunity: protection for how politicians vote and what they say in parliament, and protection for the politician personally, even from charges that have nothing to do with the job. It follows the wording from the English Bill of Rights of 1689 to the US Constitution, compares the United States, Britain and the Czech Republic, and looks at one case that ended up before two parliaments: the Stork's Nest case of Andrej Babiš and Jana Nagyová.

The full narration of the video.

0:00January 1642

In January 1642, King Charles the First of England walked into the House of Commons. Armed soldiers waited at the door. He had come to arrest five members of Parliament, for treason. But the five had been warned, and they were already gone. The king asked the Speaker where they were. The Speaker knelt, and said: "I have neither eyes to see, nor tongue to speak in this place, but as this House is pleased to direct me." The king left empty-handed. Behind him, the members shouted one word: "Privilege! Privilege!"

The problem that day is older than any modern constitution. What happens when those in power can arrest the people who are meant to check them? One answer democracies came up with is parliamentary immunity. Which raises a harder question. How do you protect politicians from persecution… without putting them above the law?

1:00Why parliaments need protection

Start with the obvious danger. The government wants to pass a controversial law, and the vote is going to be close. It controls the police. So the night before the vote, a few opposition members get arrested. The government doesn't need to win the argument. It only needs to remove the people who were going to vote against it. That's the first thing immunity is for. Parliament has to be able to work without the government deciding who gets to show up.

1:33The Bill of Rights

Forty-seven years after the king came for the five, England wrote a rule into its Bill of Rights. Speech and debate in Parliament "ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or place out of Parliament." Remember that wording. It will come back.

1:51Voting without fear

The second danger is quieter. Say Parliament passes a law that costs a big company ten million dollars. You voted for it. The company knows exactly who you are, and it can afford far more lawyers than you. If it could sue you personally for that vote, your question would no longer be "Is this law good for the country?" It would be: "What will this vote cost me?" That's why many democracies protect legislators from legal consequences for how they vote, or what they say in the chamber. Notice who that really protects. Not the politician. The institution. Voters need their representatives to vote without fear.

2:35Two very different immunities

But there's a second, very different kind of immunity, and this is where the argument starts. There's a big difference between saying "You can't punish me for how I voted"… …and saying "You can't prosecute me, because I'm a member of Parliament." The first protects the job. The second protects the person, even for things that have nothing to do with the job. Democracies disagree sharply about how much of the second one to allow.

3:11The United States

Take the United States. The Constitution says members of Congress, for any speech or debate in either House, "shall not be questioned in any other place." Sound familiar? It's the English Bill of Rights, carried across the Atlantic. American courts read the clause broadly. It covers votes, committee work, reports, the whole business of making laws. But that's where it stops. A senator who commits an ordinary crime can be investigated and charged like anyone else. Nobody has to ask Congress first. The American answer, roughly: protect the lawmaking. Not the lawmaker.

3:51Britain and Europe

Britain, where it all started, goes the same way. A British MP accused of an ordinary crime has no general immunity from prosecution. Across Europe, the rules vary enormously, from almost nothing to protection from arrest and prosecution.

4:09The Czech Republic

The Czech Republic goes further. Its Constitution protects votes and speeches too. But it adds a second layer. A member of Parliament can't be criminally prosecuted without the consent of their own chamber. For any crime, even one that has nothing to do with Parliament. And if the chamber says no, the prosecution is blocked for as long as the mandate lasts. There is a real argument for this. A government could invent charges to get rid of an opposition leader. Even an investigation can be a weapon, if the police and prosecutors answer to the wrong people. The chamber's vote is meant to catch that.

But it creates an obvious problem. The people deciding whether a politician can be prosecuted are politicians themselves.

5:01Andrej Babiš and the Stork's Nest case

Which brings us to Andrej Babiš. A billionaire businessman, and, since December 2025, the Czech Prime Minister. For years, he has faced charges in what's known as the Stork's Nest case. Prosecutors say a farm and conference resort obtained about two million euros in EU funding meant for small businesses by falsely presenting the company as a small, independent business. Babiš denies any wrongdoing. Each time he was elected to Parliament, the case needed the Czech lower house's consent again. And three times, in 2017, 2018 and 2022, it said yes.

The case went to court. He was acquitted twice, and twice the appeal court overturned the acquittal. In June 2025, it went further. It bound the lower court to find both defendants guilty, based on the evidence already heard. Then, in October 2025, his party won the election. A new mandate meant a new vote. This time, his coalition had the majority.

6:12A vote, not a verdict

In March 2026, a hundred and four MPs voted against allowing the prosecution. Eighty-one voted for it. One abstained. That vote did not declare him innocent. It wasn't a verdict at all. It means that for the rest of this term, the courts cannot go on with his case. When his mandate ends, it can resume.

6:37Same case, two parliaments

And the same case produced an almost perfect comparison. Babiš wasn't the only defendant. His former close aide, Jana Nagyová, was charged alongside him. She is a member of Parliament too, of the European Parliament. In April 2025, it voted to lift her immunity. After the Czech vote, the two cases were split, and hers went on without him. In May 2026, the court found her guilty of subsidy fraud. A three-year suspended sentence, and a fine of half a million Czech crowns, about twenty thousand euros. In August, the appeal court upheld it.

That tells us nothing about how Babiš's own case would have ended. But it shows exactly what immunity can do. Same case. Two parliaments. One said yes. The other said no.

7:32So why do politicians have immunity?

So, why do politicians have immunity? Not because they matter more than anyone else. A parliament has to be able to vote against the powerful, the government included, without politicians being punished for doing their job. That part is easy to defend. The hard part is everything beyond it. When the accusation has nothing to do with Parliament, should a politician still be protected? For how long? And who gets to decide?

Charles the First showed why Parliament needs protection from those in power. The Stork's Nest shows what can happen when that protection depends on politicians themselves. So the real question isn't whether politicians should have immunity. It's what exactly that immunity should protect. Your vote? Your speech? Your right to oppose the government? Or yourself… when you're accused of an ordinary crime? Democracy needs politicians who are free to make hard decisions. It also needs politicians who answer to the law. Finding the line between those two is the real problem of immunity.
