Where Did the Idea of Hell Come From?

12 min2026-11-07

The older books of the Bible don't have a hell at all. This video follows how the idea came together over thousands of years: a court in Egypt, a sentence in Plato, a motive in Judea, a valley outside Jerusalem, and pictures from a book that came very close to being in the Bible. It doesn't tell you whether hell is real. No historian can.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Hell has an address

Hell has an address. It's a valley on the edge of old Jerusalem, just below the city walls. You can walk through it today. In summer, it hosts concerts. In 1987, Bob Dylan played there. In Hebrew, it's Ge Hinnom, the Valley of Hinnom. In the Greek of the New Testament, that became Gehenna. In the Arabic of the Quran, very probably, Jahannam. Most English translations turn both of those words into one: hell. Over about two thousand years, for billions of people, the name of that valley came to mean one thing: a place of fire for the wicked.

So how does a valley you can walk through become hell?

0:44No hell at all

Here's the strange part. In the older books of the Bible, the people living next to that valley don't have a hell at all. Their scriptures do have a place for the dead. It's called Sheol. It's dark. It's silent. And everyone goes there. The Bible even shows you. King Saul, abandoned by God, has a medium call up the dead prophet Samuel. Samuel is annoyed at being disturbed. Then he tells the king: tomorrow, you and your sons will be with me. The prophet, and the king God rejected. Same place. Nobody is rewarded. Nobody is punished.

In ancient Mesopotamia, how you fared among the dead had little to do with how you'd behaved. It depended on how many sons you had, because sons brought offerings. A Sumerian poem spells it out. One son, and you sit and weep. Seven sons, and you get a throne among the junior gods. So what happened to bad people when they died? Nothing special. Being bad wasn't what counted.

1:54It had to be built

Hell wasn't there from the start. It had to be built. And nobody built it all at once. You can follow it through five parts, over thousands of years. Whether any of it is true, no historian can tell you. How much each culture borrowed from the last is still debated. But the parts are there to see.

2:16Part one: a court

Part one is a court. Egypt, about three and a half thousand years ago. You die, and you're brought before the gods. You swear you haven't committed any of forty-two sins. Then your heart is weighed against a feather. If it fails, a monster called Ammit is waiting, part crocodile, part lion, part hippo. It eats the heart, and your afterlife is over. It's one of the oldest versions of an idea we now take for granted: how you live decides what happens next. There was just one loophole. Another spell spoke directly to your heart, and begged it not to testify against you.

You could have it written on your scroll. A scroll could cost perhaps half a labourer's yearly pay. So one of the first moral courts in history already came with a paid workaround. What it didn't have was forever. Egyptian texts also describe lakes of fire, but the fire was there to destroy you. The worst fate was to stop existing.

3:21Part two: a sentence

Part two is a sentence. Athens, around 380 BC. Plato has Socrates tell a story. After death, a judge inspects the soul, stripped of every title and honour, and sends the bad to Tartarus, labelled curable or incurable. The curable are improved by punishment. The incurable suffer forever, as a warning to others. And Plato gives an example. Not a legend. Archelaus, a real king of Macedon, said to have murdered his way to the throne. Keep that in mind. A real, powerful man, named in a story about who gets punished.

4:03Part three: a motive

Part three is the one that matters most. It's a motive. Remember, those older books had no hell. Justice was supposed to be done here, in this life. The Book of Deuteronomy spells out the deal. Obey, and you're blessed. Disobey, and the curses come for you. The trouble with that deal is that anyone can check it. The Book of Ecclesiastes did. There are just people, it says, who get what the wicked deserve, and wicked people who get what the just deserve. Some people do terrible things, and die old, rich and comfortable. So, more than two thousand years ago, someone wrote down an answer.

It's in a text called First Enoch, which most Bibles don't include. An angel shows Enoch the hollow places where the dead wait. There's a section for the righteous. And there's a section for sinners. But not all sinners. It's for those who die, and, quote, "judgement has not been executed on them in their lifetime". Listen to that again. Not a place for sinners. A place for sinners who got away with it.

5:17When the deal broke

Then the deal broke completely. In 167 BC, Antiochus the Fourth, the Greek king ruling over Judea, moved to crush traditional Jewish worship. Now it wasn't just that good people failed to prosper. They were being killed for keeping the law. The Second Book of Maccabees tells of a mother and seven sons, killed one after another, for refusing to eat pork. One son, dying, tells the king: you will have no resurrection to life. Under the old deal, none of this made sense. Either God had failed them, or the story wasn't over. In those same years, the Book of Daniel was finished. Its last chapter says:

"Many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt." The dead were no longer equal. And the verdict had no end.

6:16Part four: the location

Part four is the location. Back to that valley below Jerusalem. Why that one? Because it already had the worst reputation in the country. Jeremiah accuses people of building a shrine there, "to burn their sons and their daughters in the fire". Archaeology hasn't confirmed those burnings. But the reputation didn't need proof. And then Jeremiah makes a threat, about that same spot. It will be called the Valley of Slaughter, and the dead will be buried there until there's no room left. The place of the crime becomes the place of the punishment.

The very last verse of Isaiah imagines people going out to look at the corpses of the rebels: "their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched". And First Enoch describes an accursed valley beside a holy mountain, "for those who are accursed for ever". Fire. Corpses. A curse. And a real place, just outside town.

7:18More than a valley

By the time of Jesus, Gehenna meant more than a valley. It was the name for the place where God punishes the wicked. In the Gospel of Mark, he warns of Gehenna, and quotes that line from Isaiah about the worm and the fire. The word appears twelve times in the New Testament, eleven of them in the words of Jesus. You may have heard that Gehenna was the city's rubbish dump, always burning. It's a neat explanation. But it's first found around 1200 AD, in a rabbi's commentary, and no direct evidence for it has turned up.

7:54Where the word comes from

The word hell itself comes from somewhere else entirely. It's an old Germanic word, meaning something like the concealed place. A world of the dead. There's no fire in the word. Then the King James Bible used it for Gehenna, for Greek Hades, for Tartarus, and about half the time, for Sheol. So when Jesus warns of hell in an English Bible, the word underneath is usually Gehenna. The name of a valley.

8:23Part five: the pictures

Part five is the pictures. Picture hell. You probably see torture, demons, and the devil in charge. But in the Book of Revelation, the devil isn't running the place. He's thrown in. The oldest detailed Christian tour of hell we have is another book, the Apocalypse of Peter, written between 100 and 150 AD. Blasphemers hang by their tongues. Usurers stand to their knees in filth. Persecutors burn up to the waist. It came very close to being in the Bible. One of the oldest lists of Christian scripture accepts it alongside Revelation, while admitting some didn't want it heard in church.

In the end, it stayed out of the Bible. Its pictures lived on. They passed into a later tour of hell, the Apocalypse of Paul, which Dante probably knew. In his Inferno, Pope Nicholas the Third is stuck head down in a hole. He hears someone coming, and calls out: "Are you already standing there, Boniface?" The poem is set in 1300. Boniface the Eighth was alive, and he was the pope. Dante had reserved his place.

9:38Who were these hells built for?

Now look at who these hells were built for. Plato's murderous king. Enoch's sinners who died unpunished. The persecutors of the righteous. Dante's living pope. Hell isn't just a theory about death. It's also a complaint about life. Somebody did something terrible, and nothing happened to them. Hell is where the story goes to fix that. That doesn't tell you whether hell is real. Where an idea comes from can't prove it or disprove it. It only tells you why people needed it.

10:16A list that doesn't stop

But a list like that has a problem. It doesn't stop at tyrants. Look again at the Apocalypse of Peter. On the same list as the persecutors, there are women hanging by their hair, for plaiting it to lure men into adultery. A place imagined for people who got away with murder also had room for your neighbour. And a threat that covers your neighbour also covers you.

10:42Rather than a place

A little over half of American adults say they believe in hell. But the picture keeps shifting. In 1999, Pope John Paul the Second told a public audience: "Rather than a place, hell indicates the state of those who freely and definitively separate themselves from God." Rather than a place. It wasn't a change of doctrine. But after two thousand years, a pope was describing hell without an address. The valley still has one.

11:16So where did it come from?

So where did the idea of hell come from? A court in Egypt. A sentence in Greece. A motive in Judea. A valley outside Jerusalem. And pictures from a book that didn't make it into the Bible. And holding them together, one old feeling: somebody got away with it.

11:37A question for you

So here's a question for you. If there's no court after death, is it up to us to make sure nobody gets away with it? Or is some unfairness just something we have to live with? Let me know in the comments.
