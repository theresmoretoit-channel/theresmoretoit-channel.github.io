Where Do Microplastics Come From?

11 min2026-08-31

Tyres wear out. We can literally watch the tread disappear. What we rarely ask is where that missing material actually goes — because it does not simply vanish. A car tyre is thought to lose something like a tenth to a fifth of its own weight while it is used, which is roughly a kilogram per tyre, scattered invisibly along every road you have ever driven. And there are well over a billion vehicles on this planet.

This video follows that material: what a tyre is actually made of, why scientists call the result "tyre and road wear particles" rather than microplastics, what 6PPD-quinone has to do with dead salmon, where the particles end up, and what Europe's Euro 7 rules really say — including the table of tyre limits that is printed with every single value left blank.

The full narration of the video.

0:00What we picture when we hear microplastics

Have you ever wondered where all those microplastics actually come from? When we hear the word microplastics, most of us probably think of plastic bottles. Plastic bags. Food packaging. Synthetic clothes shedding tiny fibres in the washing machine. Maybe cosmetics. Or simply plastic waste slowly breaking down into smaller and smaller pieces. And all of these things really do contribute to the problem. But there is another major source of tiny plastic particles that most people probably don't think about. And you probably have some of it very close to you right now.

The tyres on your car.

0:43Tyres wear out — so where does it go?

You probably know that tyres wear out. The tread slowly disappears. After tens of thousands of kilometres, the tyres are worn enough that you have to replace them. But have you ever stopped to think about where all that missing material actually goes? Because it doesn't simply disappear. Every time you drive, the tyres rub against the road. Tiny amounts of material are worn away. Some of it stays on the road. Some gets blown around by traffic and moving air. And when it rains, some of it is washed away into drains and towards rivers.

Some of these particles are so small that you can't see them at all.

1:26What a tyre is actually made of

And a modern tyre isn't simply a piece of rubber. It is a complex mixture of natural rubber, synthetic polymers, carbon-based materials and a variety of chemical additives. As the tyre wears, it produces what scientists call tyre wear particles. And a portion of these particles falls into the category of microplastics. But that phrase deserves a footnote. A tyre is not simply plastic. The tread is a blend of natural rubber, which grows on trees, and synthetic rubbers, which do not. And by the time a particle has left the road, it usually isn't pure tread at all. It is tyre rubber with road surface ground into it, in roughly equal parts.

That is why scientists prefer to say tyre and road wear particles. Whether those count as microplastics depends on the definition you use. They are not quite a fragment of a plastic bottle, and that is what makes them so hard to study.

2:306PPD: the chemistry a tyre leaves behind

And there is another reason these particles are hard to study. The problem may not be the rubber particle itself. A tyre contains hundreds of different chemicals. One of them is called 6PPD. It is there to protect the rubber from ozone, so that the tyre lasts longer. It does that by reacting with ozone before the rubber can. And one of the things that reaction leaves behind is a chemical called 6PPD-quinone. And that one is extremely toxic to some fish. It has been linked to mass deaths of coho salmon in streams near busy roads, after heavy rain.

So sometimes the question isn't what happens when a piece of tyre enters the environment. It is what that tyre releases along the way.

3:19How much material is that, really?

So every time a car drives down a road, it is gradually leaving a tiny part of its tyres behind. Individually, the amount produced by one car doesn't sound very impressive. But add it up over the life of a tyre. A car tyre is thought to lose something like a tenth to a fifth of its own weight while it is used. For a typical tyre that is about a kilogram. Several kilograms for a full set. Scattered, invisibly, along every road you have ever driven. And there are well over a billion vehicles on this planet. Each one has several tyres. And all of them are constantly wearing down.

That's why tyre wear is considered one of the major sources of microplastic pollution. The exact numbers vary considerably between studies, because researchers use different definitions, measurements and assumptions. But the basic idea is not controversial. A lot of material is leaving our tyres and entering the environment.

4:24Where the particles end up

So where does it all end up? The models disagree on the details, but they agree on the shape of it. Most of the material stays close to the road, trapped in the asphalt or in the soil beside it. A large part is carried away by rain. And something like a tenth simply goes into the air. Which means some of it is not travelling to a river at all. It is travelling to your lungs.

4:52Brakes, roads and non-exhaust emissions

And tyres aren't the only source of particles from cars. There are also brakes. Every time you slow down, the brake pads and other components wear down too. This produces brake dust, a mixture containing metals and other materials. It isn't simply microplastic, but it is another significant source of tiny particles from road traffic. And there is also the road itself. Tyres wear. Brakes wear. Road surfaces wear. And traffic can stir up particles that have already settled on the road. So when we talk about emissions from cars, the exhaust pipe is only part of the story.

These are sometimes called non-exhaust emissions.

5:37Euro 7 and a vacuum cleaner for your brakes

And this brings us to something that may sound slightly ridiculous at first. You may have heard about the European Union introducing new requirements for vehicle emissions. But Euro seven isn't only about exhaust gases. For the first time, Europe puts a number on how much dust your brakes are allowed to shed. Three milligrams per kilometre for a battery electric car, seven for everything else. Tyres are in there too, but in a much weaker form. Those limits have not actually been written yet. The regulation prints the headings, leaves every single limit blank, and hands the job to a United Nations working group.

And here is the part that people get wrong. The rules set a limit. They do not say how to meet it. And one of the routes they are trying sounds almost like a joke. A vacuum cleaner for your brakes. Not a household vacuum cleaner strapped to the wheel. But one French system cuts a groove in the brake pad and sucks the dust out through the caliper. On test rigs it captures most of it. Nobody is required to fit one. It is simply one answer to a limit that has to be met. And suddenly, the idea doesn't sound quite so crazy. Because if you know that a brake is constantly producing tiny particles… why not try to catch them before they spread everywhere?

7:07Electric cars do not make it disappear

And here's another interesting part. Electric cars don't make this problem disappear. They don't have exhaust emissions from an internal combustion engine. And most of them barely touch their brake pads. They slow down with the motor, recovering energy instead of rubbing a pad against a disc. Measurements suggest this can cut brake particles by well over half. Europe already asks them for three milligrams instead of seven. But no motor can help with the tyres. They still have tyres. They still drive on roads. In fact, because some electric vehicles are heavier than comparable combustion-engine cars, tyre wear can be an interesting issue of its own.

So even a car with zero tailpipe emissions can still produce particles as it moves.

8:00What can actually be done

So what can we actually do about it? There are obvious technological approaches. Tyres could be designed to last longer and produce less wear. Their materials could be improved. Road runoff could potentially be filtered before it reaches rivers and other waterways. But there is also a much simpler solution. Don't wear the tyre out in the first place. Driving style matters. Hard acceleration, aggressive cornering and heavy braking all put additional stress on tyres. But even the gentlest driver cannot eliminate tyre wear. Because that's what a tyre is designed to do. It grips the road. And some of its material is inevitably lost in the process.

8:48What does it do to us?

And this brings us to the most uncomfortable part of the story. What does all of this actually do to us? The honest answer is that we don't fully know yet. Microplastics have been detected in water, food, air and human tissues. And nanoplastics are an even newer and more difficult subject to study. But finding particles in the human body doesn't automatically mean that they are causing disease. We still don't know what levels of exposure are harmful, or what happens over decades. And this is one reason why simply calling all of this microplastics can be misleading. The research is still catching up with the problem.

9:32Where did the missing material go?

And perhaps that's the strangest thing about all of this. We've been driving cars for more than a century. We've always known that tyres wear out. We can literally see the tread disappearing. But only relatively recently have we started asking a much more basic question. Where did all that missing material go? It didn't disappear. It was gradually scattered into the world around us. And some of it became particles so small that we can no longer see them.

So the next time you look at a worn-out tyre… remember that it isn't just a tyre that has become thinner. A small part of that tyre has become part of the environment. Which leaves one open question. Can we learn to catch those particles before they get away… or does the tyre itself have to change?
