How Precise Can Time Actually Be?

9 min2026-11-13

In 2025, physicists in Rome and Budapest published a calculation that puts a crack in the idea of perfectly sharp time. This video goes through how a clock actually works, how gravity changes the rate of time (measured over thirty-three centimetres, and then across a single millimetre), the measurement problem at the heart of quantum mechanics, collapse models, the idea of Roger Penrose and Lajos Diósi that gravity forces nature to choose, and what all of that would mean for every clock in the universe, including a twist nobody would have guessed.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The most precise clock

In a laboratory in Boulder, Colorado, there is a clock whose frequency is known so precisely that even over the entire age of the universe, the uncertainty would add up to less than a second. And it isn't the end of the road. Every generation of clocks has been better than the last. So here's the obvious question. How far can this go? Is there a point where the problem isn't the clock any more, but time itself? In classical physics, time was treated as perfectly sharp. Only our clocks were clumsy.

0:35A crack in the assumption

But in 2025, a group of physicists in Rome and Budapest published a calculation that puts a crack in that assumption. If one particular idea about quantum mechanics is right, then every clock in the universe picks up a tiny, random uncertainty. Not because it's badly built. Because, in these models, time itself would jitter. And there's a twist nobody would have guessed. The clocks that suffer most aren't the cheap ones. They're the smallest ones.

1:07Three pieces

To see why, we need three pieces. How a clock works. What gravity does to time. And a very old problem at the heart of quantum mechanics.

1:19How a clock works

A clock doesn't measure time directly. It counts something that repeats. A pendulum swings. A quartz crystal vibrates. The clock in Boulder counts the waves of a laser locked to strontium atoms. About four hundred and twenty-nine trillion of them every second. Count enough repetitions, and you have a second. The more steadily the thing repeats, the better the clock.

1:47Gravity and time

And this is where gravity enters the story. Einstein showed that clocks don't all run at the same rate. A clock deeper in a gravitational field ticks more slowly than a clock higher up. And we can measure it. In 2010, physicists raised one atomic clock by just thirty-three centimetres, and saw it run faster. In 2022, the team in Boulder went further. They saw the difference inside a single cloud of atoms, across one millimetre. The top of the cloud aged faster than the bottom. By about one part in ten billion billion. So the rate of a clock depends on the gravity around it. And if gravity itself fluctuated, at random, in the way some quantum models predict, time would fluctuate with it.

2:38A very old quantum problem

Why would gravity fluctuate? The answer comes from that very old quantum problem. In quantum mechanics, a particle doesn't have to be in one place. It can be in a superposition. Here and there, at the same time. The equations of quantum mechanics let that superposition go on forever. They never pick an answer. And yet, whenever we look, we see one answer. Here. Or there. Never both. What makes nature choose is called the measurement problem. And after a hundred years, physicists still disagree about it.

3:15Collapse models

One family of answers is called collapse models. They say nature makes the choice itself, all the time, with nobody watching. In the first complete collapse model, from the 1980s, a single particle gets pinned down about once every three hundred million years. But a cat is made of roughly a billion billion billion particles. Together, they get pinned down around a hundred billion times a second. In these models, that's why atoms can be in two places at once, and cats can't.

3:49Where gravity comes in

Now the gravity part. Roger Penrose and Lajos Diósi suggested where these collapses might come from. A mass in two places at once means two different gravitational fields at once. Penrose argued that nature can't keep that up for long. Gravity forces the choice. And the 2025 paper, with Diósi as one of its authors, makes a surprising connection. The random noise in collapse models can be interpreted as random fluctuations of gravity. And that works not just for Penrose's model, which was already tied to gravity, but also for the most studied one, which never was.

4:32Putting it together

Now put the pieces together. Gravity sets the rate of a clock. If gravity fluctuates, the rate of every clock fluctuates too. A tiny bit faster. A tiny bit slower. At random. And how much, as we'll see, depends on the size of the clock.

4:50Pixels of time?

This doesn't mean time comes in little pixels. The models don't predict a smallest possible tick. It's more like a walk where every step is a tiny bit off, in a random direction. Each step barely matters. But the steps don't cancel out completely. The uncertainty piles up. Run the clock four times longer, and its typical drift doubles. Even a flawless clock would pick up this uncertainty. No colder lab, no better engineering, would remove it.

5:23The twist

And here's the twist. Shielding doesn't matter. Size does. In the most studied collapse model, the fluctuations change from place to place over about one ten-millionth of a metre. Roughly the size of a virus. A clock that small feels the full fluctuation. A bigger clock spans many places at once, each with a slightly different change. They mostly average out. And a pulsar, a spinning dead star twenty kilometres across, would be almost completely immune. In Penrose's version, the scale is about a hundred times smaller. But the pattern holds. Bigger clocks average more of it away.

So the most fundamentally uncertain clock isn't the cheapest one. It's the smallest one.

6:14How big is the drift?

So how big is the drift?

With the standard settings of the most studied collapse model, a clock of the size where the effect is strongest, running for a whole year, would pick up an uncertainty of about ten to the minus twenty-eight seconds. That's a ten-thousandth of a trillionth of a trillionth of a second. Other settings that experiments still allow could make it a few hundred times larger, or a hundred times smaller.

6:42Today's best clocks

Today's best optical clocks have ordinary timing fluctuations of around ten to the minus seventeen seconds after just one second. Over a day, their noise is about a million billion times larger than the predicted effect. Even a clock that had been running since the Big Bang would have picked up only a few trillionths of a trillionth of a second. And Penrose's version predicts a drift a few hundred times smaller still. So the authors are clear. For every clock that exists, this effect is completely negligible.

7:18Why it matters

Which raises a fair question. If no clock today can measure it, why does it matter? Because it isn't really a question about clocks. It's a question about whether quantum mechanics is the whole story. If collapse models are wrong, this source of uncertainty simply disappears. If they're right, there's a fundamental limit to how precisely any clock can track time. Not set by engineers. Set by nature.

7:47Under a mountain in Italy

And collapse models can be tested in other ways. In 2020, researchers working deep beneath a mountain in Italy ruled out the simplest version of the Diósi–Penrose model, the one with no adjustable settings. Other versions remain possible. And the experiments keep getting better.

8:10So how precise can time be?

So, how precise can time actually be?

Maybe not infinitely precise. And if that's true, then even a perfect clock is drifting away from something. So here's a question for you. If a flawless clock still drifts, what exactly is it drifting away from? Let me know in the comments.
