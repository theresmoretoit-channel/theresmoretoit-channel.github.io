Ordinary Things, Awkward Questions

Why Do Some People Hate Apple?

10 min2026-09-20

This isn't a complaint about quality — the hardware is superb. It's a question about who the thing belongs to. We walk through eight concrete cases: the headphone socket, the connector Apple kept for eleven years, parts pairing, the repair kit that arrived as two flight cases, memory you can't change, the App Store commission, and the year millions of phones were quietly slowed down.

And then the part that makes it genuinely hard: the same control that stops you fitting a screen you bought is the reason your headphones connect before you've asked them to. Take one away and you take the other.

The full narration of the video.

0:00You own your phone

You own your phone. You paid for it. It's yours. It's in your pocket right now. And Apple decides what is allowed to run on it. Not just software that's dangerous. Not just software that's broken. Software Apple hasn't put its stamp on. That is not a complaint about quality. The phone is superb. It's a question about who the thing belongs to.

And once you notice it, you start seeing it everywhere.

And it's why some people can't stand this company.

0:39The socket that disappeared

In 2016, the iPhone 7 arrived without a headphone socket. The plug it dropped had been standard since the 1960s. Stereos. Aeroplane armrests. Almost every phone in the shop. Apple went on stage and gave a reason. Courage.

That isn't a reason. It's a slogan. And Apple was right anyway. Three months before that phone went on sale, wireless headphones had already passed wired ones in American shops. Not by how many sold. By how much people spent on them.

Being told what you need, and then discovering you did need it, is a very particular feeling.

And it wasn't the only port. In 2012, Apple replaced the connector on the bottom of the iPhone with one of its own design. It kept it for eleven years. Meanwhile the rest of the industry settled on a single shared plug. Apple switched in 2023, with the iPhone 15. The year after the European Union passed a law requiring it.

1:56The part that doesn't fit

Now take something breaking. Your screen cracks. You find the identical part from an identical phone. You fit it. It works. Every pixel lights up. And your phone tells you it doesn't recognise it. The screen, the battery, the camera and the Face ID module each carry their own serial number. That number is married to your logic board at the factory. Swap the part, and the phone knows. On the iPhone 13, changing a cracked screen stopped Face ID working. Not the screen. Face ID.

Fine, you think. Then I'll do it Apple's way. Apple does run a programme that sends you the official parts and the official tools. A journalist ordered it to change one battery. It arrived as two flight cases weighing about thirty-six kilos. The battery weighs about thirty grams.

Apple has since loosened this. Genuine used parts can now be paired. Which is the interesting bit. Not that it never changes. That it changes when somebody pushes.

3:13The memory you can't change

Then there's the computer itself. For decades, owning a computer meant you could open it. Add memory. Change a drive. Fit a better graphics card. On a modern Mac, the memory is not a component. It is packaged together with the processor. There is no slot. There is no stick. There is nothing to open. Which means the only moment you get to decide how much memory your computer will ever have… …is before you buy it.

And it isn't only the hardware. On the Mac, you can still install whatever you like. On the phone, it's the door we started with.

3:58The thirty per cent

A developer writes an iPhone app. Apple decides whether it reaches anybody at all, and the standard commission is thirty per cent. So: you can't choose the shop. You can't choose the parts. You can't choose the memory. And all of it is yours.

4:17The year the phones slowed down

Which brings us to the strangest one. In 2016, people began noticing that their older iPhones were switching themselves off. Not running out of charge. Switching off, with charge to spare. There was a real reason for it. As a battery ages, it struggles to deliver a sudden burst of power. So the phone would cut out in the middle of doing something. Apple's fix was to slow the processor down before that could happen. Which is, honestly, a sensible piece of engineering. Apple shipped it in a software update. And didn't clearly say it slowed the phone.

For almost a year, millions of people had a phone that had been deliberately slowed. The notes mentioned power management. It came out because somebody ran the numbers and published them. Apple confirmed it, apologised, and for one year dropped the price of a replacement battery from seventy-nine dollars to twenty-nine. The engineering was defensible. The communication wasn't.

5:29Now here's the hard part

Now here's the part that makes this genuinely hard. Apple isn't doing any of this to annoy you. Think about what actually happens when you take an Apple device out of its box. Your headphones connect before you've asked them to. Your photos are already on your laptop. You copy something on one machine and paste it on another. A phone call rings on your watch. None of that is magic. It works because Apple owns both ends of every one of those connections.

The same control that stops you fitting a screen you bought… …is why the headphones just work.

Take one away and you take the other. And this is why arguing about Apple goes nowhere. Because the complaint and the compliment are about the same thing.

6:27More than a device

There's one more layer, and it isn't technical at all. Apple doesn't just sell a device. It sells a way of thinking about technology. Not: here is a telephone. But: this changes everything. This is the future. Think different.

So people don't just own the thing. They tell you about it. Some critics say it behaves almost like a religion. Not literally. There are no Apple churches. But look at the shape of it. A founder with a legend attached. Events that happen every year. Believers. Sceptics. Arguments that never end.

Very few people describe their washing machine that way. And that's where the real conflict starts. Because "think different" comes from a company that is extremely specific about how you may use its products. Which sounds like a contradiction. It isn't.

7:31A room, or a cage

Apple isn't selling you freedom. It's selling you a room where you never have to worry about anything. Everything in it is placed well. Nothing is in your way. Nothing needs configuring. And for millions of people, that is exactly what they want. You can move the furniture. But if you want to move the walls… …you can't.

And Apple won't call that a wall. It'll call it design. You heard it earlier. Courage. So if it bothers you, there's nothing to report. There's only a preference.

8:13Two people, one computer

Imagine two people buying the same computer. The first one says: I don't want to configure anything. I don't want to spend Saturday fixing my own machine. I just want it to work. For that person, the room is a gift. The second one says: I want to choose my own parts. I want to install what I like. I want to mend what I bought. For that person, the room is a cage.

And neither of them is wrong. They simply want different things.

8:55Convenience against control

So what is this argument actually about? Not Apple against Android. Not Mac against Windows. It's convenience against control.

Do you want to know how everything works? Or would you rather never have to? Apple is extremely good at offering you the second one. And that is exactly why millions of people love it, and exactly why some people can't stand it.

Apple doesn't just sell technology. It sells an idea about what technology should be. And whether you love that idea or hate it… …Apple has spent decades making sure you know what it is.
