Why Does English Need "Do" to Ask a Question?

8 min2026-10-14

From a Latin schoolbook glossed in Old English to Chaucer's four negatives in one sentence, from a 13th-century poem that needed a rhyme to the King James Bible's "and he did eat": how an ordinary verb crept into English questions, spread into plain statements, retreated again, and ended up doing a job nobody ever designed.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Two "do"s in one question

Why do you do that? It's a perfectly ordinary question. But look at it closely. One "do" is the thing you're doing. The other one… isn't doing anything at all. Or take "Do you like coffee?" The verb is "like". You're asking about liking, not doing. So what is "do" doing there? Take it out, and the question breaks. "Like you coffee?" Nobody talks like that.

0:27Shakespeare used both

Except… people did. And not just anyone. "Saw you the weird sisters?" asks Macbeth, around 1606. No "do". Just the verb, jumping to the front. And a few years earlier, in Hamlet, the same writer has Polonius ask: "What do you read, my lord?" This time, with "do". Shakespeare used both. Because in his lifetime, English was halfway through changing its mind. In the 1950s, a Swedish linguist, Alvar Ellegård, went through centuries of English texts and counted. By the 1570s, more than half of the questions he counted used "do". In some kinds, three in four.

So this isn't a rule English always had. It crept in… and took over. But where did it come from? And why "do", of all words?

1:25Questions a thousand years ago

Go back a thousand years, to a schoolbook for teaching Latin. Between the Latin lines, someone wrote in the English of the day. A teacher asks a ploughman, roughly: "What sayest thou, ploughman?" That's how a question worked. You took the verb and moved it in front of the subject. "Thou sayest" became "sayest thou?" German still does exactly this. "You like coffee" becomes "Like you coffee?" And no German finds that strange. English kept the old way for centuries. The King James Bible, in 1611: "Lovest thou me?" "Understandest thou what thou readest?"

And modern English still does it. Just not with ordinary verbs. "Is she happy?" "Can you swim?" "Have you seen it?" And when you're angry enough, the old pattern comes right back. "How dare you?" Not "How do you dare?" "Dare" is a very old verb, from the same odd little family as "can" and "may", and it never fully learned the new rule.

2:31How "not" started as "nothing"

Saying "no" has an even stranger history. In Old English, to say "I don't say", you put a tiny word in front of the verb. Literally: "I not say." But tiny words wear out. Said quickly, they barely make a sound. So speakers propped it up. They added a second word after the verb, meaning "no whit". Not a bit. Nothing at all. That second word shrank over time, into the word you know as "not". "Not" started life as "nothing". For a while, English used both at once. And then some. In the 1390s, Chaucer describes a knight. Roughly, in modern words:

"He never yet no villainy not said, in all his life, to no kind of person." Four negatives in one sentence. And it wasn't bad grammar. It meant: never, ever, not once. Then the tiny word in front faded away, and "not" was left on its own, after the verb. "I know not." That's exactly what Hamlet says to his mother: "I know not 'seems'." Same play as "What do you read?" Next came "I do not know". And finally, "I don't know." Each step took centuries, and nobody planned a single one. French went round the same loop. Its word for "not", "pas", originally meant "a step". "I don't walk… a single step."

3:58Where did "do" come from?

So where does "do" come in? The first empty "do"s turn up in the 1200s, in the west of England, often in verse. In King Horn, a poem from the 1200s, a man puts down his cloak, roughly: "His cloak he did down lay." Which simply means: he laid it down. For a poet, that's handy. "Did lay" leaves the plain verb at the end of the line, where it can rhyme. But that explains why poets liked it… not where it came from. And on that, linguists still disagree. One idea: it grew out of an older "do" that meant "make something happen". "He did build a house": he had it built. Use that often enough, and the meaning drains away.

Another idea points next door. Welsh, Cornish and Breton use "do" in a similar way. In Breton, "I read" can be, quite literally, "Reading, I do." Did English pick the habit up from its Celtic neighbours? Possibly. But there's no direct evidence, and many linguists aren't convinced.

5:04"Do" in plain statements

What we do know is what happened next. Because questions weren't its only home. In the 1500s, it became far more common in ordinary statements, too. No emphasis. No extra meaning. It was just… there. Here's Genesis, in the King James Bible: "She took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat." Not "she ate". "She did eat." And William Tyndale's translation, which the King James borrowed from heavily, had simply said "ate". At its peak, around 1560, roughly one plain statement in twelve had this empty "do" in it.

And then it retreated. By the late 1600s, it was fewer than one in a hundred. It vanished from plain statements, and kept only the jobs where it was useful. Questions. Negatives. And one more. "Do you actually like coffee?" "I DO like coffee!" That's what's left of the empty "do". In a plain statement, today, it only turns up to add emphasis. Which makes this a perfectly good sentence: "I DO do that!" One "do" is the action. The other insists it's true.

6:23Why it stuck

But here's the strangest part. When linguists plotted how fast "do" was spreading, in questions and in negatives… the lines started at different heights, but until about 1575, they all rose at the same underlying rate. As if it were one single change. The most widely accepted explanation: English verbs were slowly losing the ability to move. In front of the subject, or in front of "not". But a question still needed something up front. A negative still needed something to hang "not" on. And "do" was already there. Short, common, and by now meaning almost nothing.

It's an explanation that fits the numbers. The texts show what changed, not why.

7:12Nobody chose it

By around 1700, the new rule had mostly won in yes-or-no questions. Negatives took most of another century. No committee chose it. No grammar book introduced it. The grammar books only described it, after it had happened. It grew out of centuries of people simply talking to each other. Which is all grammar ever is.

So the next time someone asks you, "Why do you do that?" One "do" is the thing you actually do. The other is a habit that's been spreading for seven hundred years, still doing its job. And if that seems needlessly complicated… do you really think English cares?
