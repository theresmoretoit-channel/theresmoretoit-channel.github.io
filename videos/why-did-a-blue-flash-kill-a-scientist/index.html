Why Did a Blue Flash Kill a Scientist Nine Days Later?

10 min2026-10-05

In May 1946, Louis Slotin was demonstrating a criticality experiment at Los Alamos with a plutonium core made for the third atomic bomb. The only thing keeping two beryllium shells apart was a screwdriver in his hand.

Why could a core like that be handled at all? What turned it dangerous in a fraction of a second? Where did the blue light come from, if it wasn't an explosion? And why was this not even the first time the same core had killed someone?

The full narration of the video.

0:00A blue flash in a laboratory

Imagine you're standing in a laboratory. In front of you is a small metallic sphere. It doesn't look particularly dangerous. There is no fire. No explosion. No warning siren. Then, suddenly— a flash of blue light fills the room. For a fraction of a second, something has happened that you cannot see. The scientist standing closest to it immediately knows what went wrong. He throws the equipment away. And everyone in the room runs for the door. Nine days later, he is dead. His name was Louis Slotin. And the object in front of him would later become known as the Demon Core.

But what actually happened in that laboratory? And where did that strange blue light come from?

0:48He had been warned

Slotin knew this work was dangerous. He had been warned. Enrico Fermi reportedly told him that if he kept doing it this way, he would be dead within a year. Richard Feynman once compared this kind of work to tickling the tail of a sleeping dragon. And this wasn't experimental plutonium made for a laboratory. This was a bomb core. It had been made for the third atomic bomb. On the day of the accident, Slotin was teaching the man who was about to take over his job. The experiment wasn't even scheduled. Slotin decided to do it as a demonstration.

The approved method used spacers, so the assembly could never close completely. Slotin took them out. His way of doing it depended on a screwdriver. So why was a screwdriver anywhere near a nearly critical plutonium core?

1:50What makes a core critical

One fission releases neutrons. Those neutrons can trigger more fissions. If enough of them keep the process going, the reaction becomes self-sustaining. The system is called critical. If too few neutrons remain available, the reaction dies out. But if the number grows— the reaction can increase extremely rapidly. The scientists at Los Alamos were studying precisely how close they could bring a piece of plutonium to that dangerous boundary.

2:20Why the core alone wasn't so dangerous

Was the core dangerous even without a screwdriver? Far less than you might think. On its own, it was below critical mass, so most of its neutrons simply escaped. Its radiation was mostly alpha particles, and a thin nickel coating stopped them. The sphere just felt warm to the touch. The danger began only when something sent those escaping neutrons back.

2:45Sending the neutrons back

Around it, scientists could place materials that reflected neutrons back toward the plutonium. One such material was beryllium. Move the reflector closer— and the system becomes more reactive. Move it away— and the system becomes less reactive. And with beryllium wrapped almost all the way around the core, even a tiny gap made all the difference. And there was no reliable way to do this by remote control yet. So it was done by hand.

3:16May 21, 1946

On May 21, 1946, Louis Slotin was performing exactly this kind of experiment. Slotin was a Canadian physicist working at Los Alamos. He had performed similar experiments before. The core sat inside a beryllium half-shell. Slotin slowly lowered a second shell over it, holding it with his thumb. The shells could almost completely surround it. But he needed to keep a tiny gap between them. So he used a screwdriver. The screwdriver wasn't some sophisticated safety mechanism. It was literally a screwdriver in Slotin's hand.

3:55The screwdriver slips

Then— the screwdriver slipped. The upper shell dropped into place. For an instant, the plutonium became supercritical. And something extraordinary happened. A burst of radiation passed through the laboratory. Witnesses reported seeing a blue flash. Slotin immediately flipped the upper shell off. The reaction stopped. The entire event had lasted only a fraction of a second. But the damage had already been done.

4:27No explosion

There was no nuclear explosion. The plutonium core did not explode like an atomic bomb. Instead, it produced an extremely intense burst of radiation. For a fraction of a second, the chain reaction ran away on its own, faster than any human could react. But for the people standing nearby, a fraction of a second was enough.

4:49"He knew he had had it"

Slotin had been standing closest to the core. He received a massive dose of radiation. The other scientists in the room also received radiation, but their exposure was considerably smaller. Slotin knew immediately that he had been badly exposed. One of the men in the room later said: "He knew he had had it at that time." Even so, he sketched where everyone had been standing, so the doctors could work out their doses. He had survived the immediate accident. But he would not survive its consequences.

5:26Where did the blue light come from?

The flash looked like Cherenkov radiation. That is the blue glow you can see around nuclear reactor fuel under water. It happens when charged particles move through a material faster than light can travel through that material. But there was a problem. This happened in air. In air, light travels almost as fast as it does in a vacuum. Hardly anything can outrun it there. The burst of radiation ionised the surrounding air. And excited air molecules give off light of their own. So the blue flash wasn't the thing that killed Slotin.

It was the warning that the invisible thing had already happened.

6:08Nine days

He was conscious when he reached the hospital. But the radiation had already fatally damaged cells throughout his body. Over the following days, his condition deteriorated. Nine days after the accident, Louis Slotin died. He was only thirty-five years old.

And this wasn't even the first time.

6:29Not the first time

The same plutonium core had already caused a fatal accident. Less than a year earlier, another scientist named Harry Daghlian had been performing a criticality experiment with the same core. He was using tungsten-carbide bricks to reflect neutrons toward it. One of the bricks slipped from his hand and fell onto the assembly. The core went supercritical. A guard sat a few metres away, reading a newspaper with his back to the table. The blue flash lit up the page in his hands. Daghlian immediately removed the brick. But he had received a lethal dose of radiation. He died twenty-five days later.

7:14The twenty-first of the month

Both fatal accidents happened on the twenty-first day of a month. One in August 1945. The other in May 1946. The coincidence meant nothing scientifically. But it made the story almost impossible to forget. It wasn't called the Demon Core when Slotin died. The name came later, after the same piece of plutonium had been involved in two fatal accidents. One core. Two scientists. Two criticality accidents. And both times, the warning came too late.

7:51What changed afterwards

The accidents changed the way scientists approached criticality experiments. Hands-on experiments like this were banned. Eventually, they were run from about four hundred metres away, watched on television. Scientists became much more cautious about bringing fissile material close to criticality. The core had been made for the third atomic bomb. After the second accident, the core was too radioactive to use straight away. It was moved to a third test at Bikini — and that test was cancelled. The age of scientists manually holding pieces of equipment around a nearly critical plutonium core was coming to an end.

8:33What happened to the core

The Demon Core itself was eventually melted down and its plutonium reused. So the infamous sphere no longer exists. But the story survived.

8:45Remember the blue flash?

Remember the blue flash? It wasn't an explosion. It wasn't the plutonium burning. It wasn't even the thing that killed Louis Slotin. It was the moment the invisible danger became visible. And all it took was a screwdriver slipping a few millimetres.
