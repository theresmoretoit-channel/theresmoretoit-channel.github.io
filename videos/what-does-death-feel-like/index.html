Your Body, and How Long It Lasts

What Does Death Feel Like?

11 min2026-09-08

This video goes through the answers one at a time — darkness, silence, an empty room, an endless wait, eternal sleep, a black screen — and shows that each of them needs somebody left over to notice it. Then it looks at what actually happens: under general anaesthesia six hours can pass with almost no subjective time at all, and in the largest audit of its kind, only about one patient in twenty thousand reported being aware.

Along the way: why your brain is a prediction machine and keeps adding time after the end, why "I will be dead forever" quietly puts you back on the timeline, and why the eternity before you were born never bothered you. That last argument is not modern — a Roman poet wrote it down about two thousand years ago.

The full narration of the video.

0:00What we think it will feel like

Have you ever wondered what it will feel like when you die? Will there be darkness? Silence? Will you somehow remain conscious, floating in nothingness? Will you wait? And if you believe in an afterlife, perhaps you imagine waking up somewhere else. Every one of those answers sounds like a different thing. But they all have the same thing hidden inside them. Let's take them one at a time. Darkness. That's usually the first answer. But darkness is something you see. Shut your eyes right now and you still get darkness. A dim, shifting grey, delivered by a visual system that is still running.

Silence, then. But silence is something you hear. An empty room? An empty room still has someone standing in it. And an endless wait? Waiting requires someone who is waiting. There it is. Every one of those answers quietly puts someone into the picture. Someone who sees the dark. Someone who hears the silence. Someone who waits. So before we ask what death feels like, we have to ask a much harder question. What happens to that someone?

1:16Eternal sleep

Here is the answer most people reach for next. "After I die, I'll sleep forever." But eternal sleep would still imply a sleeper. And sleep has a feature that death doesn't. You wake up. Imagine falling asleep tonight. Then imagine that, somehow, eight hours simply disappear from your subjective experience. You don't experience eight hours of nothing. You experience one moment before sleep… and then another moment when you wake up. That gap isn't an experience. It's a missing one.

1:53Try to imagine nothing

All right. Try it directly. Try to imagine nothing. Really nothing. Most of us immediately picture a black screen. But that's not nothing. There is a screen. There is darkness. And, most importantly, there is you, looking at it. Try again. Imagine an empty universe. Nothing in it at all. But who is observing it? Our brains are simply not very good at simulating their own absence. And there is a good reason for that.

2:25Why your brain cannot

Your conscious experience normally has a very strong sense of continuity. There was a moment ago. There is now. And there will be a moment after this one. Memory gives you a past. Perception gives you the present. Prediction gives you a future. And that is what gives you the feeling of being one continuous person. Neuroscientists have a phrase for it. Your brain is a prediction machine. It is always running a model of what comes next. So when you try to imagine your own death, it does what it always does. It builds a sequence.

Me. Then death. Then time. Then more time. Then more time. But there is a problem. After the end of consciousness, there is nobody there to experience that sequence. Your brain keeps adding time because that's what brains do. It is trying to simulate a future in which the simulator itself no longer exists.

3:26General anaesthesia

There is one situation that gets close to this, and millions of people go through it every year. General anaesthesia. The brain isn't simply switched off. Parts of it stay active, still processing information, while conscious experience is gone. You can be unconscious for six hours. Then you open your eyes. And subjectively? There isn't a six-hour-long experience of darkness. There is no memory of an empty black room. It feels more like this. Before… and after.

And somebody actually counted. Britain gives around three million general anaesthetics a year. About one patient in twenty thousand reports being aware. Of course, anaesthesia isn't death. It is temporary and controlled. And most importantly, anaesthesia has an awakening. Death doesn't. But it gives us an intuition for something very difficult to imagine. Subjective time can disappear.

4:34The simplest version of death

So let's go back to the simplest possible version of death. Your brain stops functioning. Cut the blood supply, and consciousness can be lost within about ten seconds. The processes that create perception, memory and thought stop. The integration that produces conscious experience is gone. And that is what every one of those answers missed. Death is not a state you experience. It is the absence of experience.

5:06The computer analogy

Here's a slightly morbid way of putting it. Imagine your brain as a gigantic computer. Processing units. Memory. And a huge network letting the parts talk to each other. The conscious experience isn't a file sitting somewhere on the hard drive. It's the running system. With death, the hardware itself fails. The connections are destroyed. The processes stop. And eventually there isn't even a computer left to display a black screen. Death isn't a computer showing a black screen forever. It's the computer being gone.

And we have no reliable evidence for a consciousness that continues independently of the brain. Not yet, at least.

5:56So why does it frighten us?

Which raises a much better question. If there is nothing there to feel, why does it frighten us so much? Part of the answer is hiding in our own language. We say this. "I will be dead forever." But notice what the sentence does. It puts you into an enormous timeline, as a person who remains there, experiencing the passage of time while being dead. The universe can continue for billions or trillions of years. None of it reaches someone whose consciousness no longer exists. There is no experience of ten years. There is no experience of ten billion years. There is simply no further experience.

6:40The time before you were born

And here is the strangest part. You have already been through this. Think about the time before you were born. You don't remember it. Not because your memory is damaged. Not because you forgot. You simply weren't there.

You didn't spend billions of years waiting to be born. You didn't experience darkness. You weren't bored. You weren't lonely. You weren't thinking this. "When is this going to end?" There was no you to experience any of it.

7:13Lucretius

And that argument is not new. A Roman poet wrote it down about two thousand years ago. Lucretius asked his readers to look back at the eternity before their birth, and to notice that it had cost them nothing.

So why does the eternity on the other side feel so completely different? Because the past is finished, and the future is the thing our brain is constantly trying to control. So losing it feels like losing something that already belongs to us. But it doesn't. There is no future experience waiting for you after death.

7:52Dying is not being dead

There is one more distinction, and it may be the most useful one here. When people say this. "I'm afraid of death." They may actually mean this. "I'm afraid of dying." Those are not the same thing.

Dying can involve pain, fear, confusion, loss of control, separation from people you love, or simply the knowledge that you're about to lose everything familiar. Those are real experiences. And medicine can sometimes do a great deal to reduce them. But being dead is different. Once consciousness has ended, there is no longer anyone experiencing pain. There is no fear. No boredom. No loneliness. No waiting.

8:40The observer keeps coming back

A conscious brain can even imagine "Nothing will happen to me." But it struggles with "There will be no me to whom anything happens." So the brain quietly puts itself back into the picture. An observer. A black room. A silent universe. An endless passage of time. And we end up with exactly what death isn't. An experience.

9:05The symmetry

There is something almost elegant about the symmetry. Before your existence began, there was no subjective time. Then suddenly, there was a first moment you could experience. And eventually, there will be a last one. After that, the universe continues. But your timeline doesn't. There is simply no further point on it.

9:29A better question

Which quietly changes the question. Instead of asking "What will I experience after death?" the more useful question might be "What am I experiencing now?"

Because this part is real. This moment exists. The people around you exist. The things you enjoy exist. The things you still want to see, learn, build and experience exist. There may be no second run. No backup. No save game. No loading screen after the end. And that can sound frightening. But it can also make the one thing we actually have, this life, matter enormously.

Perhaps the strangest thing about death is not that the lights go out. It is that there is nobody left to notice. We are trying to imagine the absence of the person doing the imagining. And that may simply be something consciousness cannot do.
