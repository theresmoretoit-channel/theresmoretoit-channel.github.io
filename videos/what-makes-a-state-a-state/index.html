What Makes a State a State?

9 min2026-10-03

A state has borders, a flag and a constitution. But the sociologist Max Weber pointed to something less visible: a claim to the legitimate use of force within a territory. This video follows that idea through what happens when a government can no longer enforce its own rules, through Lebanon and Hezbollah, often called "a state within a state", and through the Islamic State, which for a few years performed many of the functions of a state without ever being recognised as one.

And it ends with a paradox: the more successful a state is, the less we notice its monopoly on force.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Who is in charge?

Imagine waking up tomorrow and discovering that the police in your neighbourhood no longer take orders from the government. They take orders from someone else. The government still exists. The laws still exist. The courts still exist. But there is a new question. Who is actually in charge? We all know what a state looks like. It has borders. A flag. A government. A constitution. But what actually makes it a state?

0:31Max Weber's answer

The sociologist Max Weber had a famous answer. He described the modern state as a human community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory. That sounds extremely academic. But the basic idea is surprisingly simple. A country can contain millions of different organisations. Companies. Clubs. Religious organisations. Private security companies. Even armed groups. But the state says: "When it comes to the ultimate use of force, that authority belongs to me." If your neighbour steals your car, you don't get to create your own police department. If someone attacks you, you don't get to build your own prison.

And if two people disagree over who is right, they aren't supposed to bring their own armies and settle the argument. The state is supposed to handle that. Police. Courts. Prisons. The military. It's an enormous system built around one basic function: making rules enforceable.

1:44When rules can't be enforced

Now comes the interesting part. A state can have a constitution. It can have laws. It can have a parliament. It can have impressive government buildings. But if it can't enforce its decisions, something fundamental has changed. A rule that nobody can enforce is still written down. It just no longer decides anything. Imagine a government passes a law saying: "Starting tomorrow, stealing is illegal." But the police don't have enough people. The courts don't function. The prisons are empty. And armed groups in different parts of the country simply do whatever they want.

The law still exists. The parliament still exists. The flag is still flying. But the state is gradually losing control. And now we have a problem. Who is actually in charge?

2:40Other centres of power

Something like this can happen when a central government becomes too weak to control its territory. Other centres of power appear. Militias. Tribal groups. Warlords. Criminal organisations. Religious movements. And each one may have its own rules. In one part of a city, the government's laws apply. A few streets away, an armed group may impose its own. And if that group has enough people and enough weapons, it can enforce them. Suddenly, you have something that looks surprisingly similar to a state… without actually being one.

3:18Lebanon: a state within a state

Lebanon is an interesting example, because the situation there is not simply "there is no state." There is a Lebanese government. There is a national army. There is a police force. But for decades, Hezbollah has kept its own armed forces, alongside extensive political and social structures. This has led to Hezbollah being described as "a state within a state." In March 2026, that stopped being a theoretical problem. Hezbollah fired on Israel. The same day, the Lebanese government banned its military activities. "The decision of war and peace," said the prime minister, "rests exclusively with the state." The war went on for weeks anyway.

So if the government has the legal authority to use force… but another organisation can start a war from its territory without the government's consent… who actually has the monopoly?

4:18The Islamic State

And then there is an even more extreme example. The Islamic State. When ISIS controlled large parts of Iraq and Syria, it wasn't simply a gang hiding weapons somewhere. It had an army. Police. Courts. Taxation. Administration. Its own rules. And, most importantly, the ability to enforce those rules. That did not make ISIS an internationally recognised state. It wasn't. But for a period of time, in the territory it controlled, it performed many functions normally associated with a state. And that reveals something important. A state isn't just a piece of paper. It also has to actually control its territory.

5:04If the government disappeared

Now imagine something even stranger. What if the government disappeared tomorrow? The people wouldn't disappear. Neither would the buildings. The roads would still be there. The money would still exist. And on the first day, perhaps almost nothing would look different. People would wake up. Go to work. Buy groceries. Send their children to school. And then someone would ask: "Wait… who is arresting people today?" Because that's the moment when you discover what a state actually is. It isn't just the president. It isn't just parliament. It isn't the flag.

It's an enormous network of institutions and people capable of saying: "This is the rule." And when someone answers: "I don't care." there has to be someone capable of saying: "Then you're coming with me."

5:57The paradox of a successful state

And that creates a strange paradox. The more successful a state is, the less we actually notice its monopoly on force. Most of the time, nobody is forcing us to do anything. We follow the rules voluntarily. We pay our taxes. We stop at red lights. We don't simply take other people's property. Not because there is a police officer standing behind every person. But because everyone knows that if we cross certain lines… the state can intervene. And that's why a successful state can appear to use very little force. Its ability to use force is part of the reason it usually doesn't have to.

6:40So what makes a state a state?

So… what makes a state a state? A flag? A constitution? Borders? Elections? Perhaps. But if a state can no longer enforce its decisions across the territory it claims to control… something else begins to emerge. Parallel power. Militias. Warlords. Criminal organisations. Competing authorities. And eventually, perhaps, an entirely new state. Because history keeps showing us something uncomfortable. When two different powers claim authority over the same territory, the crucial question is not simply who has the legal right to rule.

It's: who can actually enforce the rules?

7:26Back to the beginning

So let's go back to the beginning. The police in your neighbourhood take orders from someone else. Who is in charge? The obvious answer is: the government. But the deeper answer is: whoever has enough power to enforce its authority over you. And perhaps that is one of the most fundamental questions we can ask about any state. Who has the final say? Inside a country, the state is supposed to have it. But who has the final say over the states themselves? We looked at that question in another video. You'll find it right here.
