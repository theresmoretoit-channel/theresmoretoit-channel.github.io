Where Did Health Insurance Come From?

11 min2026-09-02

Public health insurance feels like something that must have always existed. It hasn't. It is largely a product of the last hundred and fifty years, and it was invented for a reason: industrial society created a new kind of problem, where a person could be perfectly able to work and still have one illness destroy their family's finances.

This is the story of how we got from Roman burial societies and medieval guilds to Bismarck, Beveridge, and the strangest healthcare system in the developed world.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Why this feels normal

Have you ever wondered why, when you get sick, you can simply go to a doctor? You might have to pay. You might have to wait. You might have to deal with paperwork. But the idea that treatment is something you can claim when you need it seems completely normal. It isn't. In historical terms, public health insurance is a surprisingly recent invention. To understand why, we need to go back to a time when getting sick meant something very different.

0:29Europe around 1800

Imagine living in Europe around eighteen hundred. You are a craftsman, a farm worker, or one of the growing number working in a factory. You get seriously ill. You can't work. And because you can't work, you don't get paid. There is no national health service. No insurance card. There may not even be a doctor nearby. If you have money, you can pay for treatment. If you are poor, you depend on charity. And if the illness lasts for months, you have a much bigger problem. You are not just sick. You have lost your income.

1:08Rome, guilds and the common fund

The basic idea of sharing risk is actually ancient. In the Roman world there were mutual associations. Members paid in every month and could get help when something went wrong. Some of them paid for funerals. Medieval guilds continued this. A guild controlled a trade, but it was also a primitive safety net. A member who became ill might receive assistance. So might his widow. So the basic principle was already there. Everyone contributes a little. And when one member suffers a misfortune, the common fund helps them. But this wasn't modern health insurance. The communities were small. Membership was voluntary. And the help was limited.

1:56The Industrial Revolution

Then the Industrial Revolution changed everything. It didn't just create factories and steam engines. It changed the relationship between people and risk. Millions of people moved to cities. They no longer lived in villages where the whole family lived nearby. They became wage workers. And for a wage worker, illness could be financially devastating. So workers began creating their own organisations. Mutual aid societies. Friendly societies. Sickness funds. They contributed a small amount regularly. And if one of them became ill, the fund could provide money, or pay for treatment.

This was much closer to modern health insurance. But it was still voluntary, and it still didn't cover everyone.

2:48Bismarck and the first compulsory system

By the late nineteenth century, Germany was a great industrial power. And its government was worried about something else. The growing socialist and workers' movement. Enter Otto von Bismarck. Bismarck was not a socialist. Quite the opposite. In eighteen seventy-eight, his government banned socialist organisations outright. He was a conservative who wanted to preserve the existing political order. And he realised something rather clever. If workers had protection against the risks of industrial life, they might have less reason for revolution.

So Germany introduced compulsory sickness insurance in eighteen eighty-three. Five years after the ban. It is generally considered one of the foundations of modern public health insurance. Workers and employers paid in. The funds paid for treatment and sickness benefits. It was not free healthcare. It was insurance. And that distinction is important. The idea was not this. The government will simply pay for everyone's healthcare. It was this. Everyone in the covered group pays in, and the fund pays when someone becomes ill.

This became known as the Bismarck model.

4:10How it spread across Europe

The German experiment was hugely influential. Austria-Hungary introduced sickness insurance a few years later. Britain introduced the National Insurance system in nineteen eleven. And after the First World War, Czechoslovakia inherited a system based on the earlier Austro-Hungarian model. The general idea was spreading. Healthcare could be treated as a collective risk.

4:38Beveridge and the National Health Service

There is another famous model, and it came from Britain. During the Second World War, the economist William Beveridge proposed a very different approach. Instead of insurance contributions, healthcare could be funded through general taxation. The result was the National Health Service, created in nineteen forty-eight. And now there were two major approaches. The Bismarck model. You pay into health insurance funds. The Beveridge model. You pay taxes, and the government finances healthcare. Neither system appeared naturally. Both were political choices.

And both were responses to the same problem. Modern medicine is expensive. Illness is unpredictable. And almost nobody can save enough for every possible medical disaster.

5:33And then there was America

So what happened in the United States? Well… The United States did not choose either model. It gradually built something much more complicated. Today, Americans get healthcare through several different systems. There is private insurance. There is employer-sponsored insurance. There is Medicare, mainly for older Americans. There is Medicaid for eligible low-income people, with the rules varying by state. There is the Veterans Health Administration. And millions of people still pay large amounts themselves. So when someone says this. America has private healthcare. That's not really accurate.

And when someone says this. America has public healthcare. That's not accurate either. America has both. The United States never completely rejected public healthcare. It built public programmes for particular groups, while much of the rest of the population relied on private insurance. A giant historical patchwork.

6:42Why American insurance is tied to your job

And one part of that patchwork is especially strange. In much of Europe, changing your job doesn't mean losing your health insurance. In the United States, employer-sponsored health insurance became the dominant way many Americans got coverage.

6:58How that happened by accident

And it happened almost by accident. During the Second World War, the government froze wages. Factories were desperate for workers but were not allowed to pay them more. So they offered health insurance instead. It didn't count as wages. And it wasn't taxed like wages either. By the end of the war, the number of Americans covered had tripled. Nobody had chosen this as a national health policy. So you change jobs… and your healthcare changes with it. Lose your job… and you may have to find another way to get insured. It is the result of historical decisions piling up over decades.

7:42Six questions instead of one

Imagine you are sick. In a typical European system, the basic question is usually this. Where can I get treatment? In the United States, there are several more. Is the doctor in my network? Does my insurance cover this? Have I reached my deductible? How much will the hospital charge? And how much will I actually pay? The result is that two people getting the same treatment can have completely different financial experiences.

8:16Why the paths diverged

So why did Europe go one way and America another? There isn't one simple answer. History matters. Institutions matter. And timing matters most of all. Once a country builds a healthcare system, millions of people, hospitals and insurers adapt around it. Changing it later becomes enormously difficult. Nobody invented the correct system. They simply started from different places.

8:46What insurance actually is

And this brings us back to the beginning. Health insurance feels like something that must have always existed. It hasn't. For most of human history, there was no health insurance. There were families. Communities. Charities. Guilds. Mutual aid societies. And people simply paid doctors when they could. Modern public health insurance is largely a product of the last hundred and fifty years. It emerged because industrial society created a new problem. A person could be perfectly able to work, and one illness could still destroy their family's finances.

Insurance provided a solution. Instead of asking this. Can this person afford to be sick? Society could ask this. Can all of us share the risk of becoming sick? And that is what public health insurance is. Not free healthcare. Not government-owned hospitals. But a mechanism for turning an unpredictable individual disaster into a collective financial risk. And once you look at it that way, the strange thing isn't that different countries have different systems. The strange thing is that we ever managed to create systems at all.

Because for almost all of human history… if you got sick, you were basically on your own.
