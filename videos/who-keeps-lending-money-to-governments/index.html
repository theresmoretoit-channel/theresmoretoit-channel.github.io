Who Keeps Lending Money to Governments?

10 min2026-10-11

Governments spend more than they collect, year after year, and the old loans never seem to go away. So who keeps lending them the money? Who actually buys government bonds, how can Japan owe more than twice its entire economy, and does any of it ever get paid back?

Debt and deficit figures: Eurostat (April 2026 notification) and the IMF World Economic Outlook (April 2026). Ten-year government bond yields on 2 October 2026: Greece 4.52%, France 4.91%.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Borrowing every year

Imagine a country that spends more than it collects. Not once. Every year. Hospitals, schools, pensions, roads. And every year, the money runs out before the bills do. So the government borrows. The next year, it borrows again. And again the year after that. Meanwhile, the old loans don't go away. They come due, one after another. So who keeps lending it the money? And if the debt never seems to shrink, how does any of this keep working?

0:34Where it stopped working

Let's start with a country where it stopped working. In 2009, Greece owed nearly one hundred and thirty percent of what its economy produced in a year. Lenders panicked. Within two years, they were asking for interest no government could pay. In 2012, Greece went through the biggest government debt restructuring in history. Today, Greece owes even more. About one hundred and forty-six percent. And for most of the past year, it has borrowed more cheaply than France. On ten-year bonds. Same country. More debt. Cheaper money. So the size of the debt isn't the whole story. What matters just as much is who's lending, and why.

So let's follow the money.

1:30Deficit and debt

First, two words that get mixed up all the time. Deficit and debt. Imagine a government that collects one hundred billion in taxes, but spends one hundred and ten. That gap of ten billion is the deficit. It's what happens in one year. The debt is everything it still owes from all the years before. A deficit is like water running into a bathtub. The debt is the water already in it. So a country can have a lot of debt and no deficit at all. Or very little debt, and a deficit every year.

2:07Two real countries

Let's see what that looks like in two real countries. To compare them, we don't count debt in kroner or dollars. We measure it against the size of the whole economy: its GDP. A debt of a hundred billion dollars would crush a small country, and barely register in a huge one. Denmark first. At the end of 2025, its government owed twenty-seven point nine percent of GDP. And in 2025, Denmark did what governments supposedly never do. It collected more than it spent. A surplus of two point nine percent. And not for the first time.

Since 2022, its debt has fallen from thirty-three point three percent to under twenty-eight. A surplus can help reduce government debt. But it doesn't mean the debt disappears.

Now let's look at the United States. In 2025, its total government debt stood at roughly one hundred and twenty-four percent of GDP. And it ran a deficit of about six point eight percent. That's a very different picture from Denmark. The debt-to-GDP ratio can fall even while the amount owed is still growing, if the economy grows faster. But the percentage alone doesn't tell us whether a debt is sustainable. For that, we need to look at who owns it, what it costs to service, and how the economy evolves.

3:38Across Europe

Now let's widen the picture across Europe. Estonia: twenty-four percent. Denmark: twenty-eight. Czech Republic: forty-four. Germany: sixty-three and a half. France: one hundred and sixteen. Italy: one hundred and thirty-seven. Greece: one hundred and forty-six. Six times as much, from one end to the other. And every one of them still owes money to someone.

4:07Who buys government bonds?

So who on earth is lending them the money? Governments don't usually walk into a bank and ask for a loan. They sell bonds. A bond is a promise on paper. You give the government money today. It pays you interest, and gives the money back on a fixed date. Say you buy a ten-year bond for one thousand dollars. The government takes your thousand and spends it. You get interest payments every year, and your thousand dollars back in ten years' time. So who buys them? Banks. Pension funds. Insurance companies. Investment funds. Foreign governments. Central banks.

And here's the part that's easy to miss. A pension fund doesn't hold bonds for its own sake. It holds them for the people who'll one day draw a pension. If you pay into a pension, or keep savings in a fund, there's a good chance you're one of the lenders. The government's debt is somebody's savings. Very often, it's yours.

5:13Japan

And that brings us to Japan. Japan's government owes more than twice its entire economy. Far more than Greece. But about nine-tenths of it is held at home. By its banks, its insurers, its pension funds. And the Bank of Japan, which holds nearly half of its bonds. That changes the risks. But it doesn't make them disappear. Greece in 2009 was the opposite. Most of its debt was held by foreign investors, many of them banks, who could sell up and leave. And Greece used the euro. It couldn't create the money its debts were written in.

Today, more than half of Greek debt is owed to other eurozone governments, through rescue funds, on very long terms and at low rates. Same country. Different lenders. That's a big part of why it now borrows more cheaply than France.

6:11When does it get paid back?

But that leaves the other question. If governments keep borrowing, when do they ever pay it back? Imagine a government with one billion dollars of bonds coming due this year. It has to pay those lenders back. But it doesn't need to find a billion dollars in taxes. It sells one billion dollars of new bonds, and uses that money to repay the old ones. The old bonds are gone. The lenders are paid in full. And the debt is exactly the same size. This is called refinancing. And governments do it all the time. Most governments refinance a large part of their maturing debt. Individual bonds come due, but the total debt doesn't have to reach zero.

In 2015, Britain redeemed its last four undated government bonds. Some of the historic liabilities behind them could be traced back to 1720.

7:11The catch

But refinancing has a catch. It only works if somebody is willing to buy the new bonds. And at an interest rate the government can afford. When lenders get nervous, they ask for more interest. Every cheap old bond that comes due is replaced by an expensive new one, and the interest bill starts to climb. A bigger interest bill makes the deficit worse. A worse deficit makes lenders more nervous. And nervous lenders ask for even more. That's the spiral Greece fell into. By early 2012, lenders wanted around thirty percent a year. At times, far more.

It ended the only way it could. Private lenders lost more than half of what they were owed. Japan has sustained a much higher debt ratio than Greece had before its crisis. But debt ratios alone don't tell us when a country will get into trouble. What matters is whether lenders believe they'll keep getting paid.

8:13Why not pay it all off?

So why don't governments just pay everything off? Some have come close. In the early 2000s, Australia was paying off its debt so fast it expected to be clear within a few years. So it asked whether it would still need to sell bonds at all. It decided to keep selling them. Not to pay for the budget, but because financial markets relied on them. Futures contracts were tied to them, and traders used them to manage interest rate risk. By 2007, what the government owned outweighed what it owed. And it still kept selling bonds.

So a government doesn't sell bonds only because it's short of money. A working bond market can be worth keeping for its own sake.

9:01So who keeps lending?

So, who keeps lending money to governments? Banks. Pension funds. Insurers. Other governments. And, quite often, you. They can keep replacing maturing debt with new borrowing. But that only works while they can find lenders on terms they can afford. So next time you hear that a country owes trillions, don't just ask how it will ever pay. Ask who's lending it the money. And whether they'll still be there next year. Because government debt is a chain of promises. It holds for as long as people believe it. Until they don't.
