What Would a Spoonful of a Neutron Star Do?

10 min2026-10-30

This video works through it layer by layer: why ordinary matter is mostly empty space, what keeps an atomic nucleus from flying apart, how a dying star crushes a core the size of the Earth down to the size of a city in less than a second, and what the thing left behind is really made of.

Along the way: nuclear pasta, the collision of two neutron stars that telescopes all over the world turned to watch in 2017, where some of the gold on Earth may have come from, why the three quarks inside a proton add up to only about one percent of its mass, and a number with no units that nobody can explain.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A teaspoon of a neutron star

Imagine a single teaspoon of matter from a neutron star. On Earth, it would weigh billions of tonnes. That's several times the mass of every human being alive. In one teaspoon. Which raises two questions. How can that much mass fit into something so small? And if you could somehow bring it here, what would it do?

0:24Mostly empty space

Start with something ordinary. A rock. A piece of iron. Your hand. It all feels solid. But zoom in, and keep zooming, until you reach the atoms. An atom has a tiny nucleus at its centre, with electrons around it. And in between… almost nothing. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a pea in the middle of the pitch. And yet that pea holds more than ninety-nine point nine per cent of the atom's mass. So ordinary matter is mostly empty space. If you could squeeze the emptiness out of every person on Earth, all of humanity would fit into a sugar cube.

That's the first clue. A neutron star is what happens when matter is forced into a state where ordinary atoms can no longer exist.

1:13What holds a nucleus together

To see how that's possible, we need to look inside the nucleus. A nucleus is made of protons and neutrons. And protons are positively charged. Two positive charges repel each other. Pack several protons together, and they should push themselves apart. And yet nuclei exist. Carbon exists. Oxygen exists. You exist. Something holds them together. It's the strong interaction between protons and neutrons. At very short distances, it overpowers the electric repulsion. But its reach is tiny. Beyond the width of a few protons, it fades away very quickly.

So particles have to get extremely close before it can grab them. Which brings us to the squeezing.

2:04How a star collapses

A star many times heavier than the Sun spends millions of years fusing light elements into heavier ones. It ends up with a core of iron, heavier than the Sun and roughly the size of the Earth. Iron is a dead end. Fusing it doesn't release energy. So once the core gets heavy enough, it can no longer hold itself up against its own gravity. It collapses. In less than a second, something the size of a planet shrinks to the size of a city. Under that pressure, protons start capturing electrons. Each capture turns a proton into a neutron, and releases a ghostly particle called a neutrino.

A flood of neutrinos bursts out of the core, carrying away most of the energy, and helping to blast the outer layers into space in a supernova.

2:55What's left behind

What's left behind is no longer made of ordinary atoms. The electrons that kept the nuclei apart are mostly gone. It's typically about twenty-five kilometres across, made mostly of neutrons, packed to roughly the density of an atomic nucleus. It's tempting to call it one giant atomic nucleus. But it isn't. The crust is a lattice of strange, neutron-heavy nuclei. Deeper down, they merge into shapes physicists call nuclear pasta. Below that is a fluid of neutrons. And at the very centre… nobody knows yet. What holds it up is the pressure of this ultra-dense matter. Partly a quantum effect, and partly the strong interaction pushing back at close range.

But beyond a certain mass, no known pressure can stop the collapse. The star becomes a black hole. So that's how the mass fits. Gravity crushes matter into a state where ordinary atoms can't exist, and packs it as tightly as a nucleus.

4:01A spoonful on Earth

Now for the second question. Suppose you could scoop out a teaspoon and bring it to Earth. There's a catch. The strong interaction isn't what holds this matter together. On their own, neutrons don't stick. It's the gravity of the whole star that keeps it squeezed. Take it out of the star, and nothing would hold it in. Its own pressure would take over, and it would decompress violently. As the matter decompressed, it would have to turn into ordinary matter. Even the energy difference between a neutron and a hydrogen atom adds up to more than the asteroid that wiped out the dinosaurs.

4:432017: two neutron stars collide

And this isn't just a thought experiment. In 2017, we watched something like it happen. Two neutron stars, about a hundred and thirty million light-years away, spiralled into each other and collided. Gravitational-wave detectors felt the collision. Then telescopes all over the world turned to look. They saw debris from the neutron stars expanding into space, glowing as it went. As that neutron-rich matter decompressed, it built heavy atoms. Astronomers identified what is most likely strontium, and models suggest gold and platinum too.

Some of the gold on Earth may have been made in collisions like this. Neutron-star matter, released from the pressure.

5:30Why does it weigh anything?

But there's an even stranger question hiding in that teaspoon. Not why it's so dense. Why it weighs anything at all.

Every neutron and every proton is made of three smaller particles, called quarks. So you'd expect a proton to weigh as much as its three quarks. Let's add them up. About one per cent. The quarks account for roughly one per cent of the proton. The other ninety-nine per cent comes from energy. The energy of quarks moving at nearly the speed of light, and of the gluon fields binding them together. Einstein showed that energy has mass. E equals m c squared. Inside a proton, that's where almost all the mass comes from. You may have heard that the Higgs field gives particles their mass. It does give quarks theirs. But the quarks' own mass is only about one per cent of the proton.

So the matter in your body is mostly massive because of the energy of the fields that bind it together.

And the same goes for the teaspoon. Billions of tonnes, and almost all of it is the energy of the strong interaction.

6:45Numbers the theory can't predict

Using supercomputers, physicists can calculate the proton's mass from quantum chromodynamics, and the result agrees with experiment. But that calculation starts from a few numbers the theory itself doesn't predict. How strong the strong interaction is. How heavy the quarks are. Those have to be measured, and then put in by hand. The Standard Model of particle physics contains around twenty such numbers. If the universe had a settings menu, these would be the sliders.

Take the speed of light. Exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. That number looks fundamental. But its exact value depends on the units we choose. Since 1983, the metre has been defined using the speed of light. Measure in feet, or in some alien unit, and the number changes completely. The speed itself matters enormously. The number is a fact about our ruler.

7:51A number with no units

But some numbers have no units at all. And those are different. The most famous one is the fine-structure constant. It sets how strongly electric charges interact with light. It helps set how big atoms are, all that empty space from the beginning, and the strength of chemical bonds. Its value is about 1/137. Metres, feet or alien units, everyone gets the same number. We can measure it with extraordinary precision. But we still don't know why its value is what it is.

Some calculations suggest that changing certain constants could leave a universe with no stable stars, or no complex chemistry. Is that luck? Is there a deeper theory that fixes them? Or are we one universe among many? Nobody knows.

8:47Back to the teaspoon

Think about that teaspoon again. Billions of tonnes of crushed matter. To explain it, we went from atoms to nuclei, from nuclei to neutrons, from neutrons to quarks and fields. And at the bottom of all those layers, we found a handful of numbers our best theories simply take as given.

Even if a deeper theory explains them one day, we'll have a new question. Why that theory? Maybe the universe has no settings menu. Maybe it does. Or maybe… we just haven't found it yet.

So here's a question for you. If physicists one day derive 1/137 from something deeper, would that answer the question, or just move it one level down? Let me know in the comments.
