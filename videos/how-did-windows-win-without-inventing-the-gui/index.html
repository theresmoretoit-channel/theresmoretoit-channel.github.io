How Did Windows Win Without Inventing the GUI?

10 min2026-10-03

Douglas Engelbart demonstrated a computer controlled with a mouse in 1968. Xerox PARC built the Alto in the 1970s and sold the Star in 1981. Apple launched the Lisa and then the Macintosh. All of them got there before Microsoft, and none of their machines ended up on most of the world's desks.

This video follows what happened in between: a contract with IBM, a wave of compatible PCs, a six-year lawsuit, and a loop that is very hard to break once it gets going.

The full narration of the video.

0:00What Windows didn't invent

Windows. Icons. Menus. A mouse. For most people, that is simply what a computer looks like. And most of them met it through Microsoft. But when the first Windows appeared, in 1985, almost none of that was new. Other companies had already built it. So here is the real question. If Microsoft didn't invent the graphical interface, how did Windows end up on most of the world's desks? To answer that, we have to look at everyone who got there first… and ask why they didn't win.

0:36Engelbart's demo

In 1968, in San Francisco, a researcher called Douglas Engelbart sat on a stage in front of about a thousand people. For ninety minutes, he showed them a computer controlled with a small wooden box on wheels. A mouse. Text on his screen linked to other text. And a colleague thirty miles away was editing the same document with him. Engelbart showed where computing could go. But it was a research system, on one large, expensive computer. Nobody could buy it.

1:12Xerox Alto

Five years later, a few people came much closer. At Xerox PARC, a research centre in California, a team built a machine called the Alto. The first ones ran in 1973. The Alto had a screen where every single dot could be controlled separately. So it could show different fonts. Pictures. And text that looked the way it would on paper. It had a mouse. It had windows that overlapped. It had menus that popped up where you clicked. And it wasn't a lab curiosity. Xerox built around two thousand of them. People used them every day, to write, to draw, to send each other email.

So by the mid-1970s, a large part of the modern desktop already existed. A decade before Windows. So why aren't we all using Xerox computers? The Alto itself was never sold as a product. But in 1981, Xerox did release a commercial machine built on the same ideas. The Star.

2:14Xerox Star

The Star had a desktop on the screen. Documents. Folders. A printer. An in-tray for mail. The idea that your screen looks like an office desk went on sale here. And each Star workstation cost more than sixteen thousand dollars. That was more than many people earned in a year. And Xerox sold it to offices as part of a whole networked system, not as a single computer. It was enormously influential. It was a commercial failure. Xerox had the ideas first. It didn't have a machine ordinary people could afford.

3:03Apple: the Lisa and the Mac

Which brings us to Apple. In 1979, Steve Jobs and a group of Apple engineers visited Xerox PARC. Xerox let them in on a deal. It got to buy a stake in Apple before the company went public. What they saw changed what Apple was building. But Apple didn't simply copy it. It hired people from PARC, and its own engineers spent years reworking the ideas. The first result was the Lisa, in 1983. A graphical interface. A mouse. Windows. Menus. And a price of nearly ten thousand dollars. The Lisa failed too. Then, in 1984, came the Macintosh.

Smaller. Simpler. About two and a half thousand dollars. And launched with one of the most famous adverts ever shown during a Super Bowl. This was the graphical interface as a consumer product.

4:01The catch

So if anyone was going to win, surely it was Apple? There was one catch. If you wanted a Macintosh, you had to buy it from Apple. Apple controlled both the hardware and the software. That made the Mac polished and consistent. It also meant that the Mac could only spread as fast as Apple could sell it.

4:22IBM needs an operating system

And to see why that mattered, we have to leave the graphical interface for a moment… and go back to 1980. That year, IBM was building its first personal computer. And it needed an operating system. It went to a small company called Microsoft. Microsoft didn't have one. So it licensed an existing system from another Seattle company, bought it outright the following year, and adapted it for IBM. On IBM's machines, it was called PC DOS.

4:56The detail in the contract

And here is the detail the whole story turns on. IBM's contract did not stop Microsoft from licensing the same system to other manufacturers. Microsoft kept the right to sell it to anyone else… as MS-DOS. At first, that didn't seem to matter very much. Who else would need it?

5:17The clones

The IBM PC came out in 1981. And IBM had built it mostly from parts anyone could buy. The one piece rivals couldn't legally copy was a small program IBM had written for a chip at the heart of the machine. In 1983, a company called Compaq started selling a computer that ran the same software as an IBM PC. Its engineers had recreated that small program from scratch, without copying IBM's code. Others did the same. Within a few years, dozens of companies were selling IBM-compatible PCs. They all competed with each other. On price. On speed. On features.

And almost all of them ran Microsoft's operating system. So by the time Windows arrived, Microsoft already had something Xerox and Apple didn't. It wasn't a better interface. It was a place on a huge number of computers made by companies it didn't own.

6:21Windows 1.0 and 2.0

Windows 1.0 came out in 1985. And honestly… it wasn't very good. Its windows couldn't even overlap. They were laid out side by side, like tiles. But it ran on top of DOS, on the PCs people already had. You didn't need a new computer. You didn't need to throw away your old programs. Windows 2.0, in 1987, added overlapping windows.

6:50Apple v. Microsoft

And in 1988, Apple sued Microsoft. Apple claimed Windows copied the look and feel of the Macintosh. But in 1985, Apple had signed an agreement that let Microsoft use parts of the Macintosh's visual design. The court found that most of what Apple was complaining about was covered by that licence… or couldn't be protected at all. Nobody could own the idea of a window on a screen. The case dragged on for six years. And while it did, the real contest was being decided somewhere else.

7:25Windows 3.0

In 1990, Microsoft released Windows 3.0. It was finally good enough. And it could run on the huge number of PCs that were already out there. PC makers started installing it on new computers before they were sold. And Microsoft made sure that was the easy choice. Under some of its licences, a manufacturer paid Microsoft for every computer it shipped with a certain processor… whether that computer came with Microsoft's software or not. So installing a rival system meant paying twice. In 1994, the United States government challenged those contracts, and Microsoft agreed to stop.

8:08The loop

But by then, something much harder to stop was already running. Software companies wrote programs for the platform with the most users. Users bought the platform with the most programs. Every new Windows user made Windows more worth writing for. Every new program made Windows more worth buying. And once that loop gets going, it is very hard for anyone else to get in.

8:35Windows 95

In 1995, Microsoft released Windows 95. A Start button. A taskbar. Long file names. And a launch so loud that people queued at midnight to buy an operating system. Microsoft said it sold seven million copies in the first five weeks.

So how did Windows win without inventing the graphical interface?

8:57How Windows won

Not by being first. Engelbart showed the way in 1968. Xerox made it work in the 1970s. Apple put it in people's homes in 1984. And not by being the best. For years, Windows was clearly behind the Mac. Windows won because Microsoft had spent a decade getting its software onto other people's computers. When the graphical interface finally became something everyone wanted, Microsoft was already there. Nobody invented the graphical interface in a single moment. It was built in steps, by many people, over twenty years. Microsoft's contribution wasn't the idea. It was getting the idea onto most of the desks on the planet.
