Why Might Aliens Look Surprisingly Human?

9 min2026-10-08

So if there is life on another planet, would it keep finding them too? And would an intelligent alien end up looking strangely familiar, or nothing like us at all?

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00Nature keeps making crabs

The crab has evolved at least five different times. Different groups of crustaceans, each on its own branch, kept ending up with the same body. A flat, wide shell. A tail tucked underneath. It happened so often that biologists gave it a name. Carcinisation. And it's become a running joke. Nature keeps trying to make a crab. But behind the joke is a serious idea. Evolution doesn't have to invent everything from scratch. When it faces the same problem, it often finds the same answer.

0:36Eyes, wings and swimmers

Look at the eye of an octopus. A lens, an iris, a retina. It looks a lot like yours. But our last common ancestor was a small, worm-like animal that had no such eye. Octopuses and humans built theirs independently. And by one estimate, eyes have evolved on their own more than forty times. Flight evolved four times. In insects, in pterosaurs, in birds and in bats. And look at these three. A shark. An ichthyosaur. A dolphin. A fish, a reptile and a mammal. The same shape, because water pushes back on all of them the same way.

1:16Two skulls

It works on land too. This is a wolf skull. And this is the skull of a Tasmanian tiger. They look remarkably alike. But the Tasmanian tiger was a marsupial. It carried its young in a pouch. It was more closely related to a kangaroo than to any wolf. This is called convergent evolution.

1:46What about aliens?

Which raises a question. In another video, we asked how strange aliens could be. But what if evolution on another planet keeps finding the same answers too? Could an intelligent alien end up looking… surprisingly familiar?

2:02Why animals have a head

Let's start with the simplest thing. You have a left side and a right side. That sounds trivial. But almost every animal that actively moves around is built the same way. If you mostly move in one direction, it pays to have a front. The front meets the world first. So that's where the senses go. Eyes, ears, a nose. Whatever the animal has. And it pays to have the brain right next to them, so the signals don't have far to go. Biologists call this cephalisation. Building a head. You can see it in worms, insects, octopuses and us.

An alien that walks, swims or flies through its world faces the same problem. And physics is the same there too.

2:50Brains are not enough

Now, the part that matters most for a technological species. A dolphin has a large brain. It's clever, it's social, it learns. But a dolphin will never light a fire, or forge a knife. It has no hands to make one with. Crows are clever too. Some of them carve twigs into hooks. But everything they make, they make with their beak. Humans got lucky. Our legs carry us around, which leaves our hands free to do everything else. Many researchers think it worked like a loop. Better hands made better tools. Better tools rewarded better brains. And better brains made better tools again.

So an alien that builds things probably doesn't need hands like ours. It might have tentacles. Or claws. Or a trunk. Or four legs and two arms. But it would probably need something that moves it around, and something else that handles the world.

3:48The octopus problem

And even that isn't enough. An octopus has eight incredibly skilful arms. It opens jars. It escapes from aquariums. But most octopuses survive only a year or two, and they're mostly loners. Everything an octopus learns dies with it. Nobody passes it on. A technological species needs hands and brains. But it also needs time, and others to learn from.

4:18Who we would actually meet

And now, the twist. There's an even bigger reason an alien we meet might look familiar. We won't be meeting all the life in the galaxy. Imagine a galaxy full of life. Countless microbes. Endless oceans of simple creatures. Maybe some clever animals. And just a handful of species that build radios and spaceships. If we ever meet an alien civilisation, it will come from that handful. We won't travel across the galaxy to meet alien grass. Alien grass won't call us. We'll meet the ones that can sense their surroundings, handle things precisely, work together and build machines.

In other words, the ones that solved the same problems we did. That's a filter. And it doesn't select for any random shape. It selects for a toolkit. That doesn't mean the alien will look human. It means some of its problems may look familiar.

5:18Eyes, and then a face

So what else might be in that toolkit? Probably some kind of vision. Wherever there's a star, light carries an enormous amount of information about the world. The eyes might see infrared, or ultraviolet. There might be three of them, or a hundred. But using two eyes a little apart is a cheap way to judge distance. It's one reason many hunters, like cats and owls, have eyes facing forward. And now something almost unsettling. Put the eyes at the front. Put the mouth and the nose there too, because that's where the food goes in.

Add a social species that needs to understand each other's moods. What do you get? Something that looks an awful lot like a face. Not because aliens copied us. Because social animals face the same communication problem.

6:13The dinosauroid

In 1982, a palaeontologist named Dale Russell tried a thought experiment. What if the dinosaurs had never died out? He took a small, clever dinosaur, a relative of Troodon, and imagined it evolving for another sixty-six million years. The result stood upright. It had a big head, forward-facing eyes, and hands with three fingers. He called it the dinosauroid. And many scientists criticised it. They said it was far too human. Basically, a person in a dinosaur costume.

6:49Rewinding the tape of life

And that's the real debate. The biologist Stephen Jay Gould argued, if you rewound the tape of life and played it again, you'd almost never get humans. Others, like Simon Conway Morris, argue the opposite. Convergence is so common that something like us was likely to appear.

7:08Both might be right

They might both be right.

The details wouldn't repeat. Gravity alone would change everything. On a heavy world, an alien might be short and massive. On a light one, tall and slender. It might have six limbs, four eyes and an exoskeleton, and look nothing like us. And still have a front, something to process information, something to move with, and something to build with. Human in architecture, not in appearance.

7:41A surprisingly small toolbox

And that might be the answer to both questions. Life in the universe could be wildly creative. Things without heads, without bodies, without anything we'd call a face. But the aliens that build spaceships may have had to solve the same problems we did. Give life a different world, and you might get something unimaginable. Give it the same physics and the same problems, and it might find some of the same solutions. Maybe the universe doesn't have one blueprint for intelligent life. Maybe it just has a surprisingly small toolbox.
