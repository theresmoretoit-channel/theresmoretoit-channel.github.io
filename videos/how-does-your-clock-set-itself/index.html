How Does Your Clock Set Itself?

10 min2026-11-16

This video goes through DCF77 near Frankfurt and WWVB in Colorado, how a radio wave carries zeros and ones, why a whole minute carries only fifty-nine bits, the parity bits that catch interference, the strange problem of when the time actually changes, daylight saving time and leap seconds, the weather forecasts hidden in the same signal, why many clocks only listen at night, which way the antenna should point, the atomic clocks behind the transmitter, and why a clock built for Germany can't make sense of Colorado.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A clock nobody ever set

Somewhere in your home, there might be a clock that nobody has ever set. It survives power cuts. It changes for daylight saving time on its own, in the middle of the night. It has no internet connection. No Wi-Fi, no GPS, no app on your phone. All it has is a tiny radio receiver. And once every second, it hears a single pulse. So how does a clock turn one pulse per second into the exact time, the date, and even the day of the week?

In Europe, those pulses come from DCF77, near Frankfurt. In America, from WWVB, in Colorado. Different transmitters, the same idea.

0:45Short dip, long dip

Take the German one. It sends a steady radio wave, day and night. And at the start of every second, it briefly turns that wave down. The trick is how long it stays down. A dip of a tenth of a second means zero. A dip of two tenths means one. That's the whole idea. No packets, no network. Just a radio wave that dips for a short time, or a longer one.

1:13The missing dip

One bit every second. Which means it would take a whole minute to send just sixty bits. And in fact, it sends only fifty-nine. In the last second of every minute, the wave doesn't dip at all. And that missing dip is the most important part of the entire signal. Because when the next dip arrives, the clock knows a new minute has just begun. The gap works like a full stop. Everything between two gaps is one complete sentence.

1:44What fits into fifty-nine bits

So what fits into a sentence of fifty-nine bits? The minute. The hour. The day of the month, the day of the week, the month, and the year. Most numbers are sent digit by digit. Forty-seven minutes isn't sent as forty-seven. It's sent as two separate digits, a four and a seven. Four bits are enough for any digit from zero to nine. The tens of minutes never go past five, so they get only three. And the year gets just two digits. As far as the transmitter is concerned, 2026 is simply twenty-six. The clock supplies the century itself.

2:24When radio isn't clean

But radio isn't a clean channel. A motor, a phone charger, a cheap LED bulb… any of them can smudge a dip. So after each block of numbers comes one extra bit, called a parity bit. It's chosen so that the number of ones in the block always comes out even. If interference flips a single bit, the count turns odd, and the clock knows something went wrong. But if two bits flip, the count is even again, and the mistake slips through. That's why many clocks don't trust a single minute. They wait for another valid one. And that's why setting itself can take several minutes, and on a bad day, much longer.

3:05When does the time actually change?

Which leaves a strange problem. The clock spends a whole minute collecting the time for ten forty-eight. But while it's collecting that message, it's still ten forty-seven. So when does it actually change? Because the message is an announcement. It says what the time will be when the next minute begins. The clock reads the whole sentence, waits out the missing dip, and when the next dip arrives, that dip is the exact moment. The bits tell the clock what time it is. The dip tells it when.

3:41Summer time and leap seconds

But the sentence has a few more words than just the time. Two bits say whether Germany is on winter time or summer time. And a third one is a warning. For a whole hour before the clocks change, it's switched to one. So your clock doesn't need to know which Sunday in March the clocks go forward, or which Sunday in October they go back. Nobody ever taught it the rule. It's simply told, an hour in advance. If Europe ever stops changing the clocks, the warning will simply never come again.

Another bit used to warn of a leap second, an extra second added now and then, because the Earth's rotation doesn't keep perfect time. That minute could have sixty-one seconds. The last one was added at the end of 2016. But in October 2026, the world's metrology authorities decided to end leap seconds. From 20 May 2027, UTC will run continuously.

4:44Weather in the time signal

And the first fourteen bits of every minute aren't about time at all. Since 2006, they've also been used to carry encrypted weather forecasts, for compatible weather stations. So the signal that sets your alarm clock could also tell someone else's weather station whether it would rain on Thursday.

5:04Why at night?

And yet most of these clocks don't listen all the time. Listening costs battery, so many of them try once a night, in the early hours. Long waves reach a clock in two ways: along the ground, and bounced off the upper atmosphere. After dark, that upper path changes, and the signal often carries further. And at three in the morning, fewer machines, chargers and lights are usually running nearby to drown it out. Between those check-ins, the clock runs on a quartz crystal, which can drift by a second or so a day. At the next successful check-in, the radio corrects that drift.

5:44Which way the antenna points

Even where it listens matters. The antenna is a small rod of ferrite, wrapped in wire. It hears best when the rod lies across the direction of the transmitter, not pointing at it. Turn the clock around, and a weak signal can vanish completely. Which is why one clock works on a shelf, and the same clock, two metres away, behind a reinforced concrete wall, never sets itself.

6:11Where the transmitter gets its time

So where does the transmitter get its time from? From PTB, Germany's national metrology institute, which runs atomic clocks. The second itself is defined by caesium atoms. One second is exactly 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the radiation they give off. So a cheap alarm clock on a bedside table is, quite literally, following a time standard defined by caesium atoms.

Not perfectly, though. The signal travels at the speed of light, and even that takes about three milliseconds for every thousand kilometres. The receiver adds a delay of its own, and your clock corrects for none of it. It doesn't need to. It only shows seconds.

7:06Other countries, other transmitters

From one site near Frankfurt, the signal reaches clocks up to about two thousand kilometres away. That's most of Europe. Switzerland had its own transmitter, called HBG, until the end of 2011. Its antennas needed replacing, too few people used it, and Swiss clocks could simply listen to Germany instead. Others kept theirs. Britain, France, Japan and China all run their own.

7:34WWVB in Colorado

And in North America, WWVB has broadcast from Fort Collins since 1963, with a time code since 1965. Same principle, different dialect. A zero is a dip of two tenths of a second. A one lasts half a second. And instead of one gap per minute, a longer marker comes every ten seconds, like commas in the sentence. Two in a row mean a new minute. The biggest difference is which time it sends. Germany broadcasts Central European civil time, shared by much of the continent. But the United States spans several time zones. So WWVB sends universal time, UTC, and you tell the clock where you are.

It doesn't even send the month. Just the day of the year, from one to three hundred and sixty-six.

8:27Different languages

None of these stations speak the same language. A clock built for Germany can't make sense of Colorado.

But on both sides of the Atlantic, it's the same quiet routine. Night after night, a small receiver lines itself up with an atomic time standard, hundreds or thousands of kilometres away.

So here's a question for you. If every radio time signal disappeared tonight, would you even notice? Let me know in the comments.
