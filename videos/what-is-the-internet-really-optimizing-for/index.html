What Is the Internet Really Optimizing For?

10 min2026-10-12

A recommendation system decides what millions of people get to see, yet it can't watch a video and tell what it's worth. So what does it go by instead, and what happens when everyone starts playing by its rules? From a change YouTube made in 2012 and a watermelon dropped off a roof to Facebook's 2018 feed change, a political party in Poland, a famous goldfish claim nobody can trace, and one unsettling answer to the Fermi paradox.

YouTube: Creator Blog (2012); Covington, Adams and Sargin, RecSys 2016; Goodrow, YouTube Official Blog (2021). Facebook and Poland: The Wall Street Journal, the Facebook Files (2021). Goldfish: Microsoft Canada, Attention Spans (2015); BBC More or Less (2017).

The full narration of the video.

0:00A long video and a short one

Imagine spending hours on an educational video. Researching the subject. Checking the facts. Writing the script. Creating original visuals. Then imagine making a short video, uploading it to TikTok, and watching it get hundreds of views within a few hours. Meanwhile, the carefully researched long video struggles to find anyone. Of course, a single example proves very little. The short might simply be more appealing. The algorithm might have found an audience for one and not the other. But the contrast raises a question. What exactly is the internet rewarding?

If the answer were simply quality, or effort, the online world might look very different. So perhaps the problem isn't the people making the content. Perhaps it's the system deciding what millions of people get to see.

0:55What can the system see?

And that system has a problem it can never fully solve. It can't see what a video is worth. It can't watch a careful explanation and a pointless stunt, and decide which one is more valuable to humanity. What it can see is what people do. Whether they click. How long they watch. Whether they skip, like, or share. Every one of those numbers is a clue about what people value. None of them is the value itself. And trouble starts when the clue becomes the goal.

1:35What does a click prove?

YouTube found that out early. For years, its recommendations leaned heavily on clicks. But a click only proves that a title or a thumbnail worked. Not that the video did. So in 2012, YouTube shifted its weight towards watch time instead. Four years later, its own engineers explained why in a research paper. Ranking by clicks, they wrote, often promotes deceptive videos that people don't finish. In a word, clickbait. Watch time is a much better clue. It's harder to fake. But it still isn't quality.

2:13Nuclear fusion or a watermelon?

Think about how a video earns it. First, it has to stop you leaving. A video about nuclear fusion needs a few minutes to get good. It has to set up the problem, the physics, and why anyone should care. A video of someone dropping a watermelon off a roof needs no introduction. No background knowledge. Nothing to remember. Not long to wait. The watermelon doesn't win because it matters more. It wins because it's easier to consume.

2:44One swipe away

And a feed makes that advantage enormous. Picture yourself scrolling. A scientist begins with a definition and a graph about the climate. You could learn something. But you'd have to put in some effort first. Swipe. A dog falls asleep sitting up. Instant reward. Swipe. An absurd stunt. Swipe. An argument. Swipe. Something unbelievable. When the next video is one swipe away, trying something new costs nothing. And so does leaving. Short videos can be brilliant, and long ones can be terrible. Length isn't the point. The point is that a creator now has a few seconds to convince you to stay. A complicated idea may need time to become interesting.

A shocking image is interesting before you even know what it is. Guess which one has the easier job.

3:42Who pays the platforms?

And why would a platform care how long you stay? Because most of them are paid by advertisers, and advertisers pay for attention. So picture two ways to spend twenty minutes. A careful explanation of a scientific discovery. Afterwards, something finally makes sense. Or a few hundred unrelated clips. Some funny. Some irritating. Most forgotten by the next one. To the platform, both look like engagement. Only one of them taught you something.

4:16Economists have a name for this

Economists have a name for this. Goodhart's law. When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Clicks were a fair sign of interest… until titles were written to win them. Watch time is a fair sign of engagement… but it can't tell whether the time was well spent. And then there's the third clue. Reactions. A comment can mean a post matters to people. Or that it made them furious.

4:44Facebook, 2018

In 2018, Facebook changed its feed to favour posts that got comments and reactions. Its own researchers later warned that the change was rewarding outrage. And publishers complained that their most divisive posts were the ones that spread. One political party in Poland told Facebook that, by its own estimate, its posts had gone from about half negative to about eighty per cent negative. And it said why. Because of the change to the feed. That's Goodhart's law at full strength. The metric didn't just measure what people posted. It changed what they posted.

5:26Are people getting stupider?

So is it the audience? Are people simply getting stupider? That would be too easy. And probably wrong. Humans have always loved spectacle. Romans had gladiators. Crowds gathered for public executions. Newspapers learned long ago that scandal sells. Even the famous claim that our attention span is now shorter than a goldfish's seems to come from nowhere. It appeared in a 2015 report from Microsoft, and nobody has found the study behind it. Or the goldfish.

6:01What has actually changed?

What has changed is the scale and the convenience. A broadcaster used to choose what was on tonight. Now a system picks the next thing for you, every few seconds, based on what you just did. You no longer search for entertainment. Entertainment searches for you. And if the easy option wins just a little more often, across millions of people, repeated thousands of times… that becomes a cultural force. The question isn't whether people are stupid. It's whether the environment lets them use their intelligence.

6:38Popular or valuable?

A low view count doesn't prove a video is good. A high one doesn't prove it's bad. But if popularity gets confused with value, the easiest things to consume become the easiest things to find. And everything else has to fight just to be noticed.

6:57So what's the answer?

So what's the answer? Ban short videos? Blame people for watching silly clips? No. Sometimes you want astrophysics. Sometimes you want a cat behaving like a broken robot. Both are fine. The problem is when one system becomes the main doorway to what we see and learn, and the number it chases isn't quite what we value.

7:21What the platforms are trying

Platforms do know this. YouTube asks viewers in surveys to rate videos from one to five stars, and tries to reward time people say was well spent. That's a better clue. But it's still a guess about what you value, made by a system that's paid by your attention. At the very least, we can stop pretending that the most popular content must be the best content. Because those are two different questions. What do people want to watch right now? And what do we want to still be there ten years from now? We need jokes and discoveries, ten-second distractions and ideas that take an hour. The goal isn't to get rid of the fun. It's to keep the slow and the difficult from disappearing beneath it.

8:10Where is everybody?

There's a stranger way to look at all this. The universe is thirteen point eight billion years old, and our galaxy alone has hundreds of billions of stars. Yet we've found no sign of anyone else. That's the Fermi paradox. Where is everybody? Maybe civilisations destroy themselves. Maybe the distances are too great. Maybe life is just rare. Or maybe they invent smartphones. And recommendation algorithms. And spend the rest of their existence watching increasingly ridiculous videos. Their spaceships stay unfinished. Their telescopes gather dust.

And somewhere, around a distant star, an entire civilisation is watching someone drop a thousand rubber ducks into a swimming pool.

There's no evidence that this explains the silence of the universe. But the unsettling part is that the algorithm wouldn't have to be evil. It wouldn't need to hate science. It would only need to be very good at doing what it was built to do.

We built the greatest knowledge-sharing machine in history. Now we have to decide whether to use it to understand the universe… or simply to pass the time until the next video begins.
