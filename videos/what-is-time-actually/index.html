Physics That Sounds Simple Until You Ask

What Is Time, Actually?

14 min2026-08-29

This video is about telling two things apart: time itself, and our feeling that time flows. That one distinction runs from ordinary clocks to Einstein, to entropy, to the brain — and to the question of whether there is any objective "now" at all.

Along the way we run into something the second law does not actually solve. Entropy explains why disorder keeps increasing. It does not explain why the universe started out so extraordinarily ordered in the first place. That is still open.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Two different things

What is time? At first glance it is one of the simplest things in the world. We have clocks. We have calendars. We know what time it is. We know that yesterday was yesterday and that tomorrow will be tomorrow. But stop for a moment and ask yourself a simple question. What is time, actually? And suddenly it is much stranger. Because we may be struggling to separate two completely different things. Time itself. And our feeling that time flows. And that one distinction takes us from ordinary clocks to Einstein, to entropy, to the brain, and to the question of whether there is any objective now at all.

0:43What a clock actually measures

When we say that an hour has passed, what has actually happened? A clock ticked a certain number of times. An atomic clock counted the transitions between the energy states of an atom. A pendulum swung back and forth. A planet turned through a certain angle. But notice something. Clocks do not create time. They only compare one regular change against another change. We say, for instance, that an event lasted ten seconds. And what we are really saying is this. During that event, as many regular changes went past as ten seconds are made of.

So in everyday life we come to know time through change. And this is where it starts to get interesting.

1:31Newton's universal clock

Before Einstein, physics pictured time very simply. Newton imagined time as something that exists entirely on its own. The universe can change. Objects can move. But somewhere in the background there is a universal clock. Tick. Tick. Tick. And it ticks in exactly the same way for everybody. Time was supposed to be a vast invisible river, flowing through the whole universe at one steady rate.

1:59Einstein and the speed of light

But Einstein turned up with a problem. And the problem was the speed of light. Under special relativity, light in a vacuum has the same speed for every observer. And that leads to something completely mad. Time is not the same for everyone. If two observers move at different speeds relative to each other, they can measure different intervals of time. So time is not a universal backdrop. It is part of the physical system. And here is the strange part. Each of the two will say that it is the other one's clock that runs slow. Not their own. The other one's.

It is not an optical illusion. It is not a fault in the clocks. Time really does pass differently for different observers. And that was only the beginning.

2:52Spacetime and the end of 'now'

Einstein's relativity joined space and time into a single object. Spacetime. So we do not simply have space and time side by side. We have spacetime. And that has one very odd consequence. When we ask what happened at the same time as some event, the answer need not be the same for every observer. Picture two people moving quickly relative to each other. They can agree that two events took place. But they need not agree on whether those events happened at the same moment. And that brings us to one of the strangest ideas in all of physics. There is no universal now.

We feel that there is one single moment that is genuinely present. Right now. You are watching this video. This is the present. But special relativity tells us there is no single universal slice of the present that holds for the whole universe. What counts as now for me need not count as now for an observer far away who is moving very fast.

3:57The block universe

And here a rather interesting philosophical possibility opens up. Perhaps the universe is not something that keeps changing from the past, through the present, into the future. Perhaps reality is more like a four-dimensional structure of events. This view is often called the block universe. But be careful. Relativity on its own does not prove that the block universe is definitely the right description. It is an interpretation that follows very naturally from the relativistic picture. And if it is right, then our idea of flowing time starts to look very strange indeed.

4:37The broken egg and entropy

So why do we experience time as a one-way street? Here is something everybody knows. A broken egg. Drop an egg on the floor and it breaks. But you never see the pieces gather themselves back up and the egg leap off the ground into your hand. Why not? Because of something we call entropy. Very roughly, a system has an enormous number of untidy states available to it, and far fewer tidy ones. A whole egg is one very particular state. Shards on the floor can be arranged in an unimaginable number of ways. So it is far more likely that a system moves from order towards disorder. And that is how we get the arrow of time.

5:23But entropy does not rise everywhere

But wait. That cannot be the whole story. Because entropy does not increase everywhere. Look around you. Crystals form. Stars form. Planets form. And eventually… things like us form. Life is an extraordinarily complex, highly structured system. So how can something that ordered exist in a universe that is supposedly moving towards disorder? Because the second law does not say that entropy must increase everywhere. It says that for an isolated system, the total entropy does not decrease. The Earth takes in energy from the Sun in a fairly concentrated form.

And it eventually sends that energy back out into space as more dispersed thermal radiation. In between, life gets to do interesting things.

6:14The laws do not know which way time runs

And now one of the loveliest paradoxes in the whole of science. Many of the basic laws of physics work almost equally well when we run time backwards. Picture a film of planets going round their orbits. Play it in reverse. At first glance nothing looks wrong at all. Many basic equations behave the same way. They do not say: here is the past. Here is the future. They never say that time must run in this direction. So where does our arrow of time come from?

6:47Why did the universe start so ordered?

One of the main explanations is entropy again. Our universe began in a state of extraordinarily low entropy. And from that very odd starting point, entropy has been rising ever since. That creates a direction which we experience as past, and then future. And here we run into an even deeper problem. The second law may explain why entropy keeps increasing. But only if we already start with a universe in a very special state. A state of extraordinarily low entropy. So perhaps the real question is not why entropy increases. Perhaps it is this. Why was the universe ever so ordered in the first place?

We do not yet have a complete answer. We thought we were asking what time is. But now we have found an even stranger question. Why does the universe have a direction at all?

7:47Memory, and why the past feels different

But entropy alone does not explain our whole feeling of time. Because we do not only measure time. We live through it. We feel that the past has already been, that the present is happening now, and that the future is still on its way. Why? One important reason is memory. We hold records of the past. We hold no memories of the future. When I remember yesterday's breakfast, my brain holds information about an event that really happened. But I hold no equally available record of what I am going to eat tomorrow. And that creates a very powerful asymmetry.

The brain is constantly taking information in, storing some of it, comparing the new against the old, and predicting what comes next. And out of that process comes our subjective feeling. I am moving through time right now.

8:43Your 'now' is not a point

And there is another problem. Our awareness cannot take in the whole world in a single mathematical instant. The brain needs time to process information. A signal from the eye has to reach the brain. The brain has to work on it. It has to compare it against other information. And only then does a conscious perception appear. So our now is not a perfectly sharp point. It is more like the outcome of an enormous amount of processing. The brain builds a model of reality that is consistent enough for us to function. And that model contains something we call the present.

9:25Why time flies and drags

And we can watch this happening every single day. We sit in a waiting room for two hours. And it feels like a whole day. Then we spend six hours playing a brilliant game. We look at the clock. And we say: is it really that late? The clocks were working in exactly the same way. The physics did not change. What changed was our experience of time. Attention, emotion, the amount of new information, and memory all shape how long a stretch of time feels. So when we say that the day went by terribly fast, we may not be talking about physical time at all. We are talking about our model of time inside the brain.

10:09So is time an illusion?

And here we should be careful. Saying that time is merely an illusion is far too easy. Time is not something we simply made up. Relativistic effects on time are real. Atomic clocks measure them. Satellite navigation has to correct for them. Time is part of our most successful physical theories. So time is certainly not just an invention of the human brain. But something else may well be an illusion. Our idea that time really flows. And perhaps also our idea that there is one universal moment. Now.

10:48A whole life in spacetime

Picture a whole life. Childhood. School. A first love. Work. Family. Today. And one day, the last moments of that life. We experience all of it as a journey from the past into the future. But it may be more accurate to picture a whole life as a sequence of events in spacetime. Every moment has its own place in that structure. And our awareness always holds information about some earlier states, and builds expectations of the states still to come. And that may be exactly what creates the feeling that time is flowing. This does not mean that we know that time does not flow. We do not know that.

It is one of the deep open problems on the border between physics and philosophy.

11:35What is time, then?

So what is time? Perhaps the best answer is a surprisingly modest one. We do not entirely know. In modern physics, time is part of the structure that describes events and the relations between them. Relativity showed us that it is not universal. Thermodynamics gives us the arrow of time through rising entropy. And our brain builds, out of those physical processes, a subjective experience of the past, the present and the future. So perhaps there are two quite different things. Physical time. And lived time. And they need not be the same thing.

So the next time you glance at a clock and read the hour off its face, you might remember what it is not showing you. There is no invisible river running through the universe. The clock shows you a relation between changes. And your brain turns that into something far stranger. The feeling that there is a past, that there is a right now, and that the future is still waiting ahead of us. And perhaps the greatest mystery is not where time is flowing to. It is why we feel that it is flowing anywhere at all. And if that feeling is only the way our brain reads change, memory and entropy, then the strangest thing about time may not be that it exists. It is that we can experience it at all.
