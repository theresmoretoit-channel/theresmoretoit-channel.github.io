Who Really Shot Down MH17?

11 min2026-10-20

Was that the confession investigators had been waiting for? To judge it, you first need to know what the evidence says without it. This video follows that evidence: the fragments found in the wreckage, a post that disappeared from social media, intercepted phone calls, photographs of a missile launcher on the move, Russia's changing explanations, and the verdict of a Dutch court. Then it checks the colonel's words the same way.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Flight MH17

On 17 July 2014, a Boeing 777 left Amsterdam for Kuala Lumpur. Flight MH17. Less than three hours later, over eastern Ukraine, it broke apart in the sky. Two hundred and ninety-eight people died.

0:18A clip from Russian radio

Twelve years later, while we were preparing this video, a clip from Russian radio went round the internet. In it, a retired Russian colonel appeared to say: "A Russian officer shot it down, yes." It looked like the admission investigators had been waiting for. We'll come back to what he actually said. Because to judge a confession, you first need to know what the evidence says without it. So who really shot down MH17? And how can anyone know, when the crime scene was in a war zone, and the truth itself became a weapon?

0:54What destroyed the plane?

The first question was the simplest one. What destroyed the aircraft? The Dutch Safety Board answered it in October 2015. A warhead exploded just above and to the left of the cockpit. It sprayed the front of the plane with hundreds of metal fragments. The three people in the cockpit were killed instantly. Some of those fragments had an unusual shape. A bow tie. That shape comes from one kind of warhead: the one carried by the Buk, a Soviet-designed surface-to-air missile. Investigators found those fragments in the wreckage, and in the body of the captain. And they rebuilt the front of the plane from recovered pieces, to check that the damage fitted.

So within fifteen months, the weapon was known. But a weapon is not a person. It doesn't tell you who fired it.

1:55On board

For the people on board, it came without warning. A blast, a sudden loss of pressure, violent wind and cold, and an aircraft coming apart. The fall lasted a minute to a minute and a half. Whether anyone stayed aware for part of it, the investigators could neither confirm nor rule out. What they did judge likely is that people were barely able to understand what was happening. That is why precision matters here. Two hundred and ninety-eight people, and their families, are owed the right answer.

Then there was the crime scene.

2:31The crime scene

The plane came down near Hrabove, in territory held by Russian-backed separatists, in the middle of a war. International monitors arrived the next day. Armed men decided where they could go. The bodies were taken away by train. The flight recorders were handed over four days later. Parts of the wreckage were moved or cut up. Dutch investigators could not search the site properly for months. So here is the problem. If the people holding the crime scene are part of the conflict, how do you trust anything found there? The answer is that you don't have to rely on it alone. Because a lot of the evidence was never lying in that field.

3:19Evidence posted online

Some of it was posted online within about an hour of the crash. A page on VKontakte, Russia's version of Facebook, called "Dispatches from Strelkov", announced that an An-26 had been shot down near Torez. Strelkov was the field name of Igor Girkin, the separatists' military commander. An An-26 is a military transport plane. The post added: "We warned them. Do not fly in our sky." When it became clear that the plane was a passenger jet, the post disappeared. So what does that prove? Not that Girkin wrote it. The page was run by his supporters, and he has denied writing it.

Not who fired the missile, either. What it does show is that so soon after the crash, someone on the separatist side was claiming a shoot-down, at that place and that time, of what they took for a military plane.

Phone calls intercepted later that day point the same way. Separatist fighters realised the plane had been civilian. Those calls came from Ukraine's security service, which is one side of the war. So the investigators had them checked for signs of editing.

4:35The launcher

Then there were the photographs. Earlier that day, people in Donetsk, Torez and Snizhne photographed and filmed a Buk launcher, mostly on a low loader, moving east. A single photo proves very little. It can be misdated, staged, or taken somewhere else. So each one was checked. Shadows against the time of day. Buildings against satellite images. Posts against when they first appeared. The next morning, a video from Luhansk showed the same kind of launcher heading for Russia, with one missile missing. And it wasn't just any launcher. Marks on its side matched a vehicle photographed weeks earlier in a Russian army convoy.

In 2018, the international criminal investigation, the Joint Investigation Team, concluded that the launcher came from Russia's fifty-third Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade, based in Kursk. No single piece proves the case. What matters is how the photographs, recordings and physical evidence fit together, and whether independent checks support the same conclusion.

5:45Russia's explanations

Russia offered other explanations. Several of them. In July 2014, the Russian Defence Ministry pointed to a Ukrainian Su-25 warplane flying near the airliner. In 2015, the Buk's Russian maker agreed that it was a Buk, but said it was an older missile, fired from a village held by Ukraine. In 2016, the ministry released new radar data. This time, there was no warplane near MH17 at all. In 2018, it produced a serial number, and said the missile had belonged to Ukraine. Here we need to be careful. Changing stories don't prove guilt. People who are innocent can get things wrong, too.

What the changing stories did, in practice, was offer a choice of answers. And when there are so many answers, it's easy to feel there's no answer at all. The investigators examined the Russian claims. None of them changed the conclusion.

6:50Back to the colonel

Which brings us back to that colonel. His name is Viktor Baranets. Once a spokesman for a Russian defence minister, now a military commentator for the newspaper Komsomolskaya Pravda. For years, he had dismissed the case against Russia. Then, on the paper's radio station in September 2026, he said: "A Russian officer shot it down, yes, because that pest barged into our sky." A few weeks later, the clip went viral. It looked like the missing piece. It even echoed the Strelkov post: "our sky". So let's check it the way we checked everything else.

In the same answer, he said "Soviet officers" did it. He said the plane had been warned by radio, by manoeuvres, and by tracer fire across its nose. None of that happened to MH17. No fighter chased it. It was hit by a missile from the ground. But it matches another shootdown, as Moscow described it at the time. On 1 September 1983, a Soviet fighter shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007, which had strayed into Soviet airspace. Two hundred and sixty-nine people died. There, "our sky" makes sense. Baranets almost certainly mixed up two tragedies.

So it isn't a confession. Not one we can honestly use. And this is the uncomfortable part. We wanted it to be true. It fitted what the evidence already says. That is exactly when you have to check hardest. A claim that confirms what you believe gets the same test as one that denies it. And the case doesn't need him.

8:38What has been proven?

So what has actually been proven? In November 2022, a Dutch court convicted three men for their part in the downing. Igor Girkin, Sergei Dubinsky and Leonid Kharchenko. Life sentences, in their absence. A fourth man, Oleg Pulatov, was acquitted. That matters, too. The court weighed each man separately. In 2023, the investigators reported "strong indications" that the decision to supply the Buk was taken by President Putin. Strong indications are not proof. And supplying a weapon is not ordering it to be fired. The investigators could not establish who ordered the missile to be launched.

The crew were never identified well enough to be charged. Most likely, they took it for a military plane. The investigation was suspended. In the investigators' words, it had reached its limits. In 2025, two international bodies, the UN's civil aviation council and the European Court of Human Rights, found Russia responsible.

9:50One more failure

And one more failure belongs on the list. Ukraine had closed its eastern airspace up to thirty-two thousand feet. MH17 was flying at thirty-three thousand. The Safety Board found that the flight broke no rules, but that Ukraine had reason to close the airspace completely, and that nobody had properly weighed the risk. That doesn't excuse the missile. Several failures can sit inside one tragedy.

10:20So who shot it down?

So, who really shot down MH17? A Buk missile, from a Russian army launcher, fired from separatist-held ground. Three men convicted. And the hand that fired it, still without a name. Gaps like that shouldn't be hidden. But they don't mean that nothing can be known. None of this rests on a confession. It rests on pieces of evidence that were checked, and still fit together. The truth isn't the claim that sounds right. It's the one still standing after we check it.
