Why Can't You Stop Scrolling?

11 min2026-11-09

Doomscrolling means carrying on through bad news, even though you know it's making you feel worse. This video goes through more than twenty thousand headline tests, a game with two rocks and a hidden snake, the designer who came up with infinite scroll, 806 Twitter users and two versions of their feed, and the Boston Marathon bombing. And it ends with what you can actually do about it.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Ten yellow pens

Ten yellow pens on a table. Some of them will give you an electric shock. Nobody will tell you which. You're a student, waiting for an experiment to start. You're told the pens are left over from an earlier one, and you can click them to pass the time. There's no prize. The only thing a click can get you is the answer to one question: does this one shock? On average, students clicked about five. A second group got labelled pens. Red for a shock, green for nothing. They clicked about three. The only difference was not knowing. The researchers tried it again with chalkboard screeches. People kept clicking, and they felt worse for it.

0:45A phone in your hand

Now take away the pens, and put a phone in your hand. You unlock it to check the news. There's a headline about a war. Then another article. Then a video of something terrible. Forty-five minutes later, you're still there. You feel worse than when you started, and you can't do anything about most of it. One more article. One more update. One more video.

1:10Doomscrolling

This has a name: doomscrolling. Carrying on through bad news, even though you know it's making you feel worse. So why would anyone spend their free time on something that makes them miserable? Why doesn't the brain simply say: enough, let's watch something funny? There are three parts to the answer. Two of them are in your head. The third one isn't.

1:35Something behind a bush

You're walking through a forest, and you hear something behind a bush. It might be the wind. It might be a bird. Or it might be a predator. Ignore the wind, and nothing happens. Ignore the predator, and that's the end of you. A false alarm costs a few seconds. A missed threat costs everything. So the usual story goes, a brain that overreacts to danger is the one that survives. Psychologists call the result the negativity bias.

2:06Twenty thousand headlines

And you can see how it affects what people click. A news site called Upworthy used to test its headlines: same story, different wording, shown at random. Researchers later went through those tests, over twenty thousand of them. Each extra negative word raised the click rate by about two percent. Each positive word lowered it by about one. But two percent is a nudge, not a trap. And in that data, words of fear actually lowered clicks. So a brain hunting for predators can't be the whole story. It might get you to click one headline. You could read it, put the phone away, and move on. So why don't you?

2:49A snake under a rock

In 2016, researchers in London sat forty-five people down with a game of two rocks. Each time, you guess: is there a snake under this one? If a snake appears, you get a real electric shock in the hand. And the odds keep changing. Sometimes one rock is nearly always the dangerous one. Sometimes it's fifty-fifty. Every few rounds they asked how stressed people felt. The shocks mattered, of course. But stress also rose and fell with something else.

3:21Fifty-fifty

Uncertainty. And uncertainty is highest at fifty-fifty. As the lead author summed it up, not knowing whether you'll get a shock can be more stressful than knowing you definitely will or won't.

3:34A war that's just begun

Now imagine a headline about a war that's just begun. Something serious is happening, and you don't know how bad it will get. Will it escalate? Will another country get involved? Will it calm down by morning? Nobody can tell you. You don't know the odds. That's the point. So you look for another article. It answers some of your questions, and it raises new ones. So this may not be about enjoying the information at all. It may be about trying to get rid of not knowing. Surveys do find that people who struggle most with uncertainty tend to doomscroll more.

And here's the trap. The more you try to eliminate uncertainty, the more information you find that creates new uncertainty. You're trying to reach the point where you finally understand what's happening. But there's always another update, and that point keeps moving. Every headline is a yellow pen.

That's the two parts in your head. Here's the third.

4:37The last page

A newspaper has a last page. A news broadcast ends. Without saying a word, both of them tell you that you're done. In 2006, a designer called Aza Raskin came up with infinite scroll. It was meant to make browsing easier: no more clicking through to the next page. It also got rid of the last page. He later said he regretted it. If you don't give your brain time to catch up with your impulses, he said, you just keep scrolling.

5:08What the algorithm shows you

And what comes next usually isn't random. Ranking systems try to predict what you'll click, watch, share or reply to. In February 2023, researchers had eight hundred and six Twitter users install a browser extension. It captured what the algorithm was showing them, and what they'd have seen in plain time order. The algorithm's feed had more anger, above all, and more hostility to the other political side. Asked how each tweet made them feel, readers reported more anger, anxiety and sadness. And, to be fair, a little more happiness too.

Then came the question. Do you actually want to be shown tweets like this one? Overall, the algorithm's picks did only slightly better. And for political tweets, it lost. People were less keen on what it chose for them than on what they'd have got in plain time order. So what you react to isn't necessarily what you want to see. The algorithm doesn't need to know whether you're happy. It needs to predict whether you'll interact with the next post. And sometimes, being upset is enough. That's one platform, in one month. And yes, this video probably reached you the same way.

Put the three together, and you get a loop. Something disturbing makes you anxious. Anxiety sends you looking for more. The next thing you find is a new threat.

6:37Does it do you any harm?

But does it actually do you any harm? Surveys, from Iran to the United States, link doomscrolling to anxiety. But perhaps anxious people simply seek out bad news. So in 2021, one team tried an experiment. About nine hundred people were randomly assigned: real COVID news on Twitter or YouTube, or nothing at all. They got between two and four minutes of it. That was enough to lower their mood. Stories of kindness from the same pandemic didn't do that.

7:10Boston, 2013

For the long run, the best evidence comes from Boston. Two to four weeks after the 2013 marathon bombing, researchers surveyed over four and a half thousand Americans. How much coverage had they followed? Some of them were connected to it directly. They'd been there, or they knew someone who had. Others had followed it for six hours a day or more. Heavy media exposure was more strongly associated with acute stress than direct exposure. A survey can't prove that the coverage caused the stress. So the team kept following the same people.

Heavy viewers had more stress symptoms six months later. Those symptoms went with more worry about the future. Then, in 2016, came the Orlando nightclub shooting. The people who worried most followed it more closely, and it hit them harder. That's still not proof. But it's the shape a loop would leave behind. Anxiety sends you to the news, the news feeds the anxiety, and both can be happening at once.

8:17Staying informed

Perhaps the strangest part is that it feels productive. You're not wasting time. You're staying informed. And that's not an illusion. In 2018, an experiment paid some Americans to quit Facebook for four weeks. They knew less about the news afterwards. They reported slightly higher subjective well-being, and they had an extra hour a day. So there's a real cost to looking away. The question is what the tenth look gets you. Say you check the same story ten times in one evening. Are you ten times better informed? You've picked up a few details, and spent an hour going over things you can't influence.

9:01What can you do about it?

So what can you do about it? The obvious advice is to stop scrolling. Which is about as useful as telling someone hungry to stop thinking about food. The evidence for fixes is thinner than the evidence for the problem. The closest test is about email, not news: in a week of checking it three times a day, people felt less stressed. So you could try putting the last page back. Decide what you want to know before you open the feed. Pick a time to look. And when the fifth post says what the fourth one said, stop.

9:35Back to the pens

There's one more thing, and it comes from the pens. The same researchers hid pictures of insects, and let people click to reveal them. Some were first asked how they'd feel afterwards. They clicked less, and they ended up happier. Nobody told them to stop.

9:55So why can't you stop?

So why can't you stop scrolling? Because your brain is doing something that once made sense. It's watching for threats, and trying to find out what happens next. Those instincts used to run out of newspaper. Now they never do. Being aware of everything happening in the world doesn't mean you have more control over it. Sometimes it just means you're carrying more of it around in your head. You can care about what's happening without watching it happen. And the world isn't only what's terrible on the internet right now. It's also whatever is right in front of you.

So here's a question for you. What do you keep checking, even though it never changes what you do? Let me know in the comments.
