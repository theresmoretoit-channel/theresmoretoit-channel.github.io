How Do You Make Microplastics Without Knowing It?

12 min2026-10-02

A kitchen sponge. A cutting board. Containers in a dishwasher. Clothes in a washing machine. A string trimmer. The soles of your shoes. A toothbrush. An artificial football pitch. They don't look like the same problem, but they all involve plastic being worn away simply by being used.

This video goes through the research behind each of them: what the studies measured, what they only estimated, and what we still don't know about what these particles do to our bodies.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The plastic you never throw away

Have you ever wondered how much plastic you actually throw away? Bottles. Packaging. Plastic bags. Broken toys. All the obvious things. But what if some of the plastic around you doesn't have to be thrown away at all? What if you can create microplastics… simply by using something? Because some of the things we use every day are slowly wearing themselves away. And some of that missing material doesn't disappear. It becomes tiny plastic particles. And you may be producing them right now. In another video, we followed one of the biggest sources of microplastic pollution. The tyres on your car.

This time, we'll stay much closer. Much closer to your hands.

0:47Kitchen sponges

A kitchen sponge. You use it to remove food from a plate. You squeeze it. You scrub with it. You rinse it. And every time you do that… the sponge itself is being worn away. A 2026 study measured microplastic release from kitchen sponges during normal use. Depending on the type of sponge, the researchers estimated roughly zero point seven to four point two grams of microplastics per person per year. That doesn't sound like much. Until you remember what is happening. You are using an object made partly of plastic… to clean your dishes… and some of that object is going down the drain.

1:27Magic erasers

And ordinary kitchen sponges aren't the only cleaning products that behave this way. Melamine cleaning sponges, the famous white "magic erasers", can produce millions of microscopic polymer fibres as they wear down. That means about six and a half million fibres for every gram that wears away. The sponge isn't just cleaning the surface. The sponge is also slowly disappearing.

2:02Cutting boards

Now let's move from the sink to the kitchen counter. Because there is another object that can put plastic surprisingly close to your food. A plastic cutting board. Every time a knife hits the board, it doesn't only cut the food. It also scratches the board. The knife creates grooves. The surface becomes rougher. And tiny pieces of the polymer can be removed. Researchers have measured microplastics released from both polyethylene and polypropylene cutting boards during chopping. And newer experiments have even detected plastic particles transferred from worn cutting boards onto meat.

So when you see thousands of little knife marks on a plastic cutting board… you're looking at something more than cosmetic damage. You're looking at evidence that the surface has been physically removed.

2:56The dishwasher

But the kitchen has one more trick. The dishwasher. We normally think of a dishwasher as a machine that just cleans the dishes. But the cleaning process involves hot water, detergents, jets of water… and mechanical forces. Researchers have now tested plastic containers and utensils made from several common polymers in domestic dishwashers. They found that mechanical dishwashing can release both microplastics and nanoplastics. In other words… the thing you put into the dishwasher to get clean… can itself shed material during the process.

3:34The washing machine

And the dishwasher has a neighbour. Your washing machine. You probably already know that synthetic clothes can release microfibres. Polyester. Nylon. Acrylic. These materials are made from polymers. And when you wash them, the fabric is repeatedly stretched, bent and rubbed against itself. Tiny fibres can break away. And they leave with the wastewater. Researchers have been studying this for years. But different studies get very different numbers. The fabric matters. So does the machine, the washing programme… and even the way researchers count the fibres.

So there isn't one magic number. But the basic phenomenon is clear. Washing synthetic textiles can release microfibres. And those fibres can end up in wastewater.

4:30The string trimmer

And now let's leave the house. Because one of the strangest examples is waiting in your garden. The string trimmer. You probably think of the spinning nylon line as a tool. But it is also something that gets used up. It hits grass. It hits weeds. It hits stones. It hits fences. And as it hits those things, some of the line wears away or breaks off. Researchers actually tested what happens during this process. They estimated that thousands of microplastic particles are released every minute. But the really surprising part was the nanoplastics.

The researchers estimated that the trimmer could release billions of nanoscale plastic particles per minute under their test conditions. Billions. From a machine whose job is simply… cutting grass. This isn't plastic pollution from throwing something away. The plastic is being used exactly as intended.

5:31Shoes

The same basic thing happens under your feet. Your shoes. Most people think of a shoe as leather, fabric, rubber… something that protects your feet. But modern soles often contain synthetic polymers such as polyurethane and synthetic rubber. And soles wear down. Every step creates friction. A little material disappears. Then another little bit. And another. Researchers have measured microplastic particles produced by footwear abrasion. In outdoor environments, particles from shoe soles have even been detected alongside fibres from synthetic clothing.

So every step is a tiny wear event. Usually far too small to notice. But multiply it by thousands of steps. Millions of people. And years of use. And suddenly the missing material becomes a source of pollution.

6:30Toothbrushes

And then there is something even closer to your body. Your toothbrush. You use it twice a day. The bristles bend thousands of times. They rub against your teeth. They rub against each other. And eventually they become worn. A 2025 study simulated normal brushing with commercial toothbrushes and found microplastic particles released from their bristles. The exact amount varied between products. The worst one released about thirty-nine particles a day, on average. That doesn't mean you are swallowing thirty-nine dangerous particles every morning. The study did not establish that.

It simply shows something much more basic. A plastic toothbrush can shed plastic particles during ordinary use. And that's important because this is not an unusual failure. The toothbrush is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

7:30Artificial turf

Until now, most of our examples have been things inside a house. But we can make the scale much larger. Consider an artificial football pitch. It looks like grass. But it isn't. The blades are synthetic fibres. And the surface is subjected to thousands of footsteps, running, turning, sliding and jumping. The fibres bend. They stretch. They rub. And they can eventually break away. Researchers studying artificial turf have found that mechanical activity can contribute to the release and movement of synthetic fibres and other plastic materials.

But this is also an area where the science is still developing. We don't yet have a perfect number for how much every artificial pitch releases. Different turf systems use different materials. Different infills behave differently. And weathering, sunlight and maintenance all change the result. But the mechanism is easy to understand. Something made from plastic is being repeatedly rubbed against shoes. And some of that material can leave the pitch.

8:41The pattern

And now we can see the pattern. A sponge. A cutting board. Containers in a dishwasher. Clothes in a washing machine. A string trimmer. A shoe. A toothbrush. Artificial grass. They don't look like the same problem. But they are. They all involve something being worn. And that gives us a very different way of thinking about plastic pollution.

9:06Plastic, friction, wear, particles

We normally imagine it like this. A plastic object is thrown away. Then it breaks down. Then it becomes smaller. Then eventually… microplastics. But that isn't the only pathway. Sometimes it looks like this. Plastic. Friction. Wear. Particles. No bottle has to be thrown into a river. No plastic bag has to be abandoned on a beach. The object can be working perfectly. And still lose tiny pieces of itself. That may be one of the strangest things about microplastic pollution. The plastic doesn't always become pollution because we used it incorrectly.

Sometimes… pollution is the consequence of using it correctly.

9:55What we don't know

And there is one more important point. Finding microplastics does not automatically tell us what they are doing to our bodies. Just as with the tyres, we still don't know what level of exposure is harmful. So this isn't a story about a toothbrush poisoning you… or a cutting board secretly making you sick. The science isn't there to justify claims like that.

10:21A world of durable materials

It is a story about something much simpler. We built a world out of materials that are extremely durable. And then we made those materials rub against things… wash against things… cut against things… walk on things… and spin through things… every single day. With the tyres, we asked where all that missing tread went. Here, the question is the same. Only this time, they're things we use every day. Sometimes the missing material goes somewhere we can see. And sometimes… it becomes so small that we can't see it anymore. But becoming invisible isn't the same as disappearing.

And that leaves us with a strange question. If some plastic pollution is created simply by using the things around us… how much microplastic are we creating… without ever realising it?

But now there's another question. If we can't stop using all these things… what can we actually do about it? That's a question for another video.
