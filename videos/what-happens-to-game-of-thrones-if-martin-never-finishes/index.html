What Happens to Game of Thrones If Martin Never Finishes?

10 min2026-09-22

The drive belonged to Terry Pratchett. It held up to ten unfinished novels, and nobody will ever read them.

That wasn't vandalism — it was exactly what Pratchett wanted, and he had left instructions to do it. But look at who actually did it. Pratchett was already dead. Somebody else started the engine.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A steamroller and ten unfinished novels

In August 2017, at a steam fair in Dorset, a six-and-a-half tonne steamroller drove over a computer hard drive. Then they put what was left of it through a steam-powered stone crusher. The drive belonged to Terry Pratchett. It held up to ten unfinished novels. Nobody will ever read them. This wasn't vandalism. It was exactly what Pratchett wanted. He had left instructions to do it. But look at who actually did it. Pratchett was already dead. Somebody else started the engine.

0:39Fifteen years of The Winds of Winter

George R. R. Martin has been writing The Winds of Winter for fifteen years. He has said that if he dies first, the book simply won't be finished. That sounds like an instruction. But an instruction needs somebody to carry it out. And that somebody won't be him.

0:58The DOS machine, and what else is on it

Martin writes on a DOS machine running WordStar 4.0. He keeps two computers. One for email. One that only writes. He has said there are around eleven hundred pages of the book on it. That part isn't a rumour. He has repeated it publicly for years. What nobody outside that room knows is everything else. Older drafts. Chapters with two different endings. Scenes he wrote and then abandoned. Notes about where a character ends up, three hundred pages later. So the first question isn't who finishes the book. Who decides what happens to the files?

1:43Kafka told Brod to burn everything

That question has been answered before. Franz Kafka left instructions with his friend Max Brod. Burn everything. Unread. Brod didn't. The Trial exists because of that. So does The Castle. Every novel Kafka wrote is a disobeyed instruction. Brod had told him to his face that he would refuse. Kafka left him the papers anyway. Nobody asks the manuscript. They ask whoever is holding it.

2:23Two hundred boxes in Texas

Martin has already answered part of this question. He answered it in 1992. That year a truck left Santa Fe carrying his papers to Texas. Some of the earliest pages of A Game of Thrones were in it. Still on dot matrix paper, with the perforated edges attached. Today that archive runs to over two hundred boxes. Manuscripts. Letters. Notes. Board games. Replica swords. Most of it is open to any researcher who asks. His private correspondence is closed. And the rule for the closed part is written down. To see it, you have to present a letter of permission.

A letter from Martin.

It is a rule that will one day be impossible to satisfy. The door doesn't lock when he dies. It just stops having anyone behind it.

3:21Publish the mess: Tolkien and Nabokov

There is a third answer, and it has been used before. Publish the mess. Christopher Tolkien spent decades editing his father's unfinished papers. Not into a finished novel. Into twelve volumes of drafts. Versions that contradict each other. Notes in the margin. Dead ends. Vladimir Nabokov asked for his last manuscript to be destroyed. His son published it instead, as photographs of the index cards. You can see the handwriting. You can see the crossings-out. Nobody there pretended to be the author. They published what was on the page.

Now picture that in Texas. A historian opens a box and finds something labelled The Winds of Winter, draft seven. A chapter with three different endings. A note in the margin saying: this doesn't work. Another one saying: try again. And a later version that contradicts them both. For a historian, that's a treasure. For anyone who just wants to know how the story ends, it's torture.

4:31Finish it: Dickens and the Vermont printer

And then there is the fourth answer. Finish it. When Martin is asked about this, he reaches for one comparison. The Mystery of Edwin Drood. Charles Dickens died in 1870, halfway through a murder mystery. He had told his biographer how it ends. He had not written it down. Three years later, a printer in Vermont finished it. He said Dickens had asked him to. At a seance. A note appeared, signed with Dickens's name. The printer said he wrote the rest of the book word for word, exactly as it came to him. He included a preface that Dickens had supposedly dictated.

Decades later, Arthur Conan Doyle went through it hunting for the fake. He checked the spelling. He checked it for Americanisms. He found almost none.

The manner was right, he decided. The trick of thought was right. It just sounded, he said, like Dickens gone flat. But look at the shape of it. A reconstruction that passes every test anyone knows how to run. And underneath it, a story nobody would call Dickens. That was 1873.

5:51A thousand endings, and pressure to pick one

Now imagine the same thing with much better tools. Software could read every draft on that DOS machine. Line them up. Date them. Find the contradictions. And work out which ending he was moving towards. It wouldn't produce one book. Change how you read one vague note and you get a different ending. Trust the later drafts more and you get another. You could generate hundreds of them. Thousands. So which one is the real one? Version 37? Version 814? And there would be pressure to pick one. Publishers would want it. Fans would want it. The people who own the rights would have every reason to want it.

And somewhere in the world there would be a writer saying… I can finish this.

6:46The producers already know part of it

There's one more detail that makes all of this worse. We already know part of the ending. In 2013, Martin sat down with the television producers and told them how it ends. Not the whole thing. A handful of destinations. What happens to Hodor. What happens to Shireen. That Bran ends up on the throne. Think of the story as a map. He gave them some of the places it has to arrive at. He didn't give them the road. The series had to build its own. It cut characters. It merged storylines. Which means a reconstruction could hit every single destination and still be a completely different book.

7:31What only a living author can do

Because there is one thing no amount of material can replace. A living author can throw away three hundred pages. He can decide a character survives. He can rewrite the whole ending. He can look at your reconstruction and say… No. That isn't what I meant. That is the real difference. Not that the book is unfinished. That the author can still say no.

Once he's gone, the manuscript stops being instructions. It becomes evidence. Evidence of what he was trying to do. Not a description of what he'd have done.

8:14The quietest answer of all

There is one last answer, and it's the quietest of all. The estate does exactly what he asked. Nothing gets finished. Nothing gets published. The files are kept, and sealed. It's his work. He gets to say. But look at it once more. Even that is somebody else's decision.

Pratchett's estate said yes, and started the steamroller. Brod said no, and that is why we have Kafka. Christopher Tolkien published the contradictions too. The printer in Vermont didn't ask anybody.

Martin has said what he wants. But saying what you want is not the same as deciding. The files might not survive him. The archive will. So will the theories. He can still have the last word. He just has to finish it himself.

Otherwise it belongs to whoever opens the archive and asks for a letter that nobody can write.
