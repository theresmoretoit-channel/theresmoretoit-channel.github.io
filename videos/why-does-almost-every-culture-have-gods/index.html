Why Does Almost Every Culture Have Gods?

8 min2026-10-26

Almost every culture we know of has spirits, ancestors or gods. But for most of human history, the greatest gods people believed in probably weren't moral judges. So this video asks two questions: why do humans imagine invisible minds so easily, and when did those minds start watching us?

Along the way: a puppet show with a baby mouse and an alligator, hunter-gatherer societies whose high gods mostly stayed out of human affairs, the psychologist Ara Norenzayan's idea of "big gods", a coin game played with 591 people in eight communities from Fiji to Tanzania, and a huge study that tried to settle which came first: big gods or big societies.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Princess Alice is watching

A child is alone in a room with a throwing game and a prize. There are three rules, and they make it almost impossible to win. Before the game, the researcher points to an empty chair in the corner. It isn't really empty, he says. A friendly invisible princess, Alice, is sitting in it. Then he leaves the room, and reminds the child that Alice is watching. Nobody else is there to check. A hidden camera is recording, but the child doesn't know that. The children who believed in Alice held out about as long as children watched by a real adult.

And the sceptical ones? Most of them did cheat… but only after they'd checked that Alice really wasn't there. A hand waved across her empty chair. An invisible mind, invented only minutes earlier… and for the children who believed in her, it worked like a real one.

0:55Two questions

That's a small version of something humans have done everywhere. Almost every culture we know of has spirits, ancestors or gods. But here's the strange part. For most of human history, the greatest gods people believed in probably weren't moral judges. They didn't watch whether you cheated, or punish you for it. So there are really two questions. Why do we imagine invisible minds so easily? And when did they start watching us?

1:27A mind without a body

Part of the first answer came up in an earlier video. Our brains are built to detect minds, and they would rather see one too many than one too few.

But there's a stranger ingredient. We don't only detect minds. We find it easy to imagine a mind with no body attached. In another experiment, young children watched a puppet show. A baby mouse goes for a walk in the woods… and gets eaten by an alligator. Then the researchers asked questions. Does the mouse still need to eat? Most of the youngest children said no. Its body has stopped working. Does it still want to go home? Does it still love its mother? Most of them said yes. The body had stopped. Much of the mind, they assumed, carried on.

Put those two things together. A brain that sees minds everywhere, and finds it natural for a mind to exist without a body. Invisible beings are almost the default. But an invisible being isn't the same thing as a god who watches you and cares whether you lie.

2:38Hunter-gatherers and their gods

When anthropologists compared hunter-gatherer societies, more than half had no known high god, no supreme creator or ruler of the world. And most of the others had one who stayed out of human affairs. Out of every eight societies, about seven had no high god who took an interest in people. Their world was full of spirits. A spirit could send rain or sickness, or punish a broken taboo. But it usually wasn't an all-seeing judge of how you treated your neighbour. In a small community, gossip was the surveillance system.

3:16Trusting strangers

Then societies grew. Towns, cities, kingdoms, full of people you'd never meet again, and promises nobody else had heard. Gossip doesn't reach that far. So how do you trust a stranger? One answer, proposed by the psychologist Ara Norenzayan, is gods. Big gods. A god who sees everything, including what you do in private. Who cares how you treat strangers. And who punishes. If everyone around you believes that, you don't need to know the stranger. You only need to know that the stranger believes they're being watched. It's Princess Alice, scaled up to a civilisation.

3:59Can it be tested?

It's a neat story. Can it be tested? In a study published in 2016, researchers played a simple game with five hundred and ninety-one people in eight very different communities, from Fiji and Vanuatu to Siberia, Brazil and Tanzania. Each player shared out coins between two cups, by rolling a die. One cup for themselves, one for a distant stranger of the same religion. Nobody checked the rolls, so cheating was easy. The more people believed their god knew what humans do and punished wrongdoing, the more coins they gave to the distant stranger.

And it was punishment that mattered. Belief in a god who rewards didn't explain the result. Notice who the stranger was, though. Someone of the same religion. Big gods may widen the circle of trust. They don't necessarily include everyone.

4:57Which came first?

So did big gods make big societies possible? Or did growing societies come up with big gods because they needed them? In 2019, a huge study tried to settle it, using a database of hundreds of societies across ten thousand years of history. Its conclusion, published in Nature, went against the big gods idea. Moralising gods turned up only after societies reached around a million people. The big societies came first.

5:29Two years later

Two years later, the paper was retracted.

Another team had looked at the data. For most of the early societies, the database said "unknown" about moralising gods. And in the analysis, "unknown" had been counted as "no". In the critics' reanalysis, the result flipped. Moralising gods could have come first. The original team says many of those unknowns were really mislabelled absences. They redid the analysis, and they still find that big societies came first, and that big gods didn't drive their growth. The argument isn't over.

6:10The honest state of things

So here's the honest state of things. Belief in a watching god can change how people treat strangers. Whether it helped build the first great societies, or arrived once they were already there, we don't know yet.

6:26One more twist

And there's one more twist. Some of the most trusting societies today are also among the least religious. Denmark. Sweden. Norway. Norenzayan's explanation is that courts, contracts and police took over much of the watching. In his words, those societies climbed religion's ladder… and then kicked it away.

6:49So why gods?

None of this tells us whether any god exists. Why we believe something and whether it's true are separate questions.

So why does almost every culture have gods? Part of the answer may be a mind that easily imagines invisible minds, even ones without a body. But that doesn't explain why some of them became moral judges… or whether they helped build large societies. Those are different questions. And we still don't have a complete answer.

And an invisible watcher can still work… even on a child alone in a room, next to an empty chair, as long as the child believes.

So here's a question for you. If you knew for certain that nobody would ever find out, would you behave any differently? Let me know in the comments.
