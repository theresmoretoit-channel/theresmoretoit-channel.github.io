How Society Actually Runs

Why Do People Keep Voting for Populists?

11 min2026-09-24

They disagree with each other on almost everything. The machinery underneath them is nearly identical.

This video is about that machinery. Not about who is right.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00Simple answers to complicated problems

Some politicians win elections by promising absurdly simple answers to absurdly complicated problems. And it works. Not on a few people. On enough people to win. You might expect that to happen when the stakes are low. When a country can afford a gamble. That is not when it happens. It happens when enough people stop believing that anyone already in charge can fix anything.

0:29Trump. The AfD. Fico.

Trump. The AfD. Fico. Three names, three countries, and almost nothing else in common. In November 2024, Donald Trump won the American popular vote with 49.8 percent, and 312 electoral votes. In February 2025, the AfD came second in Germany with 20.8 percent. In 2021 it had 10.4. It doubled. And in Slovakia, Robert Fico became prime minister for the fourth time. Now here is the part that makes the easy explanation fall apart. Fico's party is called Direction — Social Democracy. It sat inside the family of European socialist parties.

Not on the right. On the left.

1:21Not on the right. On the left.

The AfD is radical right. Trump is Republican. Fico ran as a social democrat. Their policies disagree. Their voters disagree. On most questions they would disagree with each other. And the machinery underneath them is nearly identical.

1:51What populism means in the research

So what is the machinery? Political scientists have a name for the thing all three are doing, and it is narrower than the way we use the word. Populism, in the research sense, is not a policy programme. It is one claim about who you are. Society splits into two groups. The ordinary people. And an elite that has taken the system and now runs it for itself. That is it. That is the whole idea. Which is why it can sit inside almost any politics. A left-wing populist and a right-wing populist will name a different elite. But they build the same sentence.

And the sentence goes like this. You have a problem. The people in charge caused it. I am the one who will end it. One problem. One villain. One solution.

Now, you would think this only works on invented problems.

2:49Why a real problem works better

It is the opposite. The story is at its strongest when the problem is completely real. Housing that nobody young can afford. Healthcare that costs more every year. Wages that stopped keeping up. Infrastructure that is quietly rotting. A border that is not under control. An economy that no longer feels the way it did. None of that has to be exaggerated. Much of it can be measured. The populist doesn't need to invent it. The populist only needs to explain it. And the explanation is always one word long. Immigrants… Brussels… Washington… Corporations… Billionaires… The establishment.

The word changes by country. The shape never does.

3:45What actually moved the votes

This is where it gets uncomfortable, because there is a measurement for it. In 2017, four economists took 220 European regions and followed them through the financial crisis. They wanted to know what actually moved the votes. Where unemployment rose, votes for anti-establishment and populist parties rose with it. That part is not surprising. This is. The same regions also lost trust in their national parliaments and in European institutions. But attitudes towards immigrants hardly moved at all. On culture, they did not harden. If anything, Europeans came out of the crisis slightly more open.

Read that again. Across those regions, the thing that tracked the populist vote was not how people felt about immigrants. It was how they felt about their own institutions. The argument everybody was having and the thing actually moving the votes were two different objects. That is one study, one continent, one crisis. It is not the last word. But it is a measurement, and it points somewhere specific.

That brings us to the part that established parties do not enjoy discussing.

5:03You had your chance

A populist needs votes. Those votes are currently sitting somewhere else. So somebody has to give them a reason to leave the party they already know. And that reason can be four words. You had your chance.

Look at what happened to American trust in government. In 1958, 73 percent of Americans said they trusted the government in Washington to do what is right.

5:30Seventy-three percent, and seventeen

By September 2025, it was 17. Two percent said just about always. Fifteen said most of the time. That is not a bad year. That is nearly seventy years of something draining away. And once it drains, something strange becomes possible. A candidate who would have been dismissed as too extreme, too strange, too obviously unqualified, becomes worth a try. Not because people love the candidate. Because of a different sentence.

6:10What do we have to lose?

What do we have to lose?

That is one of the most powerful sentences in politics, and it is not an argument for anyone. It is an argument against everyone else. Which is the whole trick, and almost nobody says it out loud.

6:30The whole trick

A populist does not have to convince you that the simple solution will work. That would be hard. Solutions can be checked. The populist only has to convince you that the current one has already failed. And that job is much easier, because quite often it is simply true.

There is a second advantage, and it is structural.

6:51Why a slogan wins

Imagine two politicians explaining why housing is expensive. The first one says: zoning rules, construction rates, interest rates, demographic change, local opposition, decades of shortfall. The second one says: they made housing unaffordable, and I will fix it. Which sentence is still in your head? A complicated explanation needs context. A slogan needs nothing at all. Thirty seconds of video will carry problem, villain and solution with room to spare. It will not carry a supply curve. And here is the trap inside that advantage.

7:32No single villain, no single fix

A country can have an immigration problem, and a housing problem, and an ageing population, and weak growth, all at once. Those things push on each other. Fixing one can worsen another. There may be no single villain. There may be no single fix. Reality doesn't fit neatly into a campaign slogan. But a slogan fits neatly into a person's memory, and that is the competition we actually hold.

So why does this keep coming back?

8:06Why it keeps coming back

Because the conditions do. Problems appear. Parties promise. Repairs take longer than terms. Some work. Some don't. New problems arrive. And eventually enough people are tired enough to gamble. And by then, politics has quietly changed subject. It stops being about policy, which can be argued with. It becomes about trust, which cannot.

8:34The occupant changed

And trust has a number too. In May 2024, 35 percent of Democrats trusted the federal government. Eleven percent of Republicans did. By September 2025, it was 9 percent of Democrats and 26 percent of Republicans. Sixteen months, and the two lines crossed straight over each other. The occupant changed. So that number was never measuring performance. It was measuring identity. And that is why facts, on their own, do not repair any of this. If the other side is not merely wrong but corrupt, then meeting them halfway is not compromise.

It is surrender.

So is populism the disease?

9:20Symptom, not disease

Maybe not. A political system that people trust can absorb enormous disagreement without breaking. When that trust goes, every failure becomes evidence. Every compromise looks like weakness. Every insider becomes a suspect, and every outsider becomes a candidate. Which leaves one uncomfortable thought.

9:44What happened before they did

Populists usually do not manufacture the frustration that carries them in. They arrive after it.

Sometimes the system produced it by itself, over years, in public, while everyone watched. So telling people not to vote for a particular person was never going to be enough. If the reason is still there when he goes, somebody else simply takes the slot. And perhaps the honest question for any democracy is not why people voted for the populist. It is what happened before they did.
