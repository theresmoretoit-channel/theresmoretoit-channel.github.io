Where Does the Fuel Go When a Plane Dumps It?

9 min2026-08-25

The answer involves a hundred-tonne gap between what an aircraft may take off at and what it may land at, a Cold War bomber that really did set its own dumped fuel on fire, and a twist most articles never mention: most airliners cannot dump fuel at all.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00The headline

Every so often, the same headline appears. A plane took off, something went wrong, and it had to come straight back. But first, it dumped twenty tonnes of fuel. Twenty thousand kilograms of kerosene, released deliberately into the sky. Which leaves two questions. Where does it actually go? And if the problem is too much fuel, why not simply burn it?

0:26Why it can't just land

Picture it. A large airliner has just lifted off. A few minutes into the climb, something goes wrong. An engine problem. A gear that will not retract. The crew turns back. Which sounds simple. Turn around, land. Except the aircraft took off eight minutes ago, so the tanks are still almost full. And here the numbers stop being comfortable. A seven four seven may take off at three hundred and ninety-seven tonnes. It may land at two hundred and eighty-six. That gap is over a hundred tonnes. A hundred tonnes of aircraft that is legal in the air, and not legal on the runway.

On a normal flight this never comes up. The aircraft burns fuel for ten hours and gets lighter on its own. But turn back fifteen minutes after departure, and none of that has happened. It is still carrying nearly everything it left with.

1:24Why not build it stronger

Which invites an obvious objection. Why not build the thing stronger? Heavier undercarriage, stiffer wings. Then it could land at any weight. Engineers could. But a stronger structure means more material. More material means more mass. More mass means more fuel to carry it. And every kilogram of it flies on every flight the aircraft ever makes, for decades, to be useful on the rare day it turns back. Which does not mean it cannot land heavy. An overweight landing is a certified procedure. If the aircraft needs to be down now, it lands.

2:04Where the fuel actually goes

But if there is time, it is better to arrive at a sensible weight. And the heaviest thing an aircraft can throw away is fuel. So where do twenty thousand kilograms of kerosene go? Not, as it turns out, into anybody's garden. Look at where the fuel leaves. Not a hatch in the belly. Not a pipe near the engines. The nozzles sit out near the wingtips. As far from everything hot as the airframe allows. Remember that. It becomes important later. And they are not simply holes. They atomise, spraying fuel into a slipstream moving at hundreds of kilometres an hour. What leaves is a mist.

Then there is altitude. Fuel is normally jettisoned no lower than about eighteen hundred metres. And that number is not arbitrary. Modelling by the American toxic substances agency found that above roughly fifteen hundred metres, more than ninety-eight percent evaporates before reaching the ground. Ground sampling under real dumps found no liquid fuel. The kerosene does not vanish. It stays in the atmosphere. But it does not fall as rain.

3:20Why not just burn it

Which handles the first question. Now the second. The aircraft has four engines whose entire purpose is destroying fuel. So why not just burn it? Because engines burn fuel at the rate engines burn fuel. A seven four seven in cruise gets through ten to eleven tonnes an hour. So to burn off a hundred tonnes you would fly for about ten hours. And you cannot instruct four jet engines to eat five times faster.

Which is where I had what felt like a very good idea. We already spray fuel out of the wings. So set fire to it.

3:51The flying flamethrower

Fuel streaming from the wingtips, and behind the aircraft, an enormous flame. Or, to use the technical term, a flying flamethrower. So why does nobody do this? Here is the part I did not expect. Somebody did.

4:14Somebody actually did it

The F one eleven was a swing-wing bomber, and its fuel dump outlet happened to sit between the two engines. Which meant that if you dumped fuel and lit the afterburners at once, the fuel ignited. A flame dozens of metres long. The Australian air force turned it into a signature airshow display. It had no operational purpose. It was never a weight reduction system. It was an accident of where somebody put a pipe. And nobody else ever took it up. That flame existed because a fuel outlet sat next to an afterburner. On an airliner the nozzles are at the wingtips, deliberately, so that never happens.

The choice that makes dumping safe is the same one that makes burning impossible. And nobody wants to hear: we have activated the emergency flamethrower.

So the fuel stays unlit. But we still need the weight gone. What else is on board? Luggage. Two hundred passengers, twenty kilograms each.

5:09What else could go overboard

Four tonnes. Against a hundred tonne problem, that is a rounding error. We would open the cargo doors in flight to solve four percent of it.

5:30The parachute solution

So, not the suitcases. Which leaves one remaining source of mass. The passengers. Obviously, we are not going to throw people out of an aircraft. This is a civilised society. We have parachutes. The computer decides the aircraft is twelve tonnes too heavy, and assumes eighty kilograms per passenger. A message appears on the captain's display. Recommendation: remove one hundred and fifty passengers. Selected at random, of course. For fairness. Congratulations. You have been enrolled in the Emergency Weight Reduction Programme. Good afternoon. I understand most of you were expecting Frankfurt.

Instead, a parachute course. It lasts four minutes. This is a parachute. This is the cord. If something goes wrong, use the other parachute. And then the cabin door opens. Good luck.

6:30Most airliners can't dump at all

So the fuel goes overboard instead. Except. And this is the part almost nobody mentions. Most airliners cannot dump fuel at all. The seven three seven cannot. The A three twenty cannot. Neither can the seven five seven, the A two twenty, or essentially every regional jet. They have no jettison system whatsoever. And that is not an oversight. It is written into the certification rules. An aircraft needs a jettison system only if it cannot meet its climb requirements after a fifteen minute flight at maximum weight. Narrowbodies can. They are light enough, and their engines have enough margin, to climb away safely even when full. So nobody fits one.

Which means that for the aircraft most of us actually fly on, the answer to where the dumped fuel goes is that there is no dumped fuel. A seven three seven that turns back early circles until it has burned enough off. Or it lands overweight, and engineers inspect it afterwards.

7:38So why it happens

Fuel dumping is not the normal solution. It is the option available to the largest aircraft, where the weight gap is simply too big. So the next time that headline appears, you will know. The aircraft was too heavy to land, and big enough to have a way out. And kerosene, unlike passengers, does not need a parachute. Does not need a four minute course. And does not, on landing, request a refund.
