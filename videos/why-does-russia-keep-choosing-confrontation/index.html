Why Does Russia Keep Choosing Confrontation?

11 min2026-10-19

Russia is the largest country on Earth, rich in oil, gas, forests, farmland and minerals. Yet its economy is roughly the size of Italy's. So why does a country with so much keep choosing confrontation with neighbours it could trade with, even when Russia itself pays the price? The easy answer is one man. But the pattern is older than any one president. This video follows it from five centuries of expansion and an open plain with no natural defences, through the collapse of the Soviet Union and the chaotic 1990s, to the way decisions are made in Moscow today, and asks who the pattern really serves.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Finland joins NATO

In February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. One of the reasons Moscow gave was NATO creeping towards its borders. Just over a year later, Finland joined NATO. Finland's border with Russia is more than thirteen hundred kilometres long. Overnight, the land border between Russia and NATO roughly doubled. Then Sweden joined too. A country that had not fought a war since 1814. A war partly justified by NATO's expansion left Russia with far more NATO on its border than before.

0:38So much land, so small an economy

And that is not the only strange thing. Russia is the largest country on Earth. It covers more than a tenth of all the land on the planet. It has oil, gas, forests, farmland, and some of the largest mineral reserves in the world. Yet measured by nominal GDP, its economy is roughly the size of Italy's. And Russia has more than twice as many people. So why does a country with so much keep choosing confrontation with neighbours it could trade with? Even when the price is paid by Russia itself?

1:13Is it just one man?

The easy answer is one man. Vladimir Putin. But Poland and the Baltic states were asking to join NATO long before he came to power. The pattern is older than any one president. And to see who it really serves, we have to start much earlier.

1:39Five centuries of growth

For five centuries, Moscow grew outwards. A small principality became an empire that reached the Pacific. Much of that growth came by conquest. And to hold it all together, power was gathered at the centre, in the hands of the tsar. Geography mattered. Much of western Russia is a wide, open plain. No mountains, no sea between Moscow and an invading army. And armies came. Polish troops occupied Moscow in 1610. Napoleon reached it in 1812. Hitler's armies came close in 1941. The Second World War cost the Soviet Union around twenty-seven million lives.

That fear is real. And it deserves to be taken seriously. But fear does not decide what a country does with it. You can defend your borders without claiming the right to run your neighbours.

2:37The security dilemma

Here is where the trouble starts. If you only feel safe when your neighbours are weak, you will try to keep them weak. More influence. More control. A buffer of countries that cannot choose against you. But your neighbours have fears too. And now you are the thing they fear. So they look for protection. Alliances. Armies. Partners far away. And you see their defence as proof that the world is closing in on you. Each side's search for safety makes the other side less safe. Political scientists call it the security dilemma.

3:14Finland changes its mind

Finland shows how fast it can turn. For decades, only about one Finn in four wanted to join NATO. Within three months of the invasion, three in four did. Nobody had to talk Finland into it. Moscow did that by itself.

3:33The Soviet Union

In the twentieth century, the empire became the Soviet Union. A nuclear superpower, with world-class science, and the first human in orbit. But it was run the same way. Power at the centre, and no room for opposition. A system that could mobilise almost anything… except the truth about its own mistakes.

3:55Collapse and the 1990s

In 1991, it fell apart. Russia was left with about three quarters of the territory, and only half of the people. Fourteen other republics became independent countries. Most had no wish to be ruled from Moscow again. The decade that followed was brutal. Between 1991 and 1998, Russia's economy shrank by around forty per cent. Hyperinflation wiped out the savings of millions. Life expectancy for Russian men fell below sixty. Meanwhile, a handful of insiders picked up some of the biggest companies for a fraction of their value.

For millions of people, freedom arrived together with chaos, crime, and humiliation. And when freedom feels like chaos, a promise of order becomes very attractive. Even if it costs some freedom.

4:51Order and a strong state

On the last day of 1999, Vladimir Putin became acting president. He promised exactly that. Order, and a strong state. And for a while, it seemed to work. Oil rose from under twenty dollars a barrel to over a hundred. Wages and pensions grew year after year. The state took back control of the biggest oil and gas companies, and of television. And many Russians accepted less political competition in exchange for stability. But there is a trap in that bargain. Institutions too weak to challenge a popular leader today are too weak to stop a destructive one tomorrow.

5:33A lost sphere of influence

There was also a wound to heal. In 2005, Putin called the collapse of the Soviet Union the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the twentieth century. In that view, the neighbours are not simply independent countries. They are a lost sphere of influence. And their choices become questions of Russian prestige. Georgia, 2008. Crimea, 2014. In 2021, Putin published an essay arguing that Russians and Ukrainians are one people. Months later, the tanks crossed the border. But Ukraine is not a lost province. It is a country, with its own history and its own choices. Living next door does not give anyone the right to rule.

6:23How decisions get made

So far, this sounds like a story about ideas. But there is a second mechanism, and it is about how decisions get made. Three days before the invasion, Russian television broadcast a meeting of the Security Council. One by one, the top officials stood up and backed the decision to recognise two breakaway regions in eastern Ukraine. When the foreign intelligence chief stumbled over his words, Putin cut him off. Speak plainly, he told him. It was a glimpse of a system where the safest thing to tell the leader is what he already believes.

When bad news is dangerous, bad news stops travelling upwards. Loyalty becomes worth more than judgement. Russia still has brilliant experts. But expertise is not much use when an inconvenient conclusion can cost you your career. A war that Moscow reportedly expected to take Kyiv within days is now in its fifth year.

7:25The third mechanism

And there is a third mechanism. The one that explains why confrontation keeps coming back. A government in conflict with the outside world can treat its critics as traitors. Since 2012, Russian law has branded more than a thousand journalists, academics, activists, and organisations as foreign agents. Economic trouble can be blamed on hostile foreign powers. And loyalty to the government can be sold as loyalty to the nation. That does not mean every conflict is staged as a distraction. The point is simpler. Once a system depends on an enemy, peace becomes politically awkward.

8:06Who does the pattern serve?

Which brings us back to the question of who this pattern serves. Not what is good for Russia. What is good for the people who run it. A country benefits from trade, investment, and neighbours who are not afraid of it. A ruling circle with few limits on its power needs something else. Control. Loyalty. And no one able to replace it. Oil and gas make that easier. In some years, they brought in about half of all federal revenue. A state that lives off exports needs its taxpayers less. What it needs is control of the pipes.

8:43The bill

And the bill for confrontation is paid by the country, not by the people who chose it. Before the invasion, Russia supplied about forty-five per cent of the gas the European Union imported. By 2025, it was about thirteen. Hundreds of thousands of Russians left the country, many of them young and skilled. And Russia now leans heavily on a single large partner. China. Russia sells it mostly oil and gas. China sells back cars, machines, and electronics. China buys a lot of Russian oil. But it has other suppliers. Russia, cut off from Europe, has far fewer buyers.

A war meant to prove Russia's independence has left it more dependent than before.

9:34Is it simply fate?

So is this simply Russia's fate? The old empire, the open plain, a national character that cannot change? Not really. Geography explains the fear. History explains the habits. Neither one makes the choices.

The pattern keeps returning because it serves a particular system. One that needs an enemy abroad to justify its power at home.

10:00What would it take to change it?

And that raises a harder question. If the pattern comes from the system, what would it take to change it? And how long would that take? And that is where we go next. Because replacing one leader may be the easy part. Changing the system around him is another story.
