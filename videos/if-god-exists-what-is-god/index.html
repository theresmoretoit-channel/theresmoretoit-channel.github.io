If God Exists, What Is God?

13 min2026-10-05

This video grants the first step and then asks what follows from it. Where would a creator of space be? What does "before" mean if time began with the universe? Could something be non-physical, conscious without a brain, or think without one thought following another? Is there only one? What does "all-powerful" mean, and what happens to free will if the future is already known? Is a creator necessarily good, and why would a being that needs nothing create anything at all?

Along the way it looks at the difference between "something caused the universe" and the much richer picture most people mean by God, and at where all that extra information comes from.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Suppose we prove God exists

Imagine that tomorrow, we somehow prove that God exists. Not just find an unexplained event. Not discover something strange in the universe. Not find an ancient text. Imagine we actually establish it. God exists. At first, that sounds like the end of the question. But it isn't. Because we have immediately created another question. What exactly have we proved exists?

Suppose we discover that the universe has a cause. That would be extraordinary. But a cause of the universe is not necessarily the God most people imagine. It could be something very different. So let's grant that much. Let's say there really is something behind the existence of our universe. Something we might reasonably call God. What can we actually say about it?

0:58Where is God?

The first strange question is: Where is God? If God created the universe, it would be tempting to imagine God sitting somewhere outside it. But what does "outside the universe" even mean? The universe isn't necessarily a giant object floating inside a larger empty space. Space itself is part of the universe. So if God created space, God wouldn't simply be located somewhere beyond the edge of space. There might not be an edge. And asking where God is might not even make sense. God wouldn't be somewhere over there. God would have to exist in some sense that doesn't depend on the space we inhabit.

1:41When did God exist?

And then we encounter an even stranger question. When did God exist? If time itself began with the universe, asking what happened before the universe may not make sense. It's a little like asking: “What is north of the North Pole?” The question sounds grammatical. But the coordinate system itself may have reached its limit. So if God created time, perhaps God isn't simply an extremely old being. Perhaps the idea of being old doesn't apply at all. God wouldn't have existed for a very long time before creating the universe. There may have been no before.

2:29What is God made of?

Now ask something even more basic. What is God made of? Everything we normally encounter is made of something. Atoms. Particles. Fields. Radiation. Even seemingly empty space has physical properties. But if God is the creator of the physical universe, saying that God is made of ordinary matter would create a problem. That matter would already have to exist. So classical concepts of God often describe God as non-physical. But now we have another problem. We have spent our entire lives experiencing physical things. We don't actually have an intuitive category for a non-physical object.

We can say the words. But what would such a thing actually be?

3:19Something, or someone?

And this is where the idea becomes particularly interesting. Suppose a cosmological argument successfully establishes a first cause. What have we learned? Perhaps: Something caused the universe to exist. But how do we get from that to: Someone caused the universe? A cause doesn't necessarily have to be a person. A rock can cause a window to break. A star can cause a planet to orbit. A physical process can cause another physical process. So why would the ultimate cause have a mind? One possibility is that a personal cause is required for some philosophical reason. But that requires an additional argument.

The word God doesn't automatically provide the missing steps.

4:09Consciousness without a brain

The major monotheistic religions describe God as conscious. God knows. God understands. God chooses. God can respond. But consciousness, as far as we know, is associated with physical nervous systems. So what would consciousness without a brain mean? Maybe consciousness doesn't fundamentally require a brain. Maybe our brains are simply one way consciousness can exist. But at this point, we are trying to extend concepts developed from human experience into a completely different kind of existence.

4:44Thinking outside time

And if God has thoughts, do those thoughts happen one after another? Because if they do, that sounds like time. One thought happens. Then another. Then another. But if God exists outside time, perhaps divine thought doesn't work like ours. Maybe there is no sequence of thoughts. No waiting. No changing from one thought to another. Instead, God might somehow know things all at once. That sounds strange. But we shouldn't reject an idea merely because it's difficult to imagine. Our inability to picture something isn't proof that it is impossible. It may simply mean that our brains evolved to understand the world at human scale.

5:28One creator, or many?

Now suppose we have established some kind of transcendent creator. Does that tell us there is only one? Not necessarily. Our universe could, at least conceptually, have multiple creators. If there are two or more of them, perhaps they cooperate. Perhaps they disagree. Perhaps one created part of reality and another created something else. And if we want to call that being the God, rather than merely a god, we're already adding information.

6:00The rock too heavy to lift

What about the familiar idea that God is all-powerful? This raises an old philosophical puzzle. Can an omnipotent God create a rock so heavy that God cannot lift it? If God can create it, then there is something God cannot do: lift it. If God cannot create it, then there is something God cannot do: create it. The puzzle sounds devastating. But it depends on what we mean by “can do anything.” Perhaps omnipotence means the ability to do anything that is logically possible. Under that definition, creating a square circle isn't a task God fails to accomplish. It's not a coherent task in the first place.

The same could apply to the rock. The paradox may reveal something about the meaning of omnipotence rather than a contradiction in the existence of God.

6:54Knowing the future

Now consider knowledge. If God knows everything, then God knows the future. But what does that mean for human freedom? Suppose God knows with absolute certainty what you will do tomorrow. Could you still choose differently? If you did choose differently, God's knowledge would have been wrong. But if you cannot choose differently, in what sense is the choice free? Philosophers have argued about this for centuries. Some say divine foreknowledge is compatible with free will. Others think there is a genuine conflict. And some reject the idea that the future contains one fixed set of facts at all.

7:36Is God good?

And then we reach perhaps the most important property of all. Is God good? If we discover that some creator exists, that alone wouldn't tell us that this creator is morally perfect. A powerful creator could theoretically be indifferent. Or morally strange. Or hostile. Or simply unlike us. If we want to conclude that God is perfectly good, we need another reason. As we saw in an earlier video, this raises the problem of suffering.

8:09Does God need anything?

And now we reach a question that I find particularly strange. If God is perfect… does God actually need anything? Suppose God is complete. God lacks nothing. God doesn't need food. Doesn't need shelter. Doesn't need money. Doesn't need protection. Doesn't even need company. Then why would God want anything? Why create a universe? Why create conscious beings? Was God lonely? That seems strange for a being that needs nothing. Was God bored? Even stranger. Why would God care whether humans worship? Why answer prayers? Why give commandments? Why judge anyone?

Maybe creation wasn't something God needed to do. Maybe it was something God chose to do. But then we have another question. Why?

9:04Two very different claims

And this may be the central point. There is a huge difference between these two statements: Something caused the universe. And: There is an all-powerful, all-knowing, perfectly good, personal God who created the universe, cares about humans, answers prayers, gives moral commands, and wants to be known. The second statement contains an enormous amount of additional information. Where did all of it come from? Not necessarily from the original argument for a first cause. Each additional property requires its own justification.

Maybe those arguments exist. Maybe some are strong. Maybe some are weak. But they are separate questions.

9:50Smuggling in extra information

There's a broader lesson here. Whenever we explain something, we have to be careful not to smuggle extra information into the explanation. Imagine finding an ancient machine. You determine that someone must have built it. That tells you something. But it doesn't tell you who built it. What they looked like. Why they built it. Whether they were kind. Or whether they even still exist. The evidence for one property doesn't automatically establish all the others. The same reasoning applies to God.

10:25So what is God?

So what is God? A person? A mind? A force? The first cause of the universe? Something outside space and time? Perhaps the word “God” is doing too much work. It may refer to very different ideas in different philosophical and religious traditions. So asking: “Does God exist?” can sound like we're asking about one clearly defined object. But perhaps we're actually asking about an entire family of very different concepts.

10:56Would we even recognise it?

And there is one final possibility worth considering. Maybe if we discovered the ultimate foundation of reality, we wouldn't immediately recognise it as God. It might not be a person. Its thinking might not resemble human thought at all. It might not even be the kind of thing for which the words “what is it?” make sense. We would have discovered something fundamental about reality. But we might still be arguing about whether it deserves to be called God.

11:30The deeper question

So perhaps the question isn't simply: Does God exist? The deeper question is: What would we actually mean by “God exists”? If we mean a first cause, that's one claim. If we mean an intelligent creator, that's another. If we mean a perfectly good being who cares about humans and answers prayers… that's another claim again. So if someone ever proves that God exists, don't stop there. Ask the next question. What exactly did they prove exists? Because proving that there is something behind the universe… may be only the beginning of the mystery.
