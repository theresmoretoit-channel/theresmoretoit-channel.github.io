Why Is the B-52 Still Flying, 70 Years Later?

10 min2026-11-03

This video follows the B-52 from a weekend in a Dayton hotel in 1948, where Boeing engineers redesigned it and carved a balsa model, through three completely different jobs: flying high into the Soviet Union, flying low under the radar, and carrying cruise missiles. Along the way: the XB-70 Valkyrie, a test flight that lost most of its tail fin, why a wing wears out by flying rather than by age, a bomber brought back from the Arizona desert, and why the new B-52J keeps eight engines instead of four.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Built in 1962, set to fly into the 2050s

The last B-52 left the factory in 1962. In some American families, three generations have flown in it. From grandfather to grandson. And the US Air Force plans to keep flying it into the 2050s. That's close to a hundred years after its first flight. Along the way, it has outlived the bomber that was meant to replace it, a giant that flew at three times the speed of sound. And it's expected to outlive the B-2 stealth bomber, too. So why does the most powerful air force in the world keep flying its oldest bomber?

0:38The first part of the answer

Here's the first part of the answer. Every one of its would-be replacements was built to be the best at something. Faster. Higher. Harder to see on radar. The B-52 was built to carry a lot of weight a very long way. And being the best at something turns out to have an expiry date. Carrying a lot, a long way, doesn't.

But that's only half the answer. A job that never goes away is no use if the plane wears out. And the B-52 has had three completely different jobs. To see how it survived all three, we need to go back to a hotel room in Ohio.

1:22A weekend in a Dayton hotel

October 1948. Dayton, Ohio. A team of Boeing engineers had come to show the Air Force their design for a new bomber. It had propellers. And the Air Force wanted to talk about jets. So over one weekend, in the Hotel Van Cleve, the engineers redesigned it. Swept wings. Eight jet engines. One of them went to a hobby shop, bought some balsa wood, and carved a model. On Monday, they handed over a thirty-three-page proposal and a model you could hold in one hand. That model became the B-52. It first flew in 1952. By 1962, Boeing had built seven hundred and forty-four of them.

2:10Job one: fly high

Its first job was simple, and terrible. Fly very high, very far, into the Soviet Union, and drop nuclear bombs. Altitude was its protection. Then, in 1960, a Soviet missile shot down an American U-2 spy plane, flying far higher than the B-52 could. Flying high had stopped being safe.

And that giant, three times the speed of sound, was already on the drawing board. The Valkyrie. It was designed to cross into Soviet airspace more than twenty kilometres up, faster than a rifle bullet. The perfect answer to a question that had just stopped mattering. Only two were ever built.

2:57Job two: fly low

The B-52 did something else. It went low. Crews trained to fly it a few hundred feet above the ground, under the enemy's radar. Job number two. It wasn't designed for that. The air near the ground is rough, and a big wing gets shaken for hours. On one test flight in 1964, over the Rocky Mountains, turbulence tore most of the tail fin off a B-52. The crew still landed it.

And by the 1970s, even flying low was getting dangerous.

3:33Job three: carry the missiles

So in 1977, the United States cancelled the first version of the B-1, the plane meant to replace the B-52. One big reason was a new weapon. The cruise missile. A cruise missile flies itself, low and alone, for more than two thousand kilometres. So the bomber no longer had to get anywhere near its target. It just had to carry the missiles to the edge of the map, and let go. Job number three. And for this job, the B-52 was almost perfect. It can carry up to twenty of those missiles. It didn't need to be fast. It didn't need to be invisible.

It needed to be big, and to fly a long way. Which is exactly what those engineers in Dayton had drawn in 1948. Three completely different jobs. And all three needed the same thing from the plane.

But even the perfect truck wears out eventually.

4:35How is it still flying?

So how is a plane built in the early 1960s still flying at all? Because a wing wears out mostly by flying, not by the calendar. Every take-off, every gust, every landing flexes it. And the metal keeps count. A busy airliner can fly three or four thousand hours in a single year. A B-52 flies a few hundred. For most of the Cold War, part of the fleet wasn't flying at all. It sat on alert, at the end of the runway, fuelled and armed, with crews sleeping nearby, ready to take off within minutes. Waiting barely wears out a wing. Flying does.

By 2016, the average B-52 had flown about eighteen thousand hours. Its wings were estimated to last at least thirty-two thousand. So by the measure that matters, the average B-52 had used up only about half its wing.

5:35Back from the desert

And when a fire wrecked one in 2014, the Air Force didn't order a new one. Instead, it went to the Arizona desert, picked a B-52 that had been sitting in the sun for seven years, rebuilt it, and sent it back to work. Its nickname is Ghost Rider. A few years later, a second one followed.

5:55Many of them, and cheap to fly

It helps that so many were built. Seven hundred and forty-four. Of the B-2, there were only ever twenty-one.

And the B-52 is the cheapest bomber for the Air Force to fly. An hour in a B-52 costs less than an hour in a B-1 or a B-2. When the job is carrying missiles to the edge of the map, what matters isn't being hard to see. It's how much you carry, and how far.

6:26What's inside it today

But here's the strange part. A lot of what's inside a B-52 today didn't exist in 1962. Radios, navigation, computers, cockpit screens, weapons. All replaced, some of them more than once. A new radar is being fitted, based on the radar of a modern fighter jet.

6:47Why still eight engines?

And next come the engines. The ones it flies on now were designed in the 1950s. They're being replaced by Rolls-Royce engines from the same family that powers Gulfstream business jets. Eight old engines out. Eight new ones in. Why not four bigger ones, like a modern airliner? Because that would turn an engine swap into a redesign. Losing one big engine on take-off would pull the plane hard to one side, so it would need a new rudder. It would also need new cockpit controls, changes to the wing, and years of new flight tests.

Eight smaller engines avoid most of that.

The upgraded plane gets a new letter. The B-52J. Its test flights are planned for the end of the 2020s.

7:42The newest bomber and the oldest

Meanwhile, the Air Force is buying a brand-new stealth bomber, the B-21 Raider. And the long-term plan is a bomber fleet of just two types. Not the B-1. Not the B-2. The newest bomber, and the oldest. One gets in, unseen. The other carries a lot, from far away.

8:05So why is it still flying?

So why is the B-52 still flying? Not because it was the best bomber ever built. It was never the fastest, the highest, or the hardest to see. Its replacements were built around a technology. The B-52 was built around a job. The technology kept expiring. The job never did.

And everything that did go out of date, the engines, the electronics, the weapons, turned out to be the parts you could swap. What's left of the 1948 design is the part that's hardest to replace. A big, strong wing that carries a lot, a long way.

8:50A question for you

So here's a question for you. What do you think will finally ground the B-52? A better plane, or a job it can't do? Let me know in the comments.
