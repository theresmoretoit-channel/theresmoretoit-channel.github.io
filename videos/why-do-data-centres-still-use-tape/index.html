Why Do Data Centres Still Use Magnetic Tape?

12 min2026-11-14

This video goes through LTO, the open tape format behind today's data archives, how forty terabytes fit into a cartridge you can hold in one hand, fifteen thousand tracks narrower than a thousandth of a millimetre, the servo patterns that keep the head on track, how the drive checks every block as it writes, why a cartridge on a shelf is safe from bugs and ransomware, what tape costs compared with hard drives, why finding a file can take a minute, the robot libraries at CERN and Lawrence Livermore, why AI is making tape grow again, and the strange problem of tapes that outlive their drives.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A cassette and a strange claim

You press play. A cassette starts to squeal, and five minutes later, a game appears on the television. In an earlier video, we took that sound apart, bit by bit. And it ended with a strange claim: that the world's data centres still keep their archives on tape. That sounds like nostalgia. It isn't.

0:22The day Gmail came back from tape

In February 2011, around forty thousand Gmail users opened their inbox and found it empty. A software update at Google had a bug in it. Google keeps several copies of every mailbox, in different data centres. The bug reached those too. Every message was recovered. It took hours instead of milliseconds, because it came back from tape. And Google isn't unusual. CERN, the particle physics lab, has stored an exabyte of data from the Large Hadron Collider. That's a million terabytes. Most of it sits on about sixty thousand tapes.

1:02Why trust tape?

So here's the question. We have hard drives, SSDs and the cloud. Why would the people with the most data still trust a strip of magnetic tape?

1:14Older than the hard drive

Tape is actually older than the hard drive. In 1951, the UNIVAC I computer stored its data on reels of metal tape. The first hard drive came five years later. The tape they use today isn't a music cassette. It's called LTO, short for Linear Tape-Open. HP, IBM and Seagate developed it in the late 1990s, as an open format that anyone could build drives for. The first cartridge, in 2000, held one hundred gigabytes.

1:48Forty terabytes in one hand

The newest generation, LTO-10, holds up to forty terabytes. Four hundred times as much, in a box of the same size. The box often says one hundred. That number assumes your data can be squeezed two and a half times. Photos and video are already compressed, so forty is the honest figure. You can hold it in one hand, and it holds more than eight hundred million games for a forty-eight kilobyte Spectrum. The drive writes four hundred megabytes every second. Even so, filling one cartridge takes more than a day. A ZX Spectrum loaded about fourteen hundred bits a second. This drive writes more than three billion. Over two million times faster.

2:36How does it all fit?

How does all of that fit on tape? First, length. Inside is a single reel with more than a kilometre of tape. It's half an inch wide, and many times thinner than a human hair. Then, tracks. The drive doesn't write one stripe along the tape. It writes more than fifteen thousand. Each one is less than a thousandth of a millimetre wide. The head writes thirty-two at once, runs to the end of the tape, shifts over by a hair's breadth, and comes back the other way. Hundreds of passes, back and forth, over the same kilometre.

3:13Tape that wanders

Now, the problem. Tape is flexible. It stretches, and it wanders sideways as it moves. At these widths, drifting by a single track means hitting the wrong data. So the tape carries its own guide rails. At the factory, servo patterns are written onto it before it ever holds a single file. The drive follows those patterns the whole time, steering the head to stay on its track. In LTO-10, the head even tilts slightly, to make up for the tape shrinking and swelling with heat and humidity. The coating changed too. Instead of the iron oxide of old cassettes, modern tape uses tiny, carefully shaped particles of barium ferrite.

3:58Built to check itself

All of this sounds fragile. A kilometre of plastic, racing past a head. But the drive checks itself as it writes. Just behind the write head sits a read head, checking every block a moment later. If it doesn't come back right, the drive writes it again further along. On top of that, every block carries error-correcting codes, so the drive can rebuild the data even when part of it is damaged. Rebuilding data you can't read sounds impossible. It deserves a video of its own.

The LTO-9 specification allows one unreadable bit in every twelve exabytes. That's about twelve times everything CERN has stored, for a single error.

4:46The real advantage

But the real advantage is something tape doesn't do. It doesn't stay connected. For real protection, a written cartridge is taken out of the library altogether and put on a shelf. No power. No network. Nothing that a bug, or an attacker, can reach through a cable. That's what saved Gmail in 2011. The bug reached the copies that were online. The tapes weren't. Today, the same idea protects against ransomware. Malware can encrypt every disk in a building. It can't encrypt a cartridge sitting on a shelf.

Some cartridges can be written only once. After that, nobody can erase them, not even the person who runs the system. And sitting on a shelf costs nothing. Large disk archives often keep thousands of drives spinning, using power and making heat. A cartridge on a shelf uses no power at all. Makers rate the cartridges for around thirty years, if they're stored properly.

5:51What tape costs

Tape is also cheap. A thirty-terabyte cartridge costs around two hundred and sixty dollars. That's under ten dollars a terabyte. A large new hard drive costs around forty dollars a terabyte, more than four times as much. The catch is the drive itself. A new LTO-10 drive costs more than twenty thousand dollars. So tape pays off when one drive serves thousands of cartridges.

6:20The big weakness

So why don't we use tape for everything? Even if we ignore the price, tape still has one big weakness. It can't jump straight to your data. A hard drive moves its head to any file in a few thousandths of a second. Tape has to travel. If your file is at the far end of a kilometre of tape, the drive has to spool all the way there. It's just like rewinding a cassette to find the right song, or the right game. Finding a file can take around a minute. Thousands of times slower than a hard drive. That rules tape out for running a computer, a database, or millions of tiny files.

But it's perfect for the opposite job. Huge amounts of data, written once, and rarely needed again.

7:09Robot libraries

The cartridges sit in tape libraries: cabinets the length of a room, with thousands of slots inside. A robot runs along a rail, picks out the right cartridge, and slides it into a drive. At Lawrence Livermore, a laboratory in California, one library has nearly twenty thousand slots, and room for about three hundred petabytes. That's three hundred million gigabytes. To the software, it all looks like one enormous, rather slow disk.

For decades, that was the whole story. Tape was the quiet basement of the data centre.

7:47Then AI arrived

And then, something changed. Artificial intelligence. Training a large AI model takes enormous collections of text, images and video. Once it's collected, that data is worth keeping. But most of it isn't needed every day. That's exactly the kind of data tape was made for. In October 2026, Fujifilm, one of the biggest makers of tape, announced it would spend about ten billion yen so it could make roughly five times as much data tape within three years. The reason it gave? The spread of generative AI. The thing we used to wait for in front of the television is growing again, because of the newest technology we have.

And tape still has room to grow. Its bits are packed roughly a hundred times less densely than on the newest hard drives. In 2020, IBM and Fujifilm showed a test tape in the lab that could fit five hundred and eighty terabytes on a single cartridge. It isn't a product yet. But it shows how far tape can go.

8:56Tapes that outlive their drives

There's one catch, though. And it's a strange one. The tape lasts for decades. The drives don't. For years, a new LTO drive could still handle tapes two generations old. From LTO-8, only one. And an LTO-10 drive can't read any of them. Not a single older cartridge. Why break it? Remember the guide rails written at the factory? LTO-10 changed their pattern, so the head could follow even thinner tracks. Older tapes carry the old pattern, and staying compatible would have cost capacity. So what if a drive breaks? Not much. LTO is an open format, so any LTO-10 drive, from any maker, reads the same cartridge.

And the drives are built for hard work. An LTO-9 drive is rated for eighty thousand cartridge loads. In a library, a worn-out drive is simply swapped for a new one. Older drives haven't vanished either. LTO-9 drives are still on sale, and they read LTO-8. For anything older, there are used drives, and companies that rescue old tapes for a living. So archives don't just keep their tapes. Every few years, they copy everything onto new ones. So why rate them for thirty years? Because the tape is never the reason to move. Archives copy on their own schedule, and if a move runs late, the data is still safe on the shelf.

The tape can outlive every machine that knows how to play it.

10:38Back to the cassette

Which brings us back to that cassette. A strip of tape, a head, and patterns of magnetism. More than forty years later, the idea hasn't changed. Only the numbers and the technology have. Somewhere right now, a robot is pulling a cartridge off a shelf, pushing it into a drive, and pressing play. So here's a question for you. If you had a tape drive at home, what would you put on tape? Let me know in the comments.
