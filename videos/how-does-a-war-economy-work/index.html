How Society Actually Runs

How War Can Make an Economy Look Richer

9 min2026-09-05

When a war starts, a government suddenly needs millions of uniforms, trucks, shells, aircraft and tanks. But printing money does not create tanks — steel, workers and factories have to come from somewhere, and that somewhere is the civilian economy.

This video works through what that actually means. Why a country can win a war and still come out of it exhausted: Britain won in 1945, and rationing there did not end until 1954, almost nine years later. Why gross domestic product counts a destroyed tank twice and never subtracts the loss. Why more money chasing the same goods leads to rationing, and rationing to a black market. And why "the economy is booming" and "people are getting poorer" can both be true at the same time.

The full narration of the video.

0:00How a peacetime economy works

Imagine a country at peace. Factories make cars and refrigerators. Farmers grow food. Companies build houses. People go to work, earn money, and spend it on things they want. And then a war begins. Suddenly the government needs millions of uniforms. Trucks. Artillery shells. Aircraft, ships and tanks. But there is a problem. Where do all these things come from? You cannot simply print a trillion dollars and magically create a trillion dollars' worth of tanks. A war economy is, fundamentally, a giant redistribution of a country's resources. And that creates some deeply counterintuitive problems.

0:47The government becomes the customer

In a normal economy, production is driven by what civilians want. People want houses, so companies build houses. During a major war, the government becomes one of the largest customers in the entire economy. Instead of asking what consumers want, the economy increasingly asks what the war effort needs. A factory that used to build cars might start building military trucks. A steel mill that supplied construction companies might be supplying armour plate. The machinery hasn't necessarily changed. The destination of the resources has.

But where do the resources come from?

1:28Where the resources actually come from

A country has only so much steel. So much copper, oil and electricity. So much factory capacity. So many skilled workers. And so much time. If the military suddenly needs much more of all of that, somebody else has to get less. Suppose the government spends billions on weapons. The money itself isn't the weapon. The real question is what happens to the steel, the workers and the machines that build those weapons. If those resources were making civilian goods before, then civilian output has to shrink… unless the country can expand what it is able to make in total.

So a war economy is not about spending more money. It is about moving real resources from one part of the economy to another.

2:19The problem of workers

And then there is labour. A modern war can require millions of soldiers. But soldiers don't make cars, build houses, or run factories. So the economy faces a rather strange problem. The war needs workers. And the war also takes workers away. Governments respond in various ways. Women enter industries that were dominated by men. Older workers stay employed. Working hours go up.

2:51Britain: winning and paying for it

Britain is a particularly interesting example. When the Second World War ended in nineteen forty-five, Britain had won. But victory did not mean the country was suddenly rich again. Quite the opposite. Years of total war had consumed enormous resources. Britain had accumulated large debts, and its industry was still committed to the war effort. The war was over. But rationing continued. People still needed ration books for many basic goods. And not just for a few months.

3:26Rationing until 1954

Rationing in Britain finally ended in nineteen fifty-four. Almost nine years after the war it fought. Which illustrates an important principle. A country can win a war and still come out of it economically exhausted. Military victory and economic prosperity are not the same thing.

3:49The strange case of GDP

Now we get to the most confusing part. Imagine a factory builds a tank. That production counts towards gross domestic product. The tank is sent to the battlefield. It gets destroyed. The government orders another tank. More production is recorded. So production is happening. But has society become richer? Gross domestic product counts the second tank. It never subtracts the first one. The measure isn't useless. It measures economic production. But production is not the same thing as wealth, or as living standards. And that gap can grow surprisingly wide.

4:35The illusion of a boom

A war can create something that looks exactly like a boom. Factories run at full capacity. Unemployment falls. Industrial output explodes. So someone looks at all that and says: war stimulates the economy. But there is a question hiding underneath that sentence. Stimulates it to do what? The economy has been mobilised. It has not discovered a magical way to create unlimited resources.

5:07Paying for the war

So how does a government actually pay for all of this? Taxes. Borrowing. War bonds. And, in some circumstances, creating additional money. But there is no free lunch. If a government tries to use more resources than the economy can physically provide, something has to give.

5:27More money, same goods

Imagine there are a hundred units of civilian goods available. But people suddenly have much more money in their pockets. The factories have been redirected, so there are still only a hundred units. More money chasing the same amount of goods means prices tend to rise.

5:45Rationing and the black market

Which is why war economies so often end up rationing things. Rationing is basically the government saying: you have money, but you cannot buy as much as you want. Why? Because money is not the scarce resource. The physical goods are. There may be enough money for everyone to buy petrol. There simply isn't enough petrol. So instead of letting the price rise without limit, the government limits how much each person can buy. And whenever legal supply is restricted while demand stays high, something else tends to appear. The black market.

6:26The American mobilisation

One of the clearest examples of wartime mobilisation was the United States during the Second World War. Factories turned out aircraft, ships, trucks, tanks, engines and ammunition. Entire production lines were reorganised. The important point is that the United States did not simply print money and become more productive. It mobilised what it already had. People. Factories. Raw materials. Energy. Technology. Logistics. And it built more capacity as well.

7:00When the war ends

And then comes another problem. The war is over. Millions of soldiers come home. Factories that built weapons need something new to build. Military spending falls. People want houses, cars, refrigerators and clothes. But the economy cannot switch back overnight. A wartime economy has enormous inertia. Factories have specialised equipment. Workers have specialised skills. Governments have accumulated debt. Getting from war back to peace can be almost as difficult as getting from peace to war.

7:40Every ton goes somewhere

So what is the lesson underneath all of it? Every ton of steel that goes into a tank cannot also build a railway. Every worker in an ammunition factory cannot also build a house. War does not remove scarcity. It changes where the scarce things go.

8:00The paradox

Which is the paradox. A war economy can be extremely efficient at one thing. Turning a country's resources into military power. But that does not automatically make it richer. A successful war economy can consume wealth that took decades to build. Which is why two sentences can both be true at the same time. The economy is booming. And people are getting poorer. There is no contradiction. The first one is about production. The second is about what anybody actually gets to use. Because in the end, war is not a machine for creating resources. It is a machine for consuming them.
