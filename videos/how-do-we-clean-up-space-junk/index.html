How Do We Clean Up Space Junk?

12 min2026-10-01

Why can't we simply catch the junk, or blow it up? What is the Kessler syndrome? And why might the most useful trick be not to remove an object at all, but to give it a small push?

We look at robotic spacecraft, nets and lasers — radiation pressure and laser ablation — at what each of them is good for and where it runs into trouble, and at why the cheapest piece of space junk is the one that never becomes space junk.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Thousands of tonnes of junk

Imagine thousands of tonnes of junk flying around Earth. Dead satellites. Spent rocket stages. Fragments left behind by explosions and collisions. And sometimes, pieces of metal so small that they are difficult to track. All of them can be travelling at several kilometres per second. Space is enormous. So how can a little bit of garbage possibly be a problem? Because in space, a piece of garbage doesn't have to be large to cause enormous damage. It just has to hit something. And when it does, it can create hundreds or thousands of new pieces.

So we have a rather strange problem. How do you clean a place where there is no floor, no broom, and no garbage truck?

0:47Why it doesn't just fall down

On Earth, leaving something behind is easy. Eventually, gravity brings it down. In orbit, that is not necessarily what happens. A satellite in low Earth orbit can travel at roughly seven to eight kilometres per second. That is more than twenty-five thousand kilometres per hour. And two objects travelling in different orbits can collide at even higher relative speeds. So a tiny screw, a fragment of insulation, or a piece of metal is not really "just a screw." It is a projectile. And that is why some pieces of space debris can remain in orbit for decades, centuries, or even longer. It depends on how high they are. The higher the orbit, the less atmospheric drag they experience. And the longer they can stay there.

1:36Why not just catch it?

This is where things get complicated. Let's say we have a dead satellite. We could simply send another spacecraft to it. The spacecraft catches it. And brings it down. Problem solved. Except it is not that simple. The cleanup spacecraft first has to reach a similar orbit. It has to approach the target at the right speed and from the right direction. And it may have to approach a satellite that is tumbling uncontrollably through space. Many old satellites were never designed to be captured. They don't have handles. They don't have docking ports. And they may not have any functioning systems left to help control them. It is basically a flying wreck.

And now imagine trying to attach another spacecraft to it while both are moving at several kilometres per second. That is why active debris-removal missions are so difficult. Europe's ClearSpace-1 mission, for example, is designed to capture a non-functional object in orbit and bring it down together with the spacecraft. But what about the enormous number of smaller pieces? For those, we need other ideas.

2:48Why not just destroy it?

This sounds reasonable. We have an object we don't want. So we destroy it. But that could make the problem much worse. If you smash one large piece of debris into hundreds of smaller pieces, you haven't removed it. You have created a cloud of debris. And every one of those pieces can collide with something else. That can create even more fragments. And the cycle can continue. This is the basic idea behind the scenario known as the Kessler syndrome. A chain reaction of collisions could create so much debris that some orbits become increasingly difficult or dangerous to use. So if we want to clean up space, we cannot simply smash everything into smaller pieces.

3:40The first trick: change the orbit

Do we actually have to carry it down? No. And this is where the first trick appears. We don't necessarily have to catch the debris and tow it away. We just have to change its orbit. If we lower the lowest point of an object's orbit enough, it can begin passing through the upper atmosphere. The atmosphere creates drag. The object loses energy. Its orbit gets lower. Eventually, it enters the denser atmosphere. And many small objects burn up on the way down. So the problem suddenly changes. It is no longer: "How do we get this satellite back to Earth?" It is: "How do we give it a tiny push?" And there are several surprisingly strange ways to do that.

4:26Method 1: a robot

The most obvious solution is still a robot. Send a spacecraft to the dead satellite. Grab it. And change its orbit. The advantage is that this can work even with relatively large objects. The disadvantage is that every piece of debris is different. Every mission costs money. Every target requires navigation, rendezvous, and some way of safely attaching to it. And if there are hundreds or thousands of objects we want to remove, we would need a lot of missions. So perhaps we need something that doesn't have to fly to every single piece.

5:03Method 2: a net

Yes. A net. The idea is surprisingly simple. A spacecraft approaches the target and deploys a large net. The net catches the object. The spacecraft can then change its orbit and bring the target down. The advantage is that you don't necessarily need a precisely designed attachment point. You just need to catch the object. But there is a small problem. You are deploying a large net while both spacecraft and debris are moving at several kilometres per second. And you really don't want your net to become tangled with something else. So even this apparently simple idea requires extremely precise navigation and control.

5:45Method 3: a laser

Now we get to a solution that sounds like science fiction. A laser. But not because we are going to blast the satellite apart. That would be exactly the problem we are trying to avoid. Instead, a laser could give the object a tiny push. There are two main ideas. The first uses radiation pressure. Photons have no rest mass, but they carry momentum. When light hits an object, it transfers some of that momentum. Normally, the effect is tiny. But in space, we can potentially apply it very precisely and over a long period of time.

The second idea is much more interesting. Laser ablation. A powerful laser heats a tiny area of the object's surface until some of the material vaporises. That material shoots away from the surface. And because of the conservation of momentum, the object receives a small impulse in the opposite direction. In other words, it is basically a tiny rocket engine. Except the fuel is the object itself. And the laser doesn't have to physically touch it. Why could a laser actually work? Because we don't need to change the object's speed by kilometres per second. We only need to change it by a tiny amount.

Imagine a ball moving around Earth. Give it a very small change in velocity. You might not notice anything immediately. But its orbit is now slightly different. And after many orbits, that tiny difference can become significant. The object's lowest point can move into the upper atmosphere. And then the atmosphere takes over. That is what makes the idea so interesting. The laser doesn't have to destroy the debris. It just has to give it a little push.

7:38Why we don't have space lasers yet

So why don't we have giant space lasers already? Because, of course, there are problems. The first is accuracy. We have to know exactly where the object is. And that is particularly difficult for small pieces of debris. The second problem is energy. Ablation requires a sufficiently intense laser beam. The third problem is the atmosphere. If we are firing a laser from the ground, the beam has to pass through Earth's atmosphere, which can scatter and distort it. And the fourth problem may be the most important. What if we get the direction wrong? With objects moving this fast, even a small error can produce a different orbit than intended.

So a laser is not a giant space vacuum cleaner. It is more like an extremely precise orbital brake.

8:30Which tool for which junk

What about the really big stuff? This is where lasers become less attractive. If you have a satellite weighing several tonnes, changing its orbit requires a lot more energy. So for large objects, a robotic spacecraft may make much more sense. For smaller debris, a laser could potentially be useful. For objects that can be captured, we could use nets or other capture systems. And for future satellites, the best solution may be to design them so they can safely leave orbit at the end of their lives. Because the cheapest piece of space junk… is the piece that never becomes space junk.

9:08Prevention

Space debris is not only something created decades ago. Every year, we launch more spacecraft. And the number of objects around Earth continues to grow. So we cannot solve the problem simply by cleaning. We also have to prevent new debris from accumulating. That means things like reliable end-of-life disposal. Tracking objects. Avoiding collisions. And designing spacecraft with their eventual disposal in mind. Otherwise, we would end up in a rather absurd situation. We would build increasingly sophisticated vacuum cleaners… while continuing to throw garbage onto the floor.

9:50Can we actually clean up space?

So, can we actually clean up space? Probably not with one magical invention. There probably won't be a single giant machine that circles Earth and collects everything. Instead, we will need a combination of technologies. Robots for large objects. Capture systems for suitable targets. Lasers for some smaller pieces. Better tracking of objects. And, perhaps most importantly, rules and engineering practices that prevent new debris from being created in the first place. Because in space, we don't always have to remove an object. Sometimes, we just have to push it. Maybe by a hundred metres per second. Barely more than one percent of its speed. Then gravity and the atmosphere can do the rest.

But these are only some of the ideas. Because lasers, robots, and nets are not the only strange ways we could deal with the junk orbiting our planet. There are concepts involving harpoons, giant sails, magnetic fields, electrodynamic tethers, and even using sunlight itself. So in the next episode, we will look at some of the other ways humanity is trying to answer an absurd question: How do you clean up something moving at twenty-eight thousand kilometres per hour?
