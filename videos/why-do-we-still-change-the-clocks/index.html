Why Do We Still Change the Clocks?

10 min2026-10-20

So if changing the clocks is such a bad idea, why is it so hard to stop? This video looks at the problem the clocks were meant to solve, how a wartime measure became part of everyday life, what happened when America tried daylight saving time all year in 1974, why Russia and the European Union got stuck too, how Spain lives with its clocks an hour off the Sun, and why simply changing our schedules instead is harder than it sounds.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The same argument every year

Twice a year, most of America moves its clocks by one hour. In March, we lose an hour of sleep. In November, the hour comes back. And every time, the same argument starts. It's bad for our health. It's pointless. Just pick one time and keep it. Politicians seem to agree. In 2022, the United States Senate passed a bill to stop changing the clocks. Without a single objection. It went nowhere. Four years later, the House passed another bill, to make daylight saving time permanent. It's now stuck in the Senate. And the clocks are still changing.

So if changing the clocks is such a terrible idea, why is it so difficult to stop? America has actually tried. In 1974, it stopped changing the clocks. The experiment didn't last a year.

0:56Daylight changes, schedules don't

To see why, we need to start with the problem the clocks were trying to solve.

The problem is simple. Daylight changes through the year. Our schedules don't. School starts at eight. Work starts at nine. In June and in December alike. But the Sun doesn't follow a timetable. In Seattle, the shortest day of the year has about eight and a half hours of light. The longest has sixteen. In Miami, the difference is much smaller. Ten and a half hours in winter, not quite fourteen in summer. The farther north you live, the more the day stretches and shrinks around a schedule that never moves. In summer, that means something odd. On standard time, the Sun in Boston rises just after four in the morning. Hours of daylight, while almost everyone is asleep.

Daylight saving time is a trick for moving some of that light from the early morning to the evening. Not by changing the Sun. By changing what we call the hour.

2:11A war measure

And as a national law, it started as a war measure. On 30 April 1916, Germany moved its clocks forward to save coal for the war. Britain followed three weeks later. The United States joined in 1918, and gave it up a year later. It was so unpopular with farmers that Congress repealed it over the President's veto. In the Second World War it came back, all year round, under the name "War Time". After the war, every state and city did more or less what it wanted. In the 1960s, one bus ride, just thirty-five miles from Ohio into West Virginia, went through seven changes of time.

In 1966, Congress finally set one set of rules for the whole country. States could opt out entirely, but they couldn't pick their own dates. And so a wartime measure quietly became part of normal life.

3:11What it costs our bodies

The cost is real, though. Our bodies run on an internal clock, and that clock is set mostly by light. When the clocks jump forward in March, the alarm rings an hour earlier, as far as our body is concerned. For days, many people sleep worse. Some studies have found a brief rise in car crashes and heart attacks right after that change.

3:36Which time would we keep?

So the obvious answer is: stop changing the clocks. Pick one time, and keep it all year. Fine. But which one?

Permanent standard time keeps winter mornings as they are. But every summer evening loses an hour of light, and in the north, the Sun rises around four in the morning. Permanent daylight saving time keeps the long summer evenings. But in winter, the mornings get dark. Really dark. In Indianapolis, the Sun would rise after nine in early January. In Detroit and Seattle, around nine.

4:14America tried it in 1974

And America knows what that feels like, because it tried. In January 1974, in the middle of an oil crisis, the United States switched to daylight saving time all year round, to save energy. When the law was signed, about four in five Americans supported it. Then came the dark January mornings, and children walking to school before sunrise. Within weeks, support fell to around two in five. By October, Congress voted to bring back standard time for the winter. It had been planned for two years. Year-round daylight saving time lasted ten months.

4:54The odd part of the law

And here's the strange part. Today, the law lets any state choose permanent standard time. Arizona and Hawaii already have it. But nineteen states have passed laws for the other one, permanent daylight saving time. And that, they can't have without Congress. So people agree that they hate the switch. They don't agree on what to switch to.

5:20Russia and Europe

And it's not just an American problem. In 2011, Russia stopped changing its clocks and kept summer time all year. In Moscow, the winter Sun now rose at around ten in the morning. Three years later, Russia went the other way, to permanent winter time. In 2018, the European Union asked its citizens what they thought. More than four and a half million answered, and eighty-four per cent of them wanted the changes to end. It wasn't a representative poll. Most of the answers came from Germany. The result showed how strongly the people who took part felt about it.

The following spring, the European Parliament voted to do it. All that was left was for the countries to agree which time each would keep. And that's where it stopped. Governments worried that neighbours would choose differently, and Europe would end up as a patchwork of time zones. That was in 2019. Europe is still changing its clocks.

6:24Move the day, not the clock

So maybe we're asking the wrong question. Maybe the clock isn't the thing we should be moving. Look at Spain. Madrid lies west of London. By the Sun, it belongs in London's time zone. But in 1940, Spain moved its clocks to Central European Time, the time of Paris and Berlin. And it never moved them back. So in summer, the Sun over Madrid is highest at around a quarter past two, and it sets close to ten in the evening. But Spanish life stayed with the Sun. Lunch at two. Dinner at nine, or later. By the clock, that's late. By the Sun, it isn't.

Spain is a reminder that the clock and the schedule are two different things. You can move the day without touching the clock. And in small ways, we already do. Summer hours at the office. Flexible start times. Shift work. So why not do that everywhere? Start work at seven in summer, and at nine in winter, and never touch the clocks again?

7:29Why not just change schedules?

Because a society is not one person adjusting their alarm clock. If your office starts earlier, your children's school has to as well. And their bus. And the daycare. And the shop you stop at on the way. And the train timetable. And the company in another state you have a call with every morning. Every one of those would have to be moved separately, by someone, and agreed with everyone else. Changing the clocks does all of that in a single step. At two in the morning on a Sunday, everyone following the same rules changes together.

It's clumsy. But it's brutally simple.

8:08Ask a programmer

Well… simple to coordinate. Anyone who has written software that deals with time knows how much trouble one hour can cause. In November, the hour from one to two happens twice. In March, the hour from two to three doesn't happen at all. Calendars, recurring meetings, alarms, payroll, night shifts… all of them have to know about it. And the rules keep changing. The database that computers use to keep track of time zones is updated again and again, whenever some government, somewhere, changes its clocks.

8:44So why do we still change the clocks?

So why do we still change the clocks? Not because anyone thinks it's perfect. Because daylight changes, and our schedules don't. We could change our schedules instead of changing our clocks. And sometimes, we already do. But a society is millions of people, businesses, schools, trains and computers, trying to agree on when the day begins. Daylight changes. Our schedules don't change nearly as easily. Maybe daylight saving time isn't a perfect solution. Maybe there isn't one. But of all the imperfect ones, switching twice a year might just be the least bad.

The strange thing isn't that we change the clocks. It's that moving one hour on a clock can be easier than changing the daily schedule of an entire society.
