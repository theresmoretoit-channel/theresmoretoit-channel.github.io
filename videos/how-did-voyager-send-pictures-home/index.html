How Did Voyager Send Pictures Home?

9 min2026-10-16

The story starts twelve years before Voyager's launch, with Mariner 4 at Mars and the first close-up image of another planet, which engineers at JPL coloured in by hand with pastels. It passes through a lab at Kodak, where Steven Sasson built a very different kind of camera in 1975. And it ends with the most famous picture Voyager ever took: the Pale Blue Dot, sent home from six billion kilometres away.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A camera built around a TV tube

In August 1989, a spacecraft flew past Neptune, more than four billion kilometres from Earth, and took pictures of it. Its camera had no memory card, and no sensor like the one in your phone. It was built around a television tube, designed in the 1970s. And yet the pictures arrived. Thousands of them. So how do you send a photograph home from the edge of the Solar System? To answer that, we have to go back twelve years before Voyager's launch, to the first close-up pictures of another planet.

0:35Mariner 4 at Mars

In July 1965, a small probe called Mariner 4 flew past Mars and took twenty-two pictures. Its camera was a vidicon, a kind of television camera tube. It didn't produce a photo. It produced a signal, rising and falling as the picture was scanned line by line. But the probe's radio was far too weak to send a live television signal. So it did something clever. It chopped every picture into two hundred by two hundred dots. The brightness of each dot became a number from zero to sixty-three. Six bits per dot. The numbers went onto a tape recorder, and then home by radio, at eight and a third bits per second. At that speed, one picture took about eight hours.

And when the numbers reached Earth, the engineers at JPL didn't want to wait for the computers. They printed the raw numbers on long strips of paper,

1:32A picture coloured in by hand

laid them side by side, and coloured them in by hand with pastels, like a paint-by-numbers kit. The first close-up image of Mars was a drawing made from data. And that's the chain this story is about. A picture becomes a signal. The signal becomes numbers. The numbers travel by radio. And on Earth, they become a picture again. So was Mariner 4 a digital camera? Not really. Its camera was analogue. Everything after the camera was digital.

2:06A camera in a lab at Kodak

A digital camera in the modern sense came ten years later, and not in space. In a lab at Kodak. In December 1975, an engineer called Steven Sasson built a camera around a new kind of chip, a CCD. A CCD isn't scanned by an electron beam. It's a grid of tiny light sensors, each measuring its own patch of light. The grid is built into the chip itself. Sasson's grid was a hundred by a hundred. Black and white. Recording one picture onto a cassette tape took twenty-three seconds. Kodak never sold it. But it answers a question people often ask. Did we have digital cameras in the 1970s? Yes. In a lab.

Voyager's cameras, though, belonged to the other branch. The one that started with Mariner.

3:10Voyager's cameras

Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 were launched in 1977. Each carried two cameras. A wide-angle one for big scenes, and a narrow-angle one for detail. Both were slow-scan vidicons, modified versions of the cameras that had flown on earlier Mariners. Light from a planet left a pattern of charge on a thin layer of selenium and sulphur. That layer could hold the image for more than a minute and a half. Then an electron beam swept across it. Eight hundred lines, and eight hundred points on each line. Reading out one frame took forty-eight seconds. Each point became a number from zero to two hundred and fifty-five. Eight bits.

That's sixteen times as many dots as Mariner 4, and four times as many shades. More than five million bits in a single picture. By today's standards, that's tiny. About six hundred and forty kilobytes of raw image data, less than one photo from your phone. And the radio link added extra bits to protect it from noise.

4:17Getting the pictures home

But taking the picture was never the hard part. Getting it home was.

In 1979, at Jupiter, Voyager could send a hundred and fifteen thousand bits per second. One picture in under a minute. When Earth was out of view, or the link was busy, pictures went onto a tape recorder, with room for about a hundred of them. But every time the distance doubles, the signal reaching Earth gets four times weaker. At Neptune, Voyager 2 would be about six times as far from Earth as it had been at Jupiter. Without changes, it would manage around three thousand bits per second. At that rate, a single picture would take almost half an hour.

5:04Rebuilding the dishes on Earth

So the engineers improved both ends. On Earth, the largest dishes of NASA's Deep Space Network were rebuilt, from sixty-four metres to seventy. And for the Neptune flyby, they listened together with other radio telescopes, including the twenty-seven dishes of the Very Large Array in New Mexico.

5:24New software for a distant spacecraft

The other end was harder. Voyager 2 was already beyond Saturn, and nobody could touch it. So they changed its software. Voyager's computers were tiny. Its flight data computer ran about eighty thousand instructions a second. Your phone manages billions. Engineers on Earth wrote a new program and uploaded it to the backup computer. From then on, Voyager 2 didn't send the brightness of every dot. It sent how much each dot differed from the one next to it. In dark space and smooth clouds, those differences are small numbers, and small numbers need fewer bits.

A typical picture shrank by sixty percent. And a new error-correcting code cut down the extra bits that protected it from noise. At Neptune, in 1989, Voyager 2 sent more than twenty thousand bits per second. Almost seven times what it would have managed. A picture in about a minute and a half.

6:27So were they digital cameras?

So, did we have digital cameras in the 1970s? In a lab at Kodak, yes. But the cameras that photographed Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune were television tubes. What made them digital was turning that signal into numbers. The numbers. The tape. The radio. And software that could be rewritten from billions of kilometres away.

6:55The Pale Blue Dot

And the most famous picture those cameras ever took wasn't of a planet they visited. By 1990, Voyager 1 had passed Jupiter and Saturn, and was heading out of the Solar System. Its planetary work was done. Back in 1981, the astronomer Carl Sagan, a member of the imaging team, had proposed one last picture. Turn the cameras around, and look back at home. On the 14th of February 1990, Voyager 1 took sixty frames. A portrait of the Solar System, from beyond Neptune. Six planets made it into the frames. Venus, Earth, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.

Voyager was about six billion kilometres away. From there, Earth covered about an eighth of a single pixel. The streak of light around it isn't something out in space. It's sunlight, scattered inside the camera. Three exposures through colour filters were combined into the image we now call the Pale Blue Dot. Every person who has ever lived lived somewhere inside it.

8:06Eleven weeks to come home

Thirty-four minutes after the pictures of Earth were taken, Voyager 1's cameras were switched off, to save power. They were never turned on again. The pictures waited on the tape recorder. The last of the data reached Earth on the 1st of May 1990. Almost eleven weeks later. A television tube. Eight bits per dot. A transmitter of about twenty watts. And a family portrait that took eleven weeks to come home.
