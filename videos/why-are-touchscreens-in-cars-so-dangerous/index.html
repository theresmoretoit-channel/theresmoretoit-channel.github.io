Why Are Touchscreens in Cars So Dangerous?

10 min2026-10-07

Researchers have measured how long that takes. A Swedish car magazine timed the same simple jobs at 110 km/h: one car needed about ten seconds, another almost forty-five. So if carmakers know all this, why is almost every new car moving its controls onto a screen?

The full narration of the video.

0:00A knob and a menu

Imagine you're driving at fifty kilometres an hour. About thirty miles an hour. You want to turn the temperature down. In an old car, you reach for a knob. You don't need to look for it. You just reach out, turn it, and you're done. Now do the same thing in a modern car. You look at the screen. Find the climate menu. Tap it. Find the temperature. Tap again. Then look back at the road. It sounds like a tiny difference. But the car hasn't stopped. At fifty kilometres an hour, every second is fourteen metres. Fourteen metres, with nobody watching the road.

For decades, we made cars safer with better brakes and airbags. And then we put computers right in front of the driver. So how dangerous is that, really? And if it's a problem, why is almost every new car doing it?

1:06It isn't the screen itself

Let's be fair to the screen first. A map on a screen is better than a paper atlas unfolded over the steering wheel. A reversing camera makes parking safer. The trouble is something more specific. Researchers call it visual-manual interaction. You have to look. You have to understand what you see. And then you have to touch exactly the right spot. A button has a shape. A knob has resistance. A switch has a position. A touchscreen has… just glass. Your fingers don't know where the virtual button is. Only your eyes do. And your eyes are exactly what driving needs most.

Think about what a computer on your desk asks you to do. Look at the screen. Move the mouse. Click. Read the result. That's perfectly reasonable at a desk. The desk isn't moving. In a car, the screen wants your eyes. So does the road. And they can't both have them at the same time.

2:13How long can you look away?

So how long can a driver look away? There's no magic number. But in 2013, America's road safety regulator published voluntary guidelines for carmakers. A task on a built-in screen should be possible with glances of no more than two seconds each, and no more than twelve seconds in total. Two seconds doesn't sound like much. At a hundred kilometres an hour, it's fifty-six metres. It's not a line between safe and dangerous. It's a test that a good interface should pass.

2:49Thirty cars, forty seconds

So how do real cars do? In 2017, researchers at the University of Utah tested the infotainment systems of thirty new cars, for the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety. They found huge differences between cars. Some systems were far more demanding than others. And the most demanding task was entering a destination into the navigation. On average, it took about forty seconds. Forty seconds. At fifty kilometres an hour, that's more than half a kilometre. Of course, nobody stares at a screen for forty seconds straight. The glances come in pieces. A second here. Two seconds there.

But every one of those pieces is a stretch of road you didn't see.

3:39The Swedish test

In 2022, a Swedish car magazine ran a simpler test. They took eleven modern cars and one Volvo from 2005. The old Volvo had only buttons and knobs. At a hundred and ten kilometres an hour, drivers did four simple jobs. Seat heating and temperature. The radio. The trip computer. Dimming the lights. The old Volvo: about ten seconds. Roughly three hundred metres. The slowest modern car: almost forty-five seconds. Nearly one point four kilometres. Same jobs. Same speed. More than four times the distance. But a brand-new Dacia took about thirteen and a half seconds. And so did a brand-new Volvo.

What mattered wasn't how modern the car was. It was how much attention the interface demanded.

4:36Phones and voice control

We already tell drivers not to look at their phones. A dashboard screen has the same basic problem. Your eyes are somewhere other than the road. That doesn't mean the two are equally dangerous. A car's screen usually sits closer to your line of sight, and some functions lock while you're driving. But the road doesn't care why you looked away.

So why not just talk to the car? Voice control helps a lot. You don't have to look at anything. But voice control doesn't solve everything. The same researchers in Utah have spent years studying this. Even with their eyes on the road, drivers busy with a demanding voice task react more slowly, and notice less around them. Hands-free doesn't mean distraction-free. It just means less of it.

5:31Why carmakers do it

So here's the strange part. None of this is a secret. Carmakers employ whole teams of engineers who study exactly this. So why is almost every new car moving its controls onto a screen? Because from the carmaker's side, a touchscreen makes a lot of sense. Every physical button is a part. It has to be designed, tested, wired and fitted. And then kept as a spare part for years. A car with a few dozen switches needs a few dozen parts, and the wiring to connect them. A screen replaces all of them with one part, and software. And software is flexible. One screen can serve every model, every trim level, every market.

A different language? Change the software. A new feature? Change the software. Some cars even get new functions years later, over the air, like a phone update.

6:27The showroom

And then there's the showroom. A buyer spends a few minutes in a new car before buying it. A big, bright screen looks like the future. The problems show up later. On a dark motorway, when you just want the fan a little lower. That's the real trade-off. The same technology that makes a car cheaper to build and easier to update can make it harder to drive. Nobody decided to make cars more dangerous. Each decision makes sense on its own. The cost just lands on the driver.

7:05Buttons come back

And now, some of that is starting to change. Volkswagen moved many of its controls to touch panels and sliders. Drivers complained. In 2025, its head of design called it a mistake, and promised physical buttons for the most important functions.

7:23Euro NCAP 2026

And in Europe, crash tests have started to count it too. From 2026, Euro NCAP, the European safety rating, looks for five functions on real, physical controls. Indicators. Hazard lights. The horn. The wipers. And the emergency call. A car that hides them in a screen loses points on its way to five stars. Some of the oldest controls in the car are now counted as safety features.

7:53What a good interface looks like

So what should a car interface actually look like? Probably not fewer computers. Just computers that ask less of the driver. Things you need while driving get physical controls, the kind you can find without looking. Information you need to see goes where your eyes already are, behind the steering wheel, or projected onto the windscreen. Voice handles what doesn't need your eyes. And complicated settings can wait until you've stopped. Many new cars also watch the driver. A camera can tell when you've looked away too long.

That helps. But it can't be the excuse. "Don't worry, the car will watch you while you look at the screen" is the wrong way round. The better answer is simpler. Don't make the driver look at the screen in the first place.

8:46A car isn't a laptop

For decades, carmakers competed on mechanical engineering. Better engines. Better brakes. Now much of that competition happens in software. Bigger screens. More menus. More apps. And that's impressive. But a car isn't a laptop. A laptop can ask for all of your attention. A car can't. Because the most important task in your car isn't the infotainment system. It's driving. And its interface is the whole world outside your windscreen.

9:20The price of a glance

So maybe the best dashboard isn't the one with the biggest screen. It's the one you can use without looking at it. Because at a hundred kilometres an hour, every second you look away is twenty-eight metres. And that's the price of every glance.
