Internet Explorer 6 — Why Did It Stop For Five Years?

0:00Five years and two months

In August 2001, Microsoft released Internet Explorer 6. The next version of the browser came out in October 2006. Five years and two months. In between, no new version arrived for the browser that almost everyone in the world was using.

And it wasn't because nobody complained. By the middle of that gap there were whole websites devoted to what was wrong with it, and a small industry of people working around it. The strange part is that Microsoft never hid what was happening. They announced it, in public, and almost nobody noticed.

0:40This is part four

This is part four. Part one was how one browser became a platform. Part two was what that did to the people building for it. Part three was why nobody could switch it off. This one is the gap underneath all three. Not what the browser did. Why it stopped.

1:01Ninety percent of the market

Start at the top. In 2001, Internet Explorer 6 arrived with Windows XP, and within a couple of years it had more than ninety percent of the market. Now ask the question that almost never gets asked about winning. What do you build next, when there is nothing left to beat?

1:22The courts

There was one obvious answer in the room, and it had been there for years. The courts. In May 1998, the American government and twenty states sued Microsoft. The heart of the case was this browser, and how it arrived on your computer. In 2000, a judge ordered the company to be broken into two. And in June 2001, an appeals court kept the finding that Microsoft held a monopoly, and threw out the breakup.

1:52Eight weeks later

Eight weeks later, Internet Explorer 6 shipped.

The settlement that followed was about how Windows had to behave. Let other people's software sit in the same places. Let a computer maker hide the icon. Nowhere in any of it was there a line that said: and the browser has to get better. So the threat of a breakup ended in the same season that Internet Explorer 6 arrived. And then the company said what it planned to do next.

2:26The last standalone version

In an online chat in May 2003, a program manager on the browser team explained the future. Internet Explorer 6 would be the last version you could install on its own. From then on, the browser would arrive only as part of Windows. Say that slowly, because it isn't a company admitting defeat. It's a company saying this is no longer a product. It is a feature of the operating system. Which is exactly what part one said had happened. The difference is that here, they said it out loud, and put a date on it. And once the browser is part of the system, the browser can only improve when the system does. So the next question is what the system was doing.

3:13The next Windows

The next Windows was going to be enormous. A new way to draw everything on screen. A new way to store files. A new way to write the applications on top. It was late. Then it was later. In 2004 the whole project was restarted from a different codebase, and pieces of it were quietly dropped. It finally shipped in 2007, more than five years after the browser we're talking about. So the browser was waiting for a system that was itself being rebuilt.

3:48Where the people went

But where did the people go? We don't have to guess, because one of them wrote it down afterwards. An engineer called Chris Wilson had worked on Internet Explorer since 1995. Every major release, all the way to version 6. Afterwards he moved onto the rethink of that Windows platform. In his words, that is what most of them moved over to work on. And he gives a list of reasons, which is more honest than giving one. Rich applications inside a browser hadn't taken off yet. The dot com bubble had burst. Security problems were eating enormous amounts of effort. They were getting stuck in their own backwards compatibility.

And they were, in his words, pretty far ahead of the competition's released product. Then he writes the sentence this whole video is about.

4:42The sentence this is about

Microsoft does seem to work best with strong competition motivating it. That is someone who had led major parts of Internet Explorer, saying the browser stopped because nothing was pushing it.

4:57The other browser

And here is where the story gets uncomfortable, because at that exact moment Microsoft had another browser. A different one, with a different engine. In March 2000, the company released Internet Explorer 5 for the Macintosh. It did not run the engine from Windows. It ran one called Tasman, written to follow the standards of the day. Better support for stylesheets. And no box model bug. That's the one from part one. The argument about whether the width of a box includes its border, the disagreement that cost a decade of workarounds. This browser simply didn't have it.

5:38Two modes, still there

It also introduced the trick that modern browsers still use today. Look at the very first line of a page, and decide from it how strictly to treat everything underneath. Two modes. One for pages written to the standards, one for the pile of pages already out there. Invented by the team building a Microsoft browser for Apple computers. So Microsoft could build a standards browser. We know, because they did.

6:06Two browsers, one summer

In June 2003 they ended its development, six weeks after announcing that Internet Explorer 6 was the last standalone version. Two browsers, one company, one summer.

Which changes the story we usually tell. It isn't Microsoft couldn't fix it. It's Microsoft no longer had a reason to.

And while the browser stood still, the web did not. Here's the part I find genuinely strange.

6:47What was already inside

A lot of what the web became next was already sitting inside that frozen browser. Go back to the late nineties. A team at Microsoft is building a version of the company's email software that runs inside a browser. They want a page that can fetch new mail without loading itself again. Nothing on the web could do that. Pages arrived whole, or not at all.

7:12A page that could ask

So they shipped a small component that let a page make a request on its own and get the answer back. It arrived as an ActiveX component, which tells you everything about where it came from, and it was named after a format that was fashionable that year. That component sat inside Internet Explorer 5. Then inside Internet Explorer 6. Then it sat there for five years, while nothing happened. Everyone else eventually built their own. Other browsers copied the idea, kept the name, and it is now part of the standard web. And then other people picked it up and built things with it. A mail service that never reloaded the page. A map you could drag with the mouse.

7:58AJAX

The technique got a name, and for about three years you couldn't go near anything about the web without hearing it. AJAX. The X in the middle stands for a format almost nobody ended up using. The rest of it came out of a component built for company email in the nineties.

So the browser everybody was cursing was also carrying the piece that made the next web feel alive. And it wasn't the only piece.

8:27What else came from here

The property that lets a script throw away a chunk of a page and put new markup in its place came from Internet Explorer. Every framework since is a descendant of that idea. The switch that turns part of a page into something you can type into came from Internet Explorer. Every rich text editor you have ever typed in sits on top of it. And the smallest one is my favourite. When a line of text is too long for its box, and it ends in three quiet dots instead of being chopped off, that came from here too.

9:02Ask your browser what it is

Then there is the joke you can check right now, on the device you are watching this on. Open any browser and ask it what it is. Chrome will tell you it is Mozilla. Then that it is like Gecko, which is the engine inside Firefox. Then it will mention Safari. Everything except what it actually is. Every browser spent the nineties claiming to be the popular one, so that websites would hand it the good page instead of the stripped down one. Internet Explorer did it to Netscape. Everyone did it back to Internet Explorer. And nobody has dared to stop since.

Your browser is still lying about who it is, because of arguments that were settled twenty five years ago.

9:50The other half

So that is one half of those five years. The web quietly built itself out of parts that browser already had. The other half is what came in through the same door. Part two ended with a crash. A broken tag in a web page could shut a window that was listing files on your own computer. Because the browser was not a program sitting on the system. It was part of it. Now run that forwards for five years.

10:20A way to extend it

Internet Explorer had a way to extend it. A small piece of code could load itself into every browser window and watch everything that happened there. It was built for useful things. Reading tools. Download managers. And it ran with everything you were allowed to do on your own machine. So by 2004 this was an industry.

10:44Four toolbars

Search toolbars. Shopping toolbars. Weather toolbars. Machines with four of them stacked above the page, eating a third of the screen, and nobody in the house remembers installing one. Some of them were paid for by whoever wanted your search box. Some came bundled with something free you actually wanted. And some needed no installing at all. You didn't click anything. You visited a page.

And there was nowhere to upgrade to. No newer browser was coming, because the browser was part of Windows now, and Windows was late. Which is why the fix, when it finally arrived, came in the strangest possible shape.

11:28A service pack, not a browser

August 2004. Not a new browser. A service pack for the operating system. Inside it, a blocker for pop-up windows. A manager for all those add-ons. A bar across the top that told you when a page wanted something. And a browser that finally asked before certain web controls could reach into the machine. Sit with that for a second. To make the browser safer, Microsoft had to update the operating system. That is the moment part one stops being an argument about strategy. The browser really had become part of Windows. The proof is in the shape of the repair.

And it landed exactly the way part three would predict. Company applications that had been written for a browser that trusted everything started asking for permission, and the complaints came in from the people who had built on it.

12:26Firefox, then RSA

Three months later, Firefox reached version 1, and people who had never chosen a browser in their lives started downloading one. Three months after that, in February 2005, Bill Gates stood up at a security conference and announced a new Internet Explorer. The reason he gave was security. Not standards. Not the pixels. Not ten years of developer complaints. Internet Explorer 7 arrived in October 2006, into a web that had spent five years learning to work without it.

13:02So why did it stop?

So why did it stop?

Not because Microsoft couldn't build a better browser. There was one, in the same company, in the same year, with the famous bug already fixed. It stopped because it had already won. The competition that had driven the first browser war was gone. The court case was over. The people had been moved onto the next thing.

And that is the part worth keeping from four videos about an old browser. A product with nothing left to beat stops changing.

And everything it already has becomes the ceiling for everyone else.
