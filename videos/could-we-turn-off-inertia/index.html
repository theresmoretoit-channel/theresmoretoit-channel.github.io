Physics That Sounds Simple Until You Ask

Could We Make Acceleration Impossible to Feel?

9 min2026-09-03

What would we actually have to invent to remove it?

Moving your seat backwards while the vehicle accelerates works on paper, until you do the arithmetic: one G for ten seconds needs a seat rail almost half a kilometre long. Pushing the passenger directly just moves the problem — something else has to take the reaction. We haven't eliminated inertia. We are simply fighting it.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The question

Could we make passengers in a plane or car feel no acceleration at all? The vehicle could speed up. It could slow down. It could turn. And the people inside would feel absolutely nothing. No pushing into the seat. No being thrown forward when the vehicle brakes. And no sudden jolts during turbulence.

0:24Acceleration is a ceiling

But this is not only about comfort. Acceleration is a ceiling. A fighter pilot wears a suit that squeezes the legs and the belly. And the pilot strains against the force the whole time. Together, that buys about nine times gravity. For tens of seconds. Without that, a few times gravity is enough to drain the blood from your head. Your vision greys out. Then you black out. So every vehicle that carries people is built around a very low limit. Not the limit of the engine. The limit of the passenger.

1:02What would we have to invent?

This sounds like science fiction. And it is. But the physics behind it is surprisingly interesting. So what would we actually have to invent to make it possible? Let's start with the simplest problem: Acceleration.

1:18What you actually feel

When a car accelerates, your body wants to keep moving at its original speed. The seat pushes you forward. That force accelerates your body along with the car. And you feel that force as acceleration. When the car brakes, the opposite happens. The seat belt stops you. So if we want you to feel no acceleration, we somehow have to prevent that force from reaching you.

1:45Idea one: move the seat

One obvious solution would be to move your seat. When the plane accelerates forward, the seat could move backward at exactly the right speed. From your point of view, you would remain almost perfectly still.

1:59What a flight simulator really does

This is not science fiction. A flight simulator sits on six moving legs. It moves well enough to train real pilots. But look at what it actually does. The legs give it a metre or so of room. So the platform cannot keep accelerating. When the pilot opens the throttle, the simulator tilts backwards. And gravity pushes the pilot into the seat. The inner ear cannot tell the difference. So the simulator is not cancelling acceleration. It is borrowing gravity to imitate it. Remember that. We will come back to it.

2:39The half-kilometre problem

But back to the moving seat. There is a problem. A very big one. Suppose the vehicle accelerates at one G for just ten seconds. That's roughly the acceleration of gravity. After ten seconds, the vehicle would be moving about three hundred and fifty kilometres per hour faster than before. To keep you stationary, your seat would have to move almost five hundred metres relative to the vehicle. So our first inertial damper would need a seat rail almost half a kilometre long. Probably not very practical for economy class. So let's try something else.

3:19Idea two: push the passenger

What if we could push the passenger directly? Sensors could measure the acceleration. A computer could work out the force required. And an actuator could push your body in exactly the right direction. That sounds much better. But there is still a fundamental problem. Where does that force come from? If you push the passenger forward, something else has to receive the corresponding reaction.

3:46Inertia itself

We haven't eliminated inertia — the resistance of matter to changing its motion. We are simply fighting it. And this is where the science fiction version gets really interesting. What if we could somehow change inertia itself?

Imagine a technology that could temporarily change how matter responds to acceleration. The vehicle could accelerate. But your body would not have to. That would be a real inertial damper. Not a better suspension system. Not a clever seat. Something fundamentally new. And we don't currently know how to do that.

4:28A force you have never felt

But now think about a force you have never felt. Gravity. Right now, gravity is pulling you towards the centre of the Earth. And you cannot feel it. What you feel is the chair under you. Take the chair away, and the feeling goes with it. In free fall, you feel nothing at all. Astronauts do not float because gravity is gone. They float because they are falling continuously. And here is why that matters so much.

4:59Why gravity is different

A seat pushes you in one place. That push then has to travel through your body. Your bones compress. Your organs shift. And that is what you actually feel. Gravity does not do that. It gives every part of you the same acceleration. Nothing is compressed. So there is nothing to feel. Einstein built general relativity on exactly that observation. A falling person feels no gravity. So the goal is not to cancel a force. The goal is to accelerate every part of you by the same amount. Which is precisely what gravity already does. And it is why the simulator could borrow it.

5:44Bending spacetime instead

So what if we could manipulate gravity itself? In Einstein's general relativity, gravity is not really a force. Matter and energy curve spacetime, and things move through that curve. So if we could control spacetime itself, we would not have to push the passenger at all. We would change the geometry the passenger moves through. From inside the vehicle, it would look almost magical. The ship accelerates at enormous speed. And inside, nothing is felt. This is roughly the kind of idea that science fiction calls an inertial dampener.

But there is a catch.

6:25What we cannot do

We can measure gravity with extraordinary precision. We can even detect gravitational waves from colliding black holes. But we have no technology that can turn it down. We don't even know whether gravity has a quantum particle at all. Physicists call it the graviton. Nobody has ever detected one.

6:49The verdict

So could we really build a science-fiction inertial damper? With today's physics: No.

But if we someday learn to manipulate inertia, gravity, or spacetime itself, the situation could change completely. Because then the problem would no longer be: "How do we make the vehicle accelerate more gently?" It would become: "How do we make the passengers not share that acceleration?"

7:16Why it matters for the stars

And that distinction could be enormous. Because a spacecraft that cannot hurt its passengers no longer has to crawl through space. You could keep accelerating. And accelerating. And accelerating. Until the spacecraft reaches a significant fraction of the speed of light. The hardest part of interstellar travel might not be reaching a high speed. It might be surviving the acceleration required to get there.

7:46The real question

So the inertial damper is more than science fiction. It is a question about one of the most basic properties of matter: Why does matter resist being accelerated in the first place? We know how to describe that behaviour. But we still don't have a technology that lets us switch it off. Maybe we never will.

Or maybe one day, the thing that sounds most impossible will turn out to be the missing technology that makes the stars reachable.
