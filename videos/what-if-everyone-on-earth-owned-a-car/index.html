What If Everyone on Earth Owned a Car?

9 min2026-10-22

So what would happen if every country reached the American level? This video works it out step by step: where eight billion cars would park, how many people one lane of a city street can actually move, what happened after Houston widened the Katy Freeway to more than twenty lanes, how many cars the world's factories would have to build every year, and how much petrol, or electricity, it would take to keep them all moving.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Eight billion cars

Imagine a world where every person on Earth owns a car. Not every family. Every person. Eight billion people. Eight billion cars. No waiting for a bus. No asking anyone for a lift. Your car is always there. It sounds like the ultimate freedom. A car is an excellent solution for one person. But what happens when everyone uses the same solution?

0:28Not as absurd as it sounds

This isn't as absurd as it sounds. In the United States, there are roughly eight vehicles for every ten people. That's more cars, SUVs and pickups than there are people with a driving licence. The rest of the world is a long way behind. There are more than one and a half billion vehicles on Earth. Roughly one for every five people. So imagine a world where every country reached the American level of motorization. That's about six and a half billion vehicles. Close enough to one each. So let's round it up. Eight billion cars.

1:06The first problem: parking

And let's say they all arrive tomorrow. The first problem isn't driving. It's parking. A parking space is about two and a half metres wide and five metres long. Twelve and a half square metres. Multiply that by eight billion, and you get a hundred thousand square kilometres. About the size of South Korea. But that's cars packed bumper to bumper. Nobody could get in or out. A real car park needs lanes to drive in and room to open the doors. That takes twenty-five to thirty-three square metres per space. Now we need somewhere between two and three South Koreas. Roughly the whole United Kingdom.

And that's just one space per car. But a car doesn't stay in one place. It needs a space at home, another at work, and another outside the shop. And in all of those places, it mostly waits. The average car is parked about ninety-five percent of the time. Those spaces can't be in the middle of nowhere. They have to be right next to people. Homes, schools, hospitals, shops. Exactly where land is already scarce. To make room, cities would have to give something up. Parks. Playgrounds. Housing.

2:32Monday morning

Now let's take the cars out of the car park. It's Monday morning, and everyone who has a car can drive it. One lane of a city street, filled with cars, moves somewhere between six hundred and sixteen hundred people an hour. Give the same lane to buses, and it can move up to eight thousand. Put trams on it, and up to twenty-five thousand. That's the geometry of the problem. A car is a wonderful way to move one person. It's a very bulky way to move a crowd.

3:05So we build more roads

So we build more roads. That's what Houston did. Its Katy Freeway was widened to more than twenty lanes, counting the side roads. It was finished in 2008, and it cost almost three billion dollars. And within a few years, the traffic came back. Between 2011 and 2014, the morning commute on it got thirty percent slower. The evening one, fifty-five percent. A wider road makes driving easier. So more people drive, until the new lanes fill up too. It's called induced demand. And every road ends somewhere. At a bridge. A junction. A city centre. You can't widen all of them.

3:50Not the only cost

And congestion isn't the only cost. The World Health Organization estimates that around one point two million people die in road crashes every year. We can't simply multiply that by the number of cars. It depends on how far people drive, how fast, on what roads, and in what cars. But a world with five times as many cars, and far more driving, would make road safety an even more important question.

4:20Someone has to build them

And we've skipped a step. Before anyone can park these cars, or drive them, someone has to build them. In 2024, the world built about ninety-three million vehicles. Cars, vans, trucks and buses, all together. In America, a typical car lasts around seventeen years. Ninety-three million a year, for seventeen years, is about one and a half billion. Which is roughly the number of vehicles the world has today. And the fleet is still growing. Keeping eight billion cars on the road would take about five times as many cars every year. And getting there in the first place would take even more.

5:03Fuelling eight billion cars

Then we need to fuel them. Let's be modest. Say each car drives ten thousand kilometres a year, less than a typical American or European car. And say it uses seven litres of petrol for every hundred kilometres. That's around ninety-six million barrels of petrol a day. The whole world today uses about a hundred and three. For everything. Planes, ships, trucks, heating, plastics.

5:31What if they were all electric?

So let's make every car electric. No petrol. No exhaust. Cleaner air in every city. The same ten thousand kilometres takes about two thousand kilowatt-hours of electricity a year. Times eight billion, that's about half of all the electricity the world generates today. And every one of those cars needs a battery. Just replacing them as the cars wear out would take around thirty times as many batteries as the world makes today. Some of this would get easier. Batteries improve. Clean power grows. Cars can charge at night.

But none of it makes space unlimited. An electric car is still a car. It still needs a road, and somewhere to park. We could change the engine. We could change the fuel. We could even change the person behind the wheel. But the geometry of the problem would stay the same.

6:31Who's going to drive them?

And there's one question we haven't asked yet. Who's actually going to drive all these cars? Almost three in ten people on Earth are children. Around two point four billion of them. In our world, every one of them owns a car. Then there are people who are too old to drive safely, who can't see, or who never learned. Even in the United States, almost three in ten people don't have a driving licence. Their cars would sit there. Or someone else would drive them. But if someone else is driving anyway, why does every passenger need a car of their own?

7:09Where the experiment falls apart

That's where the experiment falls apart. We wanted to give everyone freedom. But we confused freedom of movement with owning a particular machine. Freedom isn't the number of machines we own. It's the number of places we can actually reach.

The answer isn't to eliminate cars. Cars are essential for some journeys, especially in rural areas. The goal is to give people reliable ways to get where they need to go, without requiring everyone to own and drive the same machine.

7:45Back to our world

So let's go back to our world. Eight billion people. Eight billion cars. Car parks the size of a country, standing still most of the day. Roads that fill up as fast as we can widen them. Almost as much fuel as the whole world uses today, or half of today's electricity, just to keep them moving.

Not because cars are bad. The trouble starts when everyone uses the same solution, in the same places, at the same time. A car is a great answer for one person. It's a strange answer for eight billion.

So here's a question for you. If you could get anywhere you wanted, whenever you wanted, without owning a car, would you still buy one? Let me know in the comments.
