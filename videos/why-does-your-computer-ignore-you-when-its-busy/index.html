Why Does Your Computer Ignore You When It's Busy?

10 min2026-09-20

Here is the strange part: your computer knew. It knew within microseconds. Right now, doing nothing in particular, your machine is dealing with thousands of hardware events every second, and the mechanism that makes that possible has been there since the 1950s.

So why does the click sit there?

The full narration of the video.

0:00Click. Nothing happens.

You click a button. Nothing happens. You click it again. Still nothing. You move the mouse. Nothing. For a few seconds, your computer looks completely dead. And then everything happens at once. The window moves. The menu opens. Every click you made arrives, all together.

Here is the strange part. Your computer knew you clicked. It knew within microseconds. That is not a guess. Right now, with you doing nothing in particular, your machine is dealing with thousands of hardware events every second. A key going down. A packet arriving. A timer firing. A disk finishing a job. Each one of those is an interrupt, and computers have worked this way since the 1950s, for exactly this purpose: so that a processor could react to the world outside itself. So the hardware reported your click. The processor noticed. And you still sat there for five seconds staring at a dead screen.

Shouldn't the user come first? There is an answer, and it is not that your computer is too slow. It is that being noticed and being served are two completely different things.

1:18What an interrupt actually is

Let's start with the noticing. Imagine a computer without interrupts. The processor would have to ask, over and over: Did the keyboard receive anything? Did the disk finish? Did a packet arrive? Did the mouse move? And then ask again. And again. Almost every answer would be no, and the asking itself would eat the machine alive. So devices got a way to raise a hand. The mouse moves, the hardware raises an interrupt, and the processor drops whatever it was doing, jumps into a small piece of code called a handler, and the operating system finds out that something happened.

It is a brilliant idea. And it leads to a very reasonable assumption. If the processor can be pulled away from anything, instantly, then surely my click gets dealt with instantly too.

2:11The handler does as little as possible

Except the handler is not allowed to do anything much. While the processor is inside it, everything else is waiting. Including the next interrupt. And the next one is already coming. So the rule is the opposite of what you would expect. The handler does as little as it possibly can. It notes what happened. It tells the device that the message arrived. It drops the event into a queue. And then it gets out. Kernel engineers even split the work into two named halves: the part that must happen now, and the part that can happen later.

Everything interesting, everything you would ever see, is in the second half.

2:52First answer: attention is not a processor

Which gives us the first answer. An interrupt gets the computer's attention. It does not get your program onto a processor.

3:04So give input the highest priority

Fine, you might say. Then fix that. Make input the most important thing in the building. Mouse? Highest priority. Keyboard? Highest priority. The window in front of you? Highest priority. Everything else can wait its turn. It sounds obviously right.

3:26Mars, 1997

And the best argument against it happened on Mars. In 1997, the Pathfinder lander touched down and started working. Then, days later, it began resetting itself. Not a cosmic ray. Not a broken part. A scheduling problem. There was a high priority task that moved data around the spacecraft, and it needed a shared piece of memory. A low priority task collecting weather data had got there first and was holding it. Which would have been fine for a moment. But a medium priority task then took the processor, and the low priority one never got a chance to finish. So it never let go.

The most important task on the spacecraft was now stuck behind the least important one.

4:15Priority inversion

It has a name. Priority inversion.

A watchdog timer noticed that the important task had not run for far too long, assumed disaster, and reset the entire spacecraft. Then it happened again. And again. Engineers on Earth worked out why, and sent a fix up to Mars.

4:37High priority does not mean you get served

And the lesson costs nothing to carry home. High priority does not mean you get served. It only means you get asked first. If the thing you need is held by somebody slower, your importance buys you nothing.

4:53Priority does not create cycles

And priority has a second limit. It decides who goes first. It does not create a single extra cycle. Suppose every core on your machine is at one hundred percent. You might think that means frozen. It usually doesn't. The scheduler hands out slices of time so quickly that you cannot feel the joins. That is why you can compile something enormous and still scroll a web page while it runs. What changes under load is not really speed. The computer is not slow.

5:28Not slow. Late.

It is late.

You click, and the answer comes half a second later. Then a second. Then five. And people are extraordinarily good at noticing that. Half a second feels like a machine that is thinking. Five feels like a machine that has died.

5:50When the processor is not the problem

And there is a version of this that surprises people most. Sometimes the processor is not the problem at all. Open too many things at once and physical memory fills up. The operating system starts shuffling pages of memory out to storage. Do enough of that and the machine spends its time moving memory around instead of doing your work. It has a name too. Thrashing. And this is the confusing one, because the processor might not look busy at all. It is not working. It is waiting. You can hand a waiting task the highest priority on the system and it will wait at exactly the same speed.

6:33The application that will not answer

And then there is the most human version of this. The event arrived. The kernel has it. It is sitting in a queue with your program's name on it. And your program is not picking it up. Maybe it is doing a long calculation on the same thread that is supposed to be watching its own window. So the mouse works. The keyboard works. Other programs work. And that one window turns pale and says it is not responding. Which is not a diagnosis. Windows puts that label on a window after about five seconds of nobody collecting its messages.

The system is perfectly alive. The application simply has not come to the door. The computer receiving your click and the application answering it are not the same event.

7:31And nothing has reached the screen yet

There is one more link, and it is the one we never think about. Nothing so far has actually put anything on your screen. The application changes its state. Something has to build the next frame. The graphics card has to draw it. The display has to show it. On an ordinary screen a new picture appears about every sixteen milliseconds. So there is a difference between the computer dealing with your click and you seeing that it did.

8:03It has no idea why you moved the mouse

And underneath all of it sits the part that no amount of engineering fixes. The operating system knows that your mouse moved. It has no idea why. You might be aiming at something in a game. Or editing a document. Or you might have knocked the desk. To the hardware, those are the same event. A system can rank kinds of work. It cannot guess what you meant. So it compromises. Fast enough for you, most of the time, without starving everything else.

8:37Your click was never one event

Which brings us back to that dead screen. Your click was never one event. The hardware has to report it. The kernel has to store it. The queue has to reach your program. Your program has to run. The interface has to change. Something has to draw the new picture, and the screen has to show it to you. Every one of those steps is small and fast. Delay any single one of them and you don't experience a slow window. You experience a dead computer.

And the strangest thing about the whole business is how rarely it happens. We expect a machine running billions of operations a second to answer a twitch of the hand. And almost always, it does. So the next time you click and nothing happens, give it a moment. It heard you.

It just had a queue, and you were in it.
