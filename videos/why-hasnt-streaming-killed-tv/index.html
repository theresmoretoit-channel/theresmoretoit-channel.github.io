Why Hasn't Streaming Killed TV?

9 min2026-10-07

And yet in May 2025, after nearly twenty years of Netflix and YouTube, streaming only just overtook broadcast and cable in the United States: 44.8 percent of TV viewing against 44.2.

So why hasn't streaming killed it? What does a transmitter do that the Internet finds surprisingly hard? Are television and broadcasting even the same thing? And when would you actually want everyone to see the same thing at the same time?

The full narration of the video.

0:00An evening of linear TV

Imagine it's 2026. You turn on the television. It's half past seven in the evening. First, a few minutes of commercials. Then the news. Then more commercials. Then the weather. Finally, at eight o'clock, the film you actually wanted to see begins. It's two hours long. Except… commercial break. Another commercial break. And another one. A two-hour film now finishes at quarter to eleven. And you have to get up at six. So somewhere around ten, you give up. You never find out how it ends. And that's not even the weirdest part. You didn't choose the film. You didn't choose when it started. You can't pause it. You can't rewind it.

And if you miss the beginning? Too bad. Wait until somebody decides to show it again.

0:52Of course, you could record it

Of course, you could have it recorded. People have done that for decades. First with a VCR and a timer, today with a set-top box. Then you fast-forward through the ads. But then you're no longer watching it on the broadcaster's schedule. You've turned it into something you can watch the way you want. The broadcast itself is still linear television. And honestly… who would choose to watch like this in 2026?

1:21Streaming overtook TV. Barely.

Well… millions of people still do. In the United States, in May 2025, streaming finally overtook broadcast and cable combined. 44.8 percent of TV viewing against 44.2. Overtook. Barely. After nearly twenty years of Netflix, YouTube and everything else, the old way of watching still holds almost half of TV viewing. So why hasn't streaming killed it? The answer has less to do with television than you might think.

2:01One transmitter, millions of receivers

Start with the basic idea. A broadcaster has a transmitter. The transmitter sends out a signal. And every television in the area can pick it up. One transmission. Millions of receivers. The broadcaster doesn't have to know who is watching, or even how many. It's an incredibly efficient way to distribute information. But it has one unfortunate consequence. Everybody gets the same thing, at the same time. Broadcasting doesn't ask every viewer what they want. It asks one question: what should everyone receive? The broadcaster decides what's on, and when it starts. The viewer decides only one thing: whether to watch.

For most of television's history, that wasn't really a problem. There wasn't much of an alternative.

2:53Then the Internet happened

Then the Internet happened. Instead of "here is what we're transmitting, take it or leave it", you get "what do you want to watch?" You choose the programme. You choose when. Pause it. Rewind it. Pick the audio language. Turn on subtitles. Stop halfway and come back tomorrow. And this isn't really about commercials. It's about the direction of control. Broadcasting pushes content towards you. Streaming lets you pull it towards yourself. So for a film on a Tuesday night, streaming simply wins. But now change one thing.

3:30The World Cup final

Instead of a random film, it's the World Cup final. Ten million people want to see it. And they all want to see it right now. For a transmitter, that's no harder than one viewer. It sends the same signal either way. Ten million receivers don't cost ten million times as much. That's the whole trick of broadcasting. One to many.

3:56Why the Internet works differently

The Internet works differently. The network has to deliver the stream to a huge number of viewers, using servers, caches and network capacity all the way to their screens. And every extra viewer needs a stream of their own. Engineers have become remarkably good at this. But the underlying problem doesn't go away. A huge number of people are asking the network for the same thing at the same moment. And when it's happening right now, nobody can download it in advance. Sport. Breaking news. Election night. Things millions of people want at the same moment.

And that's exactly what broadcasting is almost ridiculously good at.

4:41Television isn't broadcasting

Which suggests we've been mixing up two different things. Television is a medium. Broadcasting is a way of delivering it. A television channel doesn't need an antenna on the roof anymore. It can come by cable. By satellite. Over the Internet. In Europe in 2024, more than half of pay-TV subscriptions already came over broadband. But the old-fashioned aerial was still in use in about two homes in five. So the old way is declining. But it's hardly gone. And streaming didn't even get rid of commercials. Many services now offer cheaper plans with advertising. Apparently, even the Internet couldn't escape that.

But traditional television has one more trick. And it sounds strange after everything we've just said.

5:34Sometimes people don't want to choose

Sometimes people don't want to choose. You sit down on the sofa. You're tired. You don't know what you want. And you have thousands of options. You scroll. And scroll. Twenty minutes later, you're still choosing. Traditional television is different. You turn it on, and something is already playing. Somebody else has chosen for you. Nobody says radio is broken because you can't pick every song. That's not what radio is for. Linear television can work the same way. It's not interactive. It's not personalised. And that's precisely the point.

6:15When the Internet goes down

And there's one more situation where broadcasting is hard to beat. Imagine a major Internet outage. Or a natural disaster. Or mobile networks so overloaded that nothing gets through. A transmitter with backup power doesn't need every viewer to have a fast Internet connection. If you can receive the signal, you can receive the information. Even a battery radio will do. That's one reason many governments and public broadcasters still keep the transmitters running. Broadcasting isn't just an old way of watching sitcoms. It's a remarkably robust way of reaching an entire country.

6:58So, has streaming killed television?

So, has streaming killed television? For watching a film whenever you like? Pretty much. Why wait until eight o'clock? Why sit through the interruptions? Why not watch the rest tomorrow? But for sending the same thing to millions of people at once, broadcasting suddenly looks much less obsolete. In fact, it looks almost elegant. One transmitter. One signal. Millions of receivers.

We often assume old technology disappears when something newer comes along. But radio didn't disappear because of podcasts. Books didn't disappear because of e-books. The old technology moves into a different role. The one it does best.

So the real question isn't whether television is dead. It's whether we need everyone to see the same thing at the same time. For a two-hour film? Probably not. For the World Cup final? Maybe. For an emergency? Absolutely. And that's why, even in 2026, you can still turn on a television and find the same old thing waiting. A signal. A schedule. A bunch of commercials. And millions of other people watching exactly the same thing. Not because we haven't invented anything better. But because sometimes, sending one thing to everyone is still very hard to beat.
