How Can a Floating Bridge Carry a Tank?

7 min2026-10-13

From Archimedes and a block of displaced water to ribbon bridges, amphibious rigs, the floating motorway near Seattle and the failed Russian crossing of the Siverskyi Donets at Bilohorivka in May 2022: why buoyancy is the easy part of a military bridge.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00Sixty tonnes on floating boxes

A main battle tank weighs more than sixty tonnes. And here it is, crossing a river on a bridge made of floating metal boxes. No pillars. No foundations in the riverbed. Just sections resting on the water, locked together, with a road on top. It looks like something that should work perfectly well… right up until the tank arrives. So how does floating metal carry sixty tonnes?

0:26Sixty cubic metres of water

The tank adds sixty tonnes of weight. So the bridge must displace sixty tonnes of extra water. In a river, that's sixty cubic metres. A block of water ten metres long, six metres wide, and one metre deep. That sounds like a lot. But a bridge is long. And that block isn't pushed aside by one box. It's shared between all the sections the tank's weight reaches. The tank drives on. The pontoons settle lower. They push aside more water, and the water pushes back harder. No pumps. No computer. The bridge finds its new balance all on its own.

It's the same rule that keeps a steel ship afloat, and Archimedes wrote it down more than two thousand years ago. Near Seattle, a motorway crosses Lake Washington on seventy-seven concrete pontoons, more than two kilometres long. Same physics. But this bridge is designed to last for decades.

1:26The hard part

So floating is the easy part. Here's the hard part. The British Army says eight of its amphibious bridging vehicles can build a bridge a hundred metres long in about thirty minutes. If an army can build a bridge in thirty minutes… why can't the enemy simply destroy it?

1:53Surviving the tank itself

We'll get there. But before the enemy gets a say, the bridge has to survive the tank itself. Put a heavy truck on a narrow boat, and the boat may float just fine. It just floats on its side. A bridge section is wide and flat. When one edge dips, that edge pushes aside more water, and the extra lift pushes it back up. And the sections are locked together. The tank isn't standing on one box. It's standing on a long strip that shares its weight along the bridge. That's why one common design is called a ribbon bridge. But a ribbon bends. The joints flex, the deck dips under every axle, and the whole thing rides the waves. Engineers don't try to stop that. They keep the movement within limits.

And here's a catch. A tank and a truck can weigh exactly the same, and still not be equally safe to cross. A tank spreads its weight along two long tracks. A truck puts all of it onto a few axles. Military bridges also use a system called Military Load Classification. Because a vehicle's weight alone doesn't tell you how it loads a bridge.

3:05The river pushes back

And then there's the river. The current pushes on every pontoon, so the bridge has to be held in place, with anchors, cables, or small boats pushing upstream. A floating bridge can have more than enough buoyancy… and still fail, because the river pushed it sideways.

3:22Why armies rely on it

Which brings us back to the question. Why would an army rely on something this delicate?

Because a river stops an army better than almost anything else. And a retreating defender will often blow up the bridges behind it. So the attacker brings their own. Some bridges travel folded on trucks. Each section slides into the water and unfolds on its own. Others are vehicles that become the bridge. They drive into the river, swing out their floats, and lock onto the next one. In 2016, British and German crews linked thirty of them into a bridge three hundred and fifty metres long across the Vistula, in Poland.

4:06Thirty minutes is not the operation

But thirty minutes is the assembly. Not the operation. The equipment has to reach the river. The banks need a way on and off. And everything that wants to cross has to wait its turn. That waiting is the real weakness.

4:24Bilohorivka, May 2022

In May 2022, Russian forces tried to cross the Siverskyi Donets river near the village of Bilohorivka, in eastern Ukraine. The crossing site was not a surprise. Ukrainian commanders had expected an attempt in this area. Russian forces tried to hide their preparations with smoke and burning trees. But Ukrainian reconnaissance confirmed the bridge was going up. Artillery opened fire. Airstrikes followed. Images later showed three destroyed pontoon bridges and dozens of wrecked military vehicles. British defence intelligence assessed that Russia had lost significant armoured manoeuvre elements of at least one battalion tactical group.

5:11What actually failed

Notice what failed. Not the buoyancy. The pontoons could float. But that wasn't enough to make the crossing succeed. The crossing couldn't be protected. And a bridge tells the enemy exactly where you're going to be. Every vehicle has to pass through one narrow point… and wait its turn to do it. A pontoon bridge isn't just a road. It's a target with a queue in front of it.

5:39Destroyed is not stopped

So yes, the enemy can destroy it. And often does. But destroying a bridge once isn't the same as stopping the crossing. A damaged section can sometimes be swapped out. Engineers can repair the crossing, or build a new one somewhere else. In some systems, the same modules can be reconfigured as rafts, ferrying vehicles across one at a time. So the crossing becomes a race. One side tries to cut the flow of fuel, ammunition and reinforcements. The other tries to restore it faster. Engineers may use darkness and smoke, or set up alternative crossing sites. But none of that guarantees safety.

Speed is one of its few protections. But only until the enemy finds it.

6:29Staying alive long enough

So floating was never the hard part. The hard part is staying alive long enough to matter. And that race keeps getting faster. If the enemy can find a new bridge within minutes, half an hour to build one starts to look slow. The bridge floats. The tank rolls. And for a brief moment, a river becomes a road.
