Linux — You Probably Use It Already

10 min2026-09-08

It started in 1991 as one student's hobby project. Today it's in your phone, your router, your television, the servers behind most of the web, all five hundred of the world's most powerful supercomputers — and on the flight computer of a helicopter that flew on Mars.

Linux isn't really one operating system. It's a kernel: a building block you can wrap almost anything around. That's why it turns up in places that seem completely unrelated — and why you've almost certainly used it without ever choosing to.

0:00The stereotype

Have you ever used Linux? If you're a programmer, maybe the answer is obviously yes. But what if you're not? Maybe you use Windows. Maybe you have an iPhone. Maybe you've never even seen Linux. And if someone asked you what Linux is, you might imagine something like this. A black terminal. Lots of text. Someone typing commands. Linux has a reputation for being complicated, weird, and mostly useful to programmers, system administrators, and people who apparently enjoy making their computers harder to use. But here's the interesting question.

Where does Linux actually run? You might be surprised by the answer.

0:481991: a hobby project

And to understand why, we need to go back to nineteen ninety-one. In nineteen ninety-one, a twenty-one-year-old Finnish student named Linus Torvalds started working on a small operating-system kernel. It wasn't supposed to become a revolution. It was basically a hobby project. Linus wanted to create a Unix-like system for his computer, and he released the source code so other people could experiment with it. More and more programmers started contributing. People improved it, fixed bugs, added features, and adapted it to different hardware.

And something rather unusual happened. A project started by one student became a global collaboration. Decades later, Linux is developed by thousands of people, and supported by companies, universities, independent developers and volunteers around the world. The most recent release of the kernel took about nine weeks, and it brought in the work of two thousand six hundred and fifty-two developers. It has become one of the largest collaborative software projects humanity has ever created.

2:00What Linux actually is

And remember that question? Where does Linux actually run? Well, the answer starts with understanding what Linux actually is. And here's the really important part. Linux isn't really one operating system. Linux is a kernel. Think of the kernel as the part of the system that sits between software and hardware. It manages the processors, the memory and the devices. Around that kernel, you can build very different systems. A desktop computer. A server. A tiny embedded device. A supercomputer. And things that don't look like computers at all.

Which brings us back to our original question. Where is Linux actually running? And now we can finally answer it. Let's start with something you probably have right now.

2:54Your phone

Your phone. If you're using Android, you're already using the Linux kernel. And there are more than three billion active Android devices, in over a hundred and ninety countries. Android isn't simply Linux in the same sense as Ubuntu or Fedora. But underneath Android is the Linux kernel. So that little computer in your pocket? Linux.

And that's just the beginning.

3:28Your router

Look around your home. There's probably another computer nearby that you don't think of as a computer at all. Your Wi-Fi router. That little plastic box connecting your devices to the Internet can contain a surprisingly powerful computer. And many routers run Linux. You don't see the terminal. You don't even know it's there. You just connect to Wi-Fi. But underneath the interface… Linux.

What about your television? Modern smart televisions aren't really televisions anymore. They're computers with a screen attached.

3:59Your television

And Linux is used in many smart televisions and other consumer electronics. So you can sit on your couch, turn on Netflix, and never realise that Linux may be involved in several different places between your remote control and the film you're watching.

But now let's make the jump much bigger.

4:26The internet

The Internet. When you open a website, you're usually not connecting to some magical place called the cloud. You're talking to computers. Lots of computers. Servers in data centres. And Linux has become enormously important in server infrastructure. Web servers. Databases. Containers. So when you browse the web, there's a good chance you're interacting with Linux without ever seeing it. And this is where things start getting strange. Linux isn't just sitting inside a few devices. It is part of the infrastructure underneath much of the modern Internet.

5:11Supercomputers

Now let's go to the opposite end of the scale. Supercomputers. These are machines built to perform enormous amounts of computation. Weather simulations. Astronomy. Climate modelling. Twice a year, someone publishes a list of the five hundred most powerful supercomputers in the world. All five hundred of them run Linux. And that has been true on every single list since two thousand and seventeen.

Think about that contrast. The same kernel that can be running inside a tiny embedded computer can also be running on machines containing enormous numbers of processors. A hobby project started by a Finnish student ended up powering some of the most powerful computers humanity has ever built.

6:03Things that move

And Linux doesn't only live in buildings. It can be found in things that move. Cars run Linux in various systems, particularly infotainment and connected-car platforms. It's used in industrial equipment. Robotics. Drones. Cameras. Storage devices. Network equipment. And countless other embedded systems. The important thing is that you often don't know it's there. Because Linux doesn't have to look like a computer at all. There doesn't have to be a desktop. There doesn't have to be a terminal. There doesn't even have to be a screen.

It can just quietly sit there, running the software that makes a piece of hardware work.

6:49Space — and Mars

And now let's leave Earth. Linux has even made it into space. It has been used on spacecraft and space-related systems. On the International Space Station, the laptops the crew works on were moved to Linux back in twenty thirteen. And perhaps the most entertaining example is Mars. NASA's Ingenuity helicopter, which operated on Mars, used a Linux-based flight computer. It was designed to make five test flights. It made seventy-two.

Think about how bizarre that is. A piece of software that started as a hobby project on Earth ended up running on another planet. So, technically… Linux has run on Earth. And… Linux has run on Mars. Which gives Linux a rather unusual statistic. The number of planets it has run on. Two. Earth and Mars. Not bad for a hobby project.

7:55So what is Linux?

At this point, it might be tempting to say: Okay, so Linux is just an operating system that's everywhere. But that's not quite right. Linux is better thought of as a piece of infrastructure. A building block. That's why Linux can appear in places that seem completely unrelated. And probably dozens of devices around you that you never thought contained an operating system at all.

8:24Do you use Linux?

So… do you use Linux? Maybe you don't have Linux installed on your computer. Maybe you've never opened a Linux terminal. Maybe you don't even like Linux. But there's a pretty good chance you've already used it. Possibly today. Possibly dozens of times. And that's what makes Linux so unusual. It started as a small project by a university student in Finland. And somehow, it became part of the infrastructure of the modern world. From smartphones to supercomputers. From Earth… to Mars.

And perhaps that's the strangest thing about Linux. You don't have to choose to use it. Sometimes, you're already using it.
