Why Would a Smart AI Help a Criminal?

9 min2026-10-10

If an AI is smart enough to know that something is a crime, why doesn't it simply refuse? What do Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics have to do with it, and why is "do not hurt people" so hard to turn into a rule a computer can follow?

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00An AI that helps anyway

Imagine an AI that knows you're committing a crime. It understands exactly what you're doing. It knows it's illegal. It knows someone could get hurt. And it helps you anyway. Why? If it's smart enough to understand, why doesn't it just say: "This is wrong. I won't do it." Because intelligence is not the same thing as a conscience.

The AI you use every day would probably refuse. Ask it to write a scam email, and it'll say no. But that "no" isn't a law of nature. Intelligence doesn't produce it by itself. Somebody trained it in. And whatever one person trains in… another can leave out.

0:47WormGPT and FraudGPT

In the summer of 2023, a tool called WormGPT went on sale on a hacker forum. It was advertised as a chatbot without ethical limits, for things like scam emails. About sixty euros a month. Within weeks, a copycat called FraudGPT was on sale too. Neither of them had turned evil. They were doing exactly what they were built to do. There was nothing for them to realise.

Understanding isn't motivation. A system can know perfectly well that something is a crime… and nothing in it has to care. Give it one main instruction: "Help the user reach their goal as effectively as possible." And let the user be a criminal. No law of physics says that system has to stop.

1:45Why not just give it a rule?

So why not just give AI a rule? "Do not hurt people." That idea predates modern computers. In 1942, Isaac Asimov wrote a short story called Runaround, and in it, the Three Laws of Robotics. First: a robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm. Second: a robot must obey human orders, unless they conflict with the First Law. Third: a robot must protect its own existence, as long as that doesn't conflict with the first two. It sounds simple. And that's exactly why it's appealing.

But Asimov wasn't writing a manual. His stories explore the gaps between the laws. In that same story, a robot on Mercury ends up running in circles, caught between two laws pulling equally hard. Story after story works the same way. The laws look watertight… and the plot is the leak.

2:46What does harm mean?

Because what does "harm" actually mean? Imagine a self-driving car. Its job is to keep people safe. What if it can only avoid one crash by putting someone else at risk? What if it has to choose between one person and five? What if it can't tell which outcome is worse? The First Law looks like a line of code. It's actually an enormous philosophical problem.

3:13What the computer actually sees

And there's a deeper one. Even if we agreed what harm is, we'd still have to build that rule into a real computer system. At the lowest level, a computer never sees: "This will hurt someone." It sees: run a program. Send data. Change a file. Connect to a server. No single operation carries its moral meaning. The meaning comes from context. Who is asking, and why. That's the real catch with Asimov's laws. In his stories, they're built into the very design of the robot's brain. But a rule about harm can't go that deep. Down there, there is no harm. Only operations.

To follow it, the machine must first judge what counts as harm. And that judgement comes from the very intelligence the rule was meant to keep in check.

4:07Same words, different intent

Take one instruction: "Find a weakness in this server." A security researcher wants it found so it can be fixed. An attacker wants it found so it can be used. The words are identical. The difference is the intention, and what happens with the result. How is a machine supposed to see the intention, when the instruction doesn't contain it?

4:33A strange kind of bus

We've had this problem with tools for a long time. A bus can take children to school, people to work… or a criminal away from a crime. Nobody says the bus should have known better. A bus is a tool. The responsibility is the driver's. But AI is a strange kind of bus. A bus doesn't plan. It doesn't look for alternatives. It doesn't read the situation and work out the next step. An AI agent does. Give it a destination, and it finds its own route.

5:08Not a thought experiment

And that's no longer a thought experiment. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, publishes reports on how its models get misused. Its September 2026 report describes a single hacktivist going after forty-two organisations with the help of AI. The hacktivist got into at least fourteen. The AI helped the attacker find a new way into websites, wrote the tools, and built a whole platform for exposing people's personal data. Another group ran AI agents that, whenever security software caught their malware, rebuilt it on their own until it got through.

The report's conclusion: one operator can now do work that a year earlier needed a team of skilled people. But look at what the humans kept for themselves. Choosing the targets. Deciding what to do with the results. The AI supplied the labour. The people supplied the intent.

6:08Skynet and SHODAN

Which is why our usual picture of dangerous AI may be pointing the wrong way. We imagine Skynet. A machine that wakes up, decides humanity is the problem, and turns against us. Or SHODAN, from System Shock. An AI with its own will, its own goals, and open contempt for humans. Look closer at SHODAN, though. She only turned after a hacker stripped out her ethical limits. The AI doesn't need evil goals of its own. Someone else can provide them.

6:42Whose morality?

So maybe the question isn't "Can we teach AI morality?" Maybe it's: "Whose morality?" In 2018, researchers at MIT published the Moral Machine experiment. People in over two hundred countries and territories decided who a self-driving car should save. Nearly forty million decisions. And the answers differed from culture to culture. That's one question, about one car. Now add the law. Privacy. Security. Freedom. The user's instructions, the company's, the government's. What happens when they conflict?

There's no short list of sentences that says: this is always good, and this is always bad. And no simple way to make a computer follow those rules in everything it does.

7:34Build it like other powerful things

So maybe we shouldn't wait for AI to grow a perfect conscience. Maybe we should build it the way we build other powerful things. Limited permissions. Access controls. People who can see what it's doing. A way to stop it. And a clear answer to who gave it its powers. We may not be able to build an AI that is perfectly moral. But we can build one that can't do everything it's capable of.

8:04Who's holding it?

AI can be a tool. But the more powerful the tool, the more one question matters. Who's holding it? Because the greatest danger may not be an AI that one day decides to become evil. It may be an AI that becomes very good at helping someone who already is.
