Physics That Sounds Simple Until You Ask

Can We Reach Another Star With Today's Technology?

11 min2026-09-09

Sort of. At Voyager 1's speed the trip takes about seventy-five thousand years, and the problem stops being the distance. It becomes whether a machine can still be working when it arrives. Everything humanity has ever kept running — the Voyagers, at forty-nine years and counting — is an invisible sliver of that journey. A plutonium-238 generator is down to less than a thousandth of its output after a thousand years, and a thousand years is barely one percent of the way.

Go faster and the problems change rather than disappear. A hundred- gigawatt laser could push a gram-scale sail to a fifth of light speed and cross the gap in twenty years — but nothing waits at the far end to slow it down, the biggest dust grain out there arrives with the energy of a rifle bullet, and the probe has to navigate itself because Earth is four years away by radio.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The nearest star

There are a few hundred billion stars in our galaxy. And one of them is our nearest neighbour. Proxima Centauri. It's about four and a quarter light-years away. Which sounds close. Until you realise that, with the spacecraft we have today, getting there would take tens of thousands of years. But here's the interesting part. We actually know how to send a spacecraft out of the Solar System and put it on a course for Proxima Centauri. We have the rockets. We have the computers. We have the navigation technology. We even have spacecraft already flying through interstellar space.

So… could we actually send a spacecraft to another star with today's technology? Well… sort of. Because once you look at what it would have to do… things get ridiculous very quickly.

0:56How fast can we go?

Let's start with the obvious problem. Distance. Four point two four light-years. That's roughly forty trillion kilometres. Our fastest spacecraft aren't even remotely close to the speed of light. Voyager one, the fastest thing we have ever sent out of the Solar System, is travelling at roughly seventeen kilometres per second. That sounds fast. It's not. At that speed, reaching Proxima Centauri would take roughly seventy-five thousand years. Seventy-five thousand years. The first pyramids were built only about five thousand years ago.

1:35Will it still work?

So this isn't a long mission. It's sending a machine into space and hoping it still works when it finally gets there. And that is a completely different problem.

Think about the machines around you. Your phone. Your car. How many of them do you expect to work perfectly in the year ten thousand? Probably none. And we're asking a spacecraft to survive not ten thousand years… but seventy-five thousand. The Voyager probes are among the longest-lived machines we have ever built. They have been running for decades. And they're already having systems switched off to conserve their remaining power. An interstellar probe would have to survive radiation, extreme temperatures, and thousands of other little things that can go wrong.

And there is one particularly nasty problem… you can't repair it. There is no engineer who can travel there and reboot the computer. The spacecraft has to be designed to repair itself… or survive without repairs.

2:43The computer

And then there's the computer. Not something that stores data for seventy-five thousand years. Something that actually operates. The processor in your phone is unimaginably more powerful than computers from a few decades ago. At some point we're asking engineering to deal with a timescale for which we have essentially no practical experience.

3:08Running out of power

And then there's energy. For decades, radioisotope generators have been extremely useful for deep-space missions. They run on plutonium two three eight, which loses half its output every eighty-eight years. After a thousand years, a generator like that is putting out less than a thousandth of what it started with. And a thousand years is just over one percent of the journey. That's not a small engineering problem. That's the mission. You would need a power source that keeps working for an absurdly long time… or a spacecraft that can shut itself down for thousands of years and wake up later.

And now we've turned our spacecraft into something that looks less like a satellite… and more like a technological time capsule.

4:00The laser idea

Of course, we don't have to use Voyager's speed. There are several ways we could make a much faster spacecraft. And there is one particularly crazy idea… laser propulsion. Instead of carrying the energy on board, we could put a huge laser array on Earth and shine an extremely powerful beam at a tiny reflective sail. No giant engine. The laser does the pushing. Concepts such as Breakthrough Starshot have proposed accelerating extremely small spacecraft to twenty percent of the speed of light. The laser they have in mind would draw a hundred gigawatts. That is nearly three times the entire electricity demand of the United Kingdom.

At that speed, a probe could reach the Proxima Centauri system in roughly twenty years. Twenty years instead of seventy-five thousand. But there's a catch. Actually… there are several.

4:59You can't slow down

Your tiny spacecraft crosses interstellar space for twenty years. And then… what? You might think… "Easy. Just slow it down when it gets there." No. That's one of the biggest problems. Slowing it down means getting rid of all the energy the laser put in. And there is no giant laser waiting for you at Proxima. So the simplest version of a Starshot-like mission is not an expedition that arrives at Proxima. It's a flyby. The spacecraft screams through the system at a significant fraction of the speed of light… takes a few pictures… collects whatever data it can… and disappears into interstellar space.

There is no second attempt. There is no U-turn. And there is no parking orbit.

5:50Dust at a fifth of light speed

And at that speed, space stops being empty. There is dust. There are tiny particles. There is radiation. At ordinary spacecraft speeds, hitting a tiny particle may cause some damage. The biggest specks out there weigh about a billionth of a gram. At a fifth of the speed of light, one of those arrives with the energy of a rifle bullet. So it may need shielding. But shielding has mass. And mass is exactly what we don't want, because every extra gram makes acceleration harder.

6:24Navigating alone

And then there is something that is easy to overlook. Navigation. Proxima is moving. So is the Sun. So is everything else in the galaxy. But that part is manageable. Astronomers are very good at predicting stellar motion. At twenty percent of the speed of light, even a tiny error in the trajectory can become enormous over four light-years. And the spacecraft can't ask Earth for directions. Because the signal takes more than four years to cross the distance. By the time we discover that something went wrong… the spacecraft may already be millions or billions of kilometres away from where we wanted it to be.

So it has to navigate itself. We already use autonomous systems in spacecraft, but not like this. It would have to find its own orientation from the stars, and decide for itself whether a measurement is real or a sensor has failed. For a probe travelling to Proxima, Earth is not mission control. Earth is a very distant observer.

7:31Getting the data home

Let's assume we've somehow solved all of this. The spacecraft survives. It reaches the Proxima Centauri system. It takes its pictures. And then it is past the star, falling away into the dark with the only copy of everything it saw. Now it has to send that home. A tiny spacecraft has a tiny transmitter, and it is trying to reach us across four light-years of space. It's like trying to shout across an ocean… while both of you are moving… and you have to hit a target smaller than a coin… from four light-years away. And a complete conversation with it takes more than eight years.

And then there's the data rate. Getting a tiny amount of information home might be possible. Getting a beautiful high-resolution movie of Proxima's planets? That's another matter.

8:26So can we?

So… can we reach another star with today's technology? If by "reach" you mean… send a piece of hardware onto a trajectory that eventually crosses the Proxima Centauri system? Then… probably yes. We already have spacecraft on their way out. But if by "reach" you mean… send a useful scientific spacecraft to Proxima and have it arrive working? Then the answer becomes much less comfortable. And if you mean… send it there today, have it work on its own for tens of thousands of years, and get the data back? Then we're no longer talking about something we know how to build.

9:08The real problem is time

And that's what makes interstellar travel so fascinating. The hardest part may not be propulsion. It may not even be the distance. It may be time.

We know how to build machines that work for years. We've built machines that work for decades. But an interstellar spacecraft may need to survive for centuries… or, with today's relatively slow propulsion, tens of thousands of years. That's a completely different engineering problem. And perhaps the first truly successful interstellar spacecraft won't be the fastest one. It might be the one designed around a different idea… don't make it last forever.

9:48Make it sleep

Make it sleep. Let it travel. Let it wake up. Check itself. Repair itself. Navigate. Do the science. And then call home.

So perhaps the question isn't… "Can we get to Proxima Centauri?" We probably can. The real question is… "Can we build a machine today that still knows what it's supposed to do when it gets there?" Because reaching another star isn't just a problem of distance. It's a problem of speed. Energy. Navigation. Communication. Reliability. Autonomy. And above all… time.

And when you're talking about another star… four light-years suddenly doesn't seem quite so close anymore.
