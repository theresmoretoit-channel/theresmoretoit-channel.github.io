Why Do Trans People Change Their Bodies?

12 min2026-10-23

This video looks at what medical transition actually involves and why some trans people pursue it. What hormone therapy can and cannot change. What surgery costs, in recovery, risk and money. What gender dysphoria is, and why it is so hard to understand from the outside when you don't feel it yourself. Why the idea that it's just a whim or a trend struggles to explain why anyone would accept all of that. And why some trans people choose not to transition medically at all.

This is the third video in a series about transgender people. The first asked why there suddenly seem to be so many trans people, the second how you know you're trans. The next one asks: why is it so hard to be trans?

The full narration of the video.

0:00Why would anyone choose that?

Imagine deciding to undergo surgery. Not because of an injury. Not because of an illness that shows up on a scan. But because something about your own body feels so profoundly wrong that changing it seems worth months of recovery, significant risks, and consequences that may last for the rest of your life. Imagine taking medication for years, knowing that it will change your body in ways you cannot completely predict. Imagine accepting that some changes may be permanent, that complications are possible, and that the results may never be exactly what you hoped for.

Now ask yourself a question. Why would anyone choose that? Why would someone accept all of those risks and difficulties when doing nothing might seem easier from the outside? For some people, not doing anything may be even harder. Their distress can become difficult to live with, and medical care may offer a way to reduce it.

For some transgender people, medical transition is not about looking fashionable. It is not about following a trend. And it is not a decision they make without thinking about the consequences. It is an attempt to bring their bodies closer to how they experience themselves.

To understand why someone would accept so much, we first need to understand what transition actually means. Because changing your body is only one part of a much larger picture.

1:33Transition is not one thing

Transition is not one thing. Transition can be social, legal, or medical. These three things are related, but they are not interchangeable. And there is no universal checklist that every transgender person must complete. For some, social changes are enough. For others, the body itself remains a source of distress. And that brings us to hormones.

1:59What hormones actually do

What hormones actually do. Hormones are chemical messengers. Medical hormone therapy uses their effects to change certain characteristics in a direction that better matches a person's goals. For a transgender man, testosterone can deepen the voice, increase facial and body hair, change fat distribution, and increase muscle mass. For a transgender woman, oestrogen-based treatment can cause breast development, change fat distribution, and soften the skin. Testosterone is usually suppressed as well. But hormones are not magic.

They do not produce identical results in everyone. Some changes develop over months. Others take years. And certain changes may be permanent even if treatment is stopped. For example, testosterone-induced voice deepening generally does not reverse simply by discontinuing testosterone. Breast development caused by oestrogen may also persist after treatment ends. Hormone therapy can affect fertility, sexual function, mood, and other aspects of health. Treatment therefore requires informed decision-making and appropriate medical monitoring.

For some people, these changes bring relief. Their reflection becomes easier to live with.

But hormones cannot change everything. And sometimes, the changes a person wants cannot be achieved through hormones alone. That is when surgery may enter the picture.

3:35Surgery: the price of a physical change

Surgery: the price of a physical change. Gender-affirming surgery is not one operation. Some transgender men undergo chest surgery to remove breast tissue and create a chest contour they are comfortable with. Some transgender women undergo breast augmentation, or procedures that alter facial features. There are also genital surgeries. Vaginoplasty can create a vagina and vulva. Phalloplasty builds a penis from tissue taken from another part of the body, such as the forearm. Metoidioplasty uses genital tissue that has grown under testosterone, and the result is usually smaller.

Some involve extensive surgery and weeks or months of recovery. Patients may experience pain, swelling, bleeding, infection, problems with healing, or complications specific to the procedure. Certain genital surgeries can require additional procedures. And there is no guarantee that the outcome will match someone's expectations. Some results are permanent. Some procedures can permanently affect fertility. Others may alter sensation, sexual function, or the ability to urinate in particular ways. Some people also need ongoing medical care after surgery.

When an operation removes the body's primary sources of sex hormones, long-term hormone replacement may be necessary to maintain health.

This is not a small decision. It requires medical assessment, realistic expectations, an understanding of the risks, and time to consider the alternatives. So why would someone accept all of this? Why would someone choose a treatment that can be painful, expensive, complicated, and sometimes irreversible? To answer that question, we need to talk about something that is difficult to understand from the outside.

5:37What if you don't feel it yourself?

What if you don't feel it yourself? Suppose your body looks perfectly unremarkable to everyone around you. Nothing about it stands out to them. Your family may see nothing wrong. Your friends may see nothing wrong. But your own experience can be very different.

For some transgender people, particular physical characteristics cause persistent distress. It may be their chest, their facial hair, their voice, their genitals, or the way their body develops. This distress is often described as gender dysphoria. Not every transgender person experiences dysphoria, and those who do can experience it in different ways and at different intensities. For some, it is an ongoing source of suffering. For others, it becomes more manageable as they find ways to live that feel right for them. If you are comfortable with your own body, it can be difficult to understand why another person would want to change theirs.

But there is an important difference between not understanding an experience and deciding that the experience cannot be real.

6:45Back to the question we started with

And this is where we need to return to the question we started with.

If you believe that gender dysphoria is merely a whim, a fashion trend, or something people could simply decide to stop feeling, consider what some people are actually willing to undergo. They may accept years of hormone treatment. They may undergo major surgery, face a long recovery, and then live with permanent physical changes. They may accept risks to their fertility, their sexual function, or other aspects of their health. They may spend enormous amounts of money, endure painful procedures, and still face the possibility that the result will not be what they hoped for.

And they do this not because every transgender person wants the same body, but because some people consider the potential improvement in their lives worth those costs. You don't have to experience their distress yourself to recognise that it can be real.

The fact that someone accepts medical risks does not, by itself, prove a diagnosis. But the existence of serious, carefully considered medical treatment, and the experiences of people who seek it, should make us cautious about dismissing their suffering as something trivial. A fashion trend does not adequately explain every person's experience. And telling someone to simply stop feeling something is not the same as helping them. For people who experience significant dysphoria, medical transition can be a way to reduce that distress and improve their quality of life.

Not a perfect solution. Not a guarantee of happiness. But a meaningful possibility.

8:33Why some people choose not to transition medically

Why some people choose not to transition medically. There is another side to this story. If medical transition can be so important to some people, why do others avoid it? The reasons are varied. Some people want some physical changes but not others. Some are concerned about medical risks or the possibility of losing fertility. Others cannot afford treatment, cannot access a qualified provider, or live in places where treatment is difficult or dangerous to obtain. And some simply do not want the procedures available to them.

Medical transition should not be treated as a test of whether someone is transgender enough. Nobody should have to prove their identity by accepting risks they do not want. The goal is not to change every transgender person's body. The goal is to understand what each person needs, and to respect the fact that those needs are not identical.

9:35The question we should really be asking

The question we should really be asking. Let's return to our original question. Why would someone change their body? The answer is not that all transgender people feel the same way. It is that, for some people, the relationship between their identity and their physical characteristics can cause profound distress. And for some of them, changing those characteristics can make life significantly more bearable. Taking that seriously means acknowledging both the potential benefits and the real risks. And recognising that nobody should be pressured into a procedure they do not want.

But it also means refusing to casually dismiss people who make these choices after considering what they may cost. Because for some transgender people, this is not about achieving perfection. It is about being able to live in their own bodies with less distress… and to feel, perhaps for the first time, a little more at home.

But even deeply personal decisions do not always remain personal. Other people notice. They make assumptions. They judge appearances. They decide who looks sufficiently masculine or feminine, who belongs, and who does not. Because changing your body is one thing. Living in a society that believes it has the right to judge that body is another. And that's the story we'll explore next. Why is it so hard to be trans?
