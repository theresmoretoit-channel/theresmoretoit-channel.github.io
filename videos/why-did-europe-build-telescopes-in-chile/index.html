Why Is Europe Building Telescopes in Chile?

10 min2026-10-16

Part of the answer is the air. Twinkling stars look lovely, but for an astronomer the atmosphere is the hardest part of the job, and the Atacama Desert has some of the driest, steadiest skies on Earth. But that only explains the desert. It doesn't explain one word in the name of the organisation behind it all: the European Southern Observatory. From a black hole at the centre of our galaxy to a planet around the nearest star, and from ALMA's sixty-six antennas to the Extremely Large Telescope and NASA's new Roman Space Telescope: why Europe looks at the sky from the other side of the world, and why telescopes in space haven't made that pointless.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The largest telescope ever made

In a desert in northern Chile, Europe is building the largest optical telescope ever made. Its main mirror will be thirty-nine metres across. Stand it on its edge, and it would be taller than a twelve-storey building. And it won't be alone. Europe already runs some of the world's most powerful telescopes there, more than ten thousand kilometres from home. The organisation behind them is called the European Southern Observatory. And that one word, Southern, turns out to matter more than it seems.

0:34The problem is the air

But first, the obvious problem. The hardest part of a telescope's job isn't the distance. It's the last few kilometres. Light from a distant galaxy can travel for billions of years with almost nothing in its way. Then, in the last thousandth of a second, it passes through our atmosphere. And the air is never still. Pockets of warmer and cooler air bend the light slightly differently, many times a second. That's why stars twinkle. Twinkling looks lovely. But for an astronomer, it smears a sharp point of light into a trembling blur.

Then there's water vapour, which swallows whole bands of infrared light. And clouds and city lights, which can end a night before it starts. So before you ask how big a telescope should be, you have to ask where on Earth the sky gets in the way the least.

1:27The Atacama Desert

One of the best answers is the Atacama Desert in Chile, among the driest places on Earth. The air above it holds very little water vapour. Clouds are rare. Cities are far away. And the mountains lift the telescopes above a large part of the atmosphere. ESO's Paranal Observatory stands two thousand six hundred metres up. More than a quarter of the atmosphere is already below it.

2:03Why "Southern"?

So that explains the desert. But it doesn't explain the word in ESO's name. Southern. There are excellent observing sites much closer to home, on the Canary Islands, for example. European astronomers use them too. But they share one limitation with every observatory in Europe. They're in the northern hemisphere. Earth is a ball. From the north, part of the sky is always hidden below the horizon. And the hidden part isn't just any part. The centre of our own galaxy lies in the southern sky. From central Europe, it never gets far above the horizon, where its light has to cross the most air.

From Paranal, it passes almost straight overhead. The Magellanic Clouds, two small companion galaxies of our own, can't be seen from Europe at all. Neither can Alpha Centauri, the nearest star system to the Sun. And in the 1950s, when ESO was first being planned, every telescope in the world with a mirror two metres or wider stood in the northern hemisphere. The southern sky had nothing as big. That's what the word Southern means. Chile gives Europe a part of the sky it can't see from home, through some of the clearest air on the planet.

And it paid off. For decades, telescopes in Chile followed stars racing around the centre of our galaxy. They were orbiting something dark and enormously heavy: a black hole about four million times the mass of the Sun. That work won a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

3:45Why Europe, together?

But why is this Europe, together, and not France or Germany on its own? Because telescopes like these cost more than most countries would ever spend alone. So they share the cost, the engineers, and the observing time. ESO was founded in 1962 by five countries: Belgium, France, Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden. Today it has sixteen member states, with Chile as the host. Their first observatory was La Silla, chosen in 1964. Then came Paranal, and the Very Large Telescope. Despite the name, the VLT isn't one telescope. It's four. Each with a mirror eight point two metres across.

4:31Measuring a star's wobble

So what does all of this actually see? Here are two examples, and they work in very different ways. The first is an instrument on the VLT, called ESPRESSO. It doesn't take pictures of planets. It watches stars move. When a planet orbits a star, the star isn't perfectly still. Both circle a shared centre of mass, so the star sways back and forth. As it moves towards us, its light shifts slightly bluer. As it moves away, slightly redder. ESPRESSO measures that shift. Take Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun. From Europe, it never rises.

It has a planet, Proxima b, discovered in 2016 with HARPS at La Silla, and confirmed in 2020 by ESPRESSO. The planet swings its star towards us and away every eleven days, at about walking pace. That's walking pace, measured on a star forty trillion kilometres away. And ESPRESSO is built to measure even smaller motions, down to about ten centimetres a second. That's roughly how fast the Earth makes the Sun wobble.

5:42ALMA and the cold universe

The second example doesn't use visible light at all. ALMA, run by ESO and its partners, is sixty-six antennas on a plateau five thousand metres up. It picks up millimetre and submillimetre waves, from cold gas and dust: the raw material of stars and planets. And here, the dry air isn't a bonus. It's essential. Water vapour absorbs exactly these waves. On a damp site, ALMA would be nearly blind. It has photographed young stars surrounded by discs of dust, with gaps where new planets seem to be forming.

6:21Why build an even bigger one?

So why build an even bigger one? Because the most interesting things are often the faintest. Small planets next to bright stars. The very first galaxies. The Extremely Large Telescope, the ELT, will collect nearly twenty times as much light as one of the VLT's mirrors. A mirror that size can't be made in one piece. It's made of seven hundred and ninety-eight hexagonal segments, working together as one surface. And it takes on the twinkling directly. More than five thousand actuators reshape one of its mirrors, up to a thousand times a second, to undo the blur of the atmosphere.

It isn't finished yet. ESO plans first light in March 2029, and the first science in December 2030.

7:14Why not just go to space?

At this point, you might reasonably ask: why bother? We have telescopes in space. No air, no clouds, no twinkling. Hubble has been up there since 1990, James Webb since 2021. And Webb, working mostly in infrared, doesn't have to look through water vapour, or the glow of warm air. But Webb's mirror is six and a half metres across, and even that had to be folded up to fit inside its rocket. A thirty-nine-metre mirror won't fit inside any rocket we have. And on the ground, upgrading an instrument takes a truck, not a space mission.

So this isn't a contest of mirror size. Webb sees what the ground can't. And where the air lets light through, the ELT will collect far more of it, and see finer detail. Different questions, often about the very same objects.

8:09Roman joins the team

And at the end of August 2026, another one joined them. NASA's Roman Space Telescope. It's heading for a point called L2, one and a half million kilometres beyond the Earth, where it will circle that point, just like Webb. Its mirror is the same size as Hubble's. But it sees about a hundred times more sky in a single shot. Its job isn't to look closer. It's to look wider. Roman might spot something strange across a huge patch of sky. Webb might take a closer look. And the ELT might split its light into a spectrum.

8:49Back in the desert

So why is Europe building its giant eyes in the Chilean desert? Because the sky it wanted was in the south. And because Chile had one of the clearest views of it. There's no single best telescope. Space or ground, big or wide, each one sees a different part of the same story. And all of them depend on the same thing. A tiny amount of light, that crossed the universe for billions of years… only to land on a mirror, built by people, in a desert on the other side of the world.
