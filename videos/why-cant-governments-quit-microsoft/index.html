Why Can't Governments Quit Microsoft?

11 min2026-11-12

This video goes through the Czech government's framework agreement for Microsoft products, why picking the software everyone already knows was the sensible decision, what vendor lock-in looks like inside a ministry, the British government's surprising advice on lock-in in the cloud, what the CLOUD Act means for data stored in Europe, what Microsoft France told the French Senate under oath, Munich's LiMux, Schleswig-Holstein's move to open source, and a simple test any government can run on itself.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00The prosecutor who lost his email

By May 2025, the chief prosecutor of the International Criminal Court had lost access to his email. Not because of a hack. Not because of a power cut. Because of a signature in Washington. That February, the United States had put him under sanctions. And his email ran on Microsoft, an American company. Microsoft says it never stopped serving the court itself. But it was in contact with the court throughout the process that led to his disconnection. The exact chain of decisions is disputed. But the dependency is not. A sanction signed in Washington reached an inbox in The Hague.

0:43Now scale that up

Now scale that up. Your tax office, your hospitals, your police all depend on software and services from a handful of companies abroad. Could your country still function if that software doubled in price overnight? Or if its maker was no longer allowed to sell it to you? Governments know all this. And they keep signing the contracts anyway. So why?

1:09Fourteen billion crowns

Take the Czech Republic. In September 2024, its Interior Ministry signed a framework agreement for Microsoft products. The ceiling? Just over fourteen billion Czech crowns, before VAT. That's well over half a billion euros. It runs for four years, covering Microsoft licences and support for ministries and other state institutions. It's a ceiling, not a bill. The money is spent order by order, in smaller tenders between approved suppliers. Pooling demand gives the government more bargaining power. But it also makes the same ecosystem the default across many institutions.

1:50Not just the Czechs

And the Czechs are not unusual. A study for the European Parliament found in December 2025 that American firms dominate almost every layer of Europe's digital stack.

2:04How did it get like this?

So how did it get like this? Mostly by making sensible decisions, one at a time. A modern government is a machine for processing information. Taxes, identity cards, court rulings, land records, emergency calls. And inside its offices, the software looks very familiar. Windows. Office. Outlook. Active Directory. Teams. Nothing exotic. The same tools millions of companies use. And that's exactly why governments chose them. Staff already know them. Administrators know how to run them. Documents and applications expect them.

When you need twenty thousand computers, picking what everyone knows isn't a scandal. It's the obvious decision. But a company can change its strategy. A state can't simply stop working while it thinks about it.

3:03Why not just switch?

So why not just switch? Picture a ministry with twenty thousand staff. Accounts in Active Directory. Email on Exchange. Workflows in SharePoint. Excel macros nobody remembers writing. Replacing that isn't a matter of installing Linux and LibreOffice. You migrate documents, rewrite applications, rebuild logins, retrain people, and test everything. And you do it without the tax office closing for a single day.

3:33Vendor lock-in

That's called vendor lock-in. Leaving a supplier becomes so hard, or so expensive, that it stops being a real option. And here's the strange part. Nobody has to force you to stay. There doesn't even need to be a clause in the contract. Every year, more applications are built on the system you already have. More staff learn it. More data piles up in its formats. The technology itself becomes the contract. And every year, the way out gets a little longer.

Now suppose the supplier raises its prices. Or moves a feature you rely on into a more expensive subscription. You can negotiate. You can threaten to leave. But if leaving takes five years and costs billions, everyone at the table knows it.

4:24The British government's advice

The British government has official guidance on exactly this. It warns that switching providers can become difficult, time-consuming and disproportionately expensive. And then it says something surprising. In the cloud, the advantages of being locked in can often outweigh the disadvantages. So the question isn't how to avoid every dependency. That's impossible. It's whether you know which ones you have, and whether there's a real way out.

4:55Back to The Hague

And price is the smaller problem. The bigger one is what happened in The Hague. A software company doesn't float above the law. It answers to the country it belongs to, including its sanctions and export rules. Most of the time, that never matters. Until relations between two countries go bad. No, Microsoft can't switch off the Czech Republic on a whim. There are contracts, courts, and a lot of money in the way. But the court in The Hague shows the real shape of the risk. Not a whole country going dark. One person, targeted by another government.

5:36Buying versus renting

Think of the difference between buying a computer and renting a service. A computer you bought keeps working even if its maker disappears. A service you rent exists only as long as someone keeps providing it.

5:50Where the data is stored

Which brings us to the data. A government might keep its files in a data centre inside its own borders. That sounds reassuring. But where a server stands doesn't decide which laws apply to the company running it. America's CLOUD Act of 2018 lets US authorities, with legal process, require American providers to hand over data in their possession, custody or control, even when it's stored abroad.

6:19Under oath in the French Senate

In June 2025, the French Senate questioned Microsoft France's director of public and legal affairs, who was testifying under oath. Could he guarantee that French citizens' data, entrusted to Microsoft by the state, would never be handed to US authorities without France's explicit consent? No, he said, he couldn't. But he added that it had never happened. No such request, he said, had ever affected a European company or public body. Both answers can be true at once. Where your data is stored and who has legal control over it are two different things.

7:01Just install Linux!

At this point, someone is shouting at the screen: just install Linux! Munich tried. Starting in 2004, the city moved around fifteen thousand office computers to its own Linux, called LiMux. In 2017, three years after a new mayor took office, the city council voted to go back to Windows. Why Munich reversed course is still debated. But the desktop migration was only one part. The harder challenge was everything around it: old applications, support, users and political commitment.

7:38Schleswig-Holstein

Others are trying now. The German state of Schleswig-Holstein has moved nearly eighty per cent of its workstations outside the tax offices to LibreOffice. It has moved almost forty-four thousand mailboxes to open-source email. And it says it already saves more than fifteen million euros in licence fees. In its State Chancellery, more than two hundred people now work on Linux. About twenty desks are still waiting, tied to specialised applications.

8:10The court in The Hague, again

And the International Criminal Court, the one in The Hague, confirmed in October 2025 that it would move to openDesk, an open-source office suite developed for the German government.

8:23Open source isn't free

Open source has real advantages. Anyone can inspect the code. You can change it. And more than one company can maintain it. But open source isn't free infrastructure. Someone still has to patch it, support it, and fix it at three in the morning. So the real goal isn't to get rid of Microsoft. It's to make sure Microsoft isn't the only realistic option.

8:51Digital sovereignty

That's what people mean by digital sovereignty. It sounds like a slogan, but the idea is practical. No country can make every chip and write every program itself. But it can know where its critical dependencies are, keep control of its data, and insist on open standards. The aim isn't to become technologically isolated. It's to avoid becoming technologically helpless.

9:20A simple test

Here's a simple test. If your biggest software supplier stopped serving you tomorrow, could you still reach your own data? Could you move your applications? Could someone else take over? And how long would it take? A week. A month. Five years. If nobody knows, that's the answer. An exit plan that exists only on paper isn't an exit plan. It has to be technically possible, affordable, and tested before the crisis, not during it.

9:51So what's the mistake?

Buying software from a company isn't the mistake. Companies often build it far better than governments ever could. The mistake is becoming unable to function without them.

In the end, that's what digital sovereignty means. Being able to choose your technology suppliers. And being able to choose again. A government can rent its software. It shouldn't rent its ability to function.

So here's a question for you. If your country's biggest software supplier walked away tomorrow, what would break first? Let me know in the comments.
