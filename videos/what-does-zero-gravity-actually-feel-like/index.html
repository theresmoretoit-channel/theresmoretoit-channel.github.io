How Do They Simulate Zero or Low Gravity on Earth?

14 min2026-09-29

What does it actually feel like? Why can it make you sick? Why does it last only about twenty seconds, when gravity isn't going anywhere? And is it really "zero gravity" at all?

We follow one parabola from start to finish — the pull-up at 1.8 g, the free fall, the way the floor slowly comes back — and look at the aircraft, the pilots who fly it, and the people who use these flights, from scientists and astronauts to film crews and anyone who buys a seat.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Floating inside an airplane

Imagine lying on the floor of an airplane. Suddenly, you weigh almost twice as much. Lifting your arm becomes difficult. Moving your head takes effort. Then, about twenty seconds later… you are floating. Your arm has no weight. Your feet aren't touching the floor. You can push yourself away from the ground and fly across the cabin. And then, gradually, the floor starts pushing you back. This isn't science fiction. This is how humans simulate weightlessness here on Earth. And the strangest part is that the airplane never actually leaves Earth's gravity.

You can't turn gravity off. The Earth is still pulling on the airplane, just as strongly as ever.

0:47How do you make someone weightless?

So how do you make somebody feel weightless? You let them fall. If you jump off a chair, you are briefly in free fall. You and everything around you are accelerating downward together. The same principle works with an entire airplane. Instead of dropping for a fraction of a second, it follows a carefully controlled ballistic trajectory.

1:10Parabolic flight

This is called a parabolic flight. Specially modified aircraft, such as the Airbus A310 Zero G, fly these trajectories again and again to create short periods of microgravity. The one flying today used to carry German government officials. It replaced an older Airbus A300, which flew these parabolas for almost eighteen years. And each parabola has several distinct phases. First, level flight at about six kilometres, with normal gravity.

1:35The phases of a parabola

Then the pilots pull the aircraft sharply upward. The acceleration rises to about one point eight g. Flying this takes three pilots at once. One controls the nose, up and down. One keeps the aircraft steady sideways. And one handles the engines. They're experienced test pilots, from France's national flight test centre. Then comes the crucial moment. The aircraft enters its free-fall trajectory. Inside the cabin, people experience roughly zero g. For about twenty seconds. Then the pilots pull out of the dive. And the passengers experience about one point eight g again. Then the cycle starts over.

2:37What 1.8 g feels like

One point eight g is easy to underestimate. At one g, the floor supports you with your normal weight. At one point eight g, the forces are almost twice as large. If you weigh eighty kilograms, your mass hasn't changed. But as far as the floor and your muscles are concerned, your body behaves roughly as if it weighed a hundred and forty-four kilograms. Your arm feels heavier. Your legs feel heavier. And your head feels heavier. Even moving your head can take noticeably more effort. That's why passengers don't normally stand around during this phase. They lie down or brace themselves against the floor.

3:20Free fall

And then something extraordinary happens. The aircraft enters free fall. The airplane is falling. And you are falling with it. Because you and the aircraft are accelerating downward together, the floor no longer needs to push you upward to support your weight. And that's what you experience as weightlessness. Try lifting your arm. Normally, your muscles have to overcome its weight. Now they barely have to do anything. A tiny movement can send your arm drifting through the air. You can push yourself away from the floor. And suddenly you're flying.

4:00Why it can make you sick

But there is a catch. Your brain has spent your entire life learning how movement is supposed to work. And now you're giving it completely different information. Your sense of orientation comes from several systems working together. Your eyes tell you what the world looks like. Your muscles and joints tell your brain where your body is. And your vestibular system, inside your inner ear, detects movement and changes in orientation. Normally, these systems agree. You stand on the floor. Your eyes see the room. Your muscles feel the floor supporting you. Your inner ear knows which way is up.

But in weightlessness, the rules change. You can rotate your body without falling. You can turn upside down. You can move in three dimensions without your feet telling you where the floor is. And your eyes can see one thing while your vestibular system is experiencing something completely different. That sensory mismatch can cause motion sickness. And that's why something as simple as turning your head can suddenly make you feel terrible.

You might think the first thing you'd want to do in weightlessness is move around. Turn around. Look behind you. Try a flip. Push yourself across the cabin. And that's exactly what can make you sick. Head movements are particularly troublesome, because they strongly stimulate the vestibular system. That's why people on parabolic flights are taught to move carefully and avoid unnecessary head movements. Anti-nausea medication may also be used. Because twenty seconds of weightlessness sounds like an incredible amount of fun… until your stomach disagrees.

NASA flew its own version for decades, a converted KC-135. It earned a nickname that says it all: the Vomit Comet.

6:01Why only twenty seconds?

Here's the really interesting part. Why can't the airplane just stay weightless for a minute? After all, gravity isn't going anywhere. So why stop? The answer is height and speed. The aircraft pulls up from about six kilometres. When the free-fall phase begins, it's roughly seven point six kilometres above the ground, travelling at around six hundred and fifty kilometres per hour, and still climbing. It continues upward for a while, to a peak of roughly eight point five kilometres. Then it starts coming down. The important thing is that the airplane is in free fall during both parts. It's weightless while climbing. It's weightless near the top. And it's still weightless while descending.

The entire microgravity phase lasts roughly twenty to twenty-two seconds. And then the pilots have to start pulling out.

6:59What if they didn't pull out?

Let's exaggerate the experiment. What if the pilots didn't pull out? What if they simply let the airplane continue on its ballistic trajectory for a full minute? At first, it would climb. Then it would reach its highest point. Then it would descend faster and faster. After about thirty-six seconds, a simple ballistic calculation puts it back at six kilometres, where it started pulling up. And it would already be diving at over nine hundred kilometres per hour. Carry the same calculation on to sixty seconds, and the trajectory ends up below ground level, at well over the speed of sound.

Obviously, that's not what would happen. The airplane would pass its speed limits long before, and then hit the ground. And pulling out isn't instant either. Turning a dive back into level flight, at one point eight g, costs well over a kilometre of height. So the problem isn't gravity. Gravity would happily keep going. The problem is that falling keeps making you faster… and you need room to stop.

8:09The whole arc

So if we plot the trajectory, it looks something like this. The airplane starts around six kilometres up. It climbs to around seven point six kilometres. Then it enters the free-fall trajectory. It keeps climbing to approximately eight point five kilometres. Then it starts descending. And somewhere during that descent, the pilots have to pull the airplane out. The important part is this: the weightless section isn't just the top of the curve. The aircraft is in free fall throughout the entire ballistic arc. Upward. Over the top. And downward.

That's why you don't get only a few seconds of weightlessness at the top. You get roughly twenty seconds across the whole arc.

8:57Zero to 1.8 g, gradually

There's another detail that is easy to miss. The change from zero g to one point eight g isn't a sudden switch. The aircraft begins pulling out of the parabola. The acceleration gradually increases. If you're floating in the cabin, you start feeling something beneath you again. At first, almost nothing. Then a little more. Then more. You begin moving toward the floor. And after several seconds, the floor is pushing against you with normal gravity again. And then the acceleration keeps increasing, until you're back at roughly one point eight g. That's when being on the floor becomes a very good idea.

So it's not zero, then bang, one point eight. It's a third of your weight. Two thirds. Your normal weight. And then heavier still. And then you spend another twenty seconds or so being almost twice as heavy.

9:54Again and again

So imagine experiencing this again and again. You start at normal gravity. Then one point eight g. Your body suddenly feels extremely heavy. Then zero g. You float. Your arm has no weight. You push yourself away from the floor. Then the floor slowly comes back. Zero. Then one. Then one point eight. And again. And again. A typical parabolic flight can perform dozens of these parabolas. So you're repeatedly switching between two completely different physical environments. Almost twice your normal apparent weight… and almost none.

It's not surprising that some people feel sick.

10:37The Moon and Mars

The same principle can be used to simulate other worlds. You don't necessarily have to create zero g. The aircraft can follow a slightly different trajectory and create partial gravity. For example, around zero point one six g can simulate the Moon. And around zero point three eight g can simulate Mars. Interestingly, these phases can last longer than the standard microgravity parabola. The lower the required acceleration, the more gradual the trajectory can be, allowing a longer period of simulated partial gravity. So researchers can study not just what it feels like to float… but what it might feel like to move around on another world.

Scientists run experiments on board. Astronauts train here. Film crews have used it too: many of the weightless scenes in Apollo 13 were filmed on NASA's KC-135. And today, members of the public can buy a seat.

11:41Is it really zero gravity?

But is it really zero gravity? Not technically. Gravity is still there. The Earth is still pulling on the aircraft. The Earth is still pulling on you. The reason you feel weightless is that you and the aircraft are falling together. Normally, the floor prevents you from falling. And that force from the floor is what you experience as your weight. Remove the floor's supporting force… and suddenly you feel weightless. So perhaps the better term isn't zero gravity. It's free fall. Or, in spaceflight terms, microgravity.

Imagine that for your entire life, one rule has always been obvious. Things fall down.

12:21Things fall down

Then, for about twenty seconds, that rule seems to disappear. You raise your hand… and it floats. You push yourself away… and you fly. You turn around… and nothing tells your body which way is down. Then the aircraft begins pulling out. The floor slowly comes back. Your body becomes heavy again. And then you're back at one point eight g. Almost twice the force you normally experience. For about twenty seconds. Then you're back to normal. And then it happens all over again.

So a parabolic flight isn't really about turning gravity off. It's about something much more interesting. Using gravity itself to make an airplane fall. And for about twenty seconds… you get to fall with it.
