Why Are iPhone Texts to Android Still Green?

8 min2026-11-01

This video follows the two roads a message can take: why green was there first and blue came later, what falling back to the old road did to group chats, videos and reactions, and why the colour became a social thing in America while most of the world barely noticed.

Along the way: emails from a court case showing Apple executives discussing iMessage on Android back in 2013, "Buy your mom an iPhone", Google's long line of messaging apps, RCS, Beeper Mini, and what changed in 2023 and 2024.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00Blue and green

Send a message from one iPhone to another, and it shows up in a blue bubble. Send the same message from an iPhone to an Android phone, and the bubble is green. Same words. Same app. Different colour. For years, that colour came with real problems. Blurry videos. Broken group chats. In 2024, the iPhone finally learned the same modern messaging standard Android phones use. And the bubbles are still green. So why?

0:34Green came first

Here's the first surprise. Green wasn't invented to mark Android users. When the iPhone launched in 2007, every text you sent on it was green. Every single one. Even between two iPhones. Blue only arrived in 2011, with a new service called iMessage. So green isn't the odd colour out. Green is the original. Blue is the exception. And the colour doesn't describe the phone on the other end. It describes the road the message travelled.

1:10Two roads

To see why that matters, we need to look at those two roads. The old road is the mobile network. SMS for text, MMS for pictures and video. Practically every phone speaks them, because they belong to the mobile network, not to the phone makers. But it's an old road. An SMS holds a hundred and sixty characters. And an MMS video is squeezed until it fits the operator's size limit, often about a megabyte, or less. The new road is the internet. iMessage sends your message as ordinary data, through Apple's own servers. No operator in the middle. No size limits from the flip-phone era.

Read receipts. Typing indicators. Full-quality photos. Group chats that actually work. But Apple's servers only talk to Apple devices. So when an iPhone sent a message to anything else, it couldn't take the new road. It fell back to the old one. Blue means Apple carried it. Green means someone else did. That's the whole technical answer. And it would be a boring story, if it ended there. It doesn't.

2:29What falling back cost

Because falling back to the old road made the conversation worse. Not just for the Android user. For everyone in the chat. Add one Android phone to a group of iPhones, and the whole group dropped to MMS. Videos arrived as a smudge. Reactions arrived as text. "Liked an image." And for the person everyone was waiting on, it looked like their phone was the problem.

2:56A social thing in America

In the United States, this became a social thing. In 2022, the Wall Street Journal reported that American teenagers dreaded the green bubble. Some said they'd been singled out, even ostracised, for switching. The same year, a survey of American teens found that eighty-seven per cent owned an iPhone. And in 2024, when the US government sued Apple for monopolising smartphones, the green bubble was in the complaint. So this wasn't a niche complaint. In America, the colour mattered.

3:33The rest of the world shrugged

But here's the strange part. In most of the world, it barely registered. Ask someone in Germany, Brazil or India about green bubbles, and you may get a blank look. And a big part of the reason goes back to the price of a text message. In much of Europe, texts often cost money, one by one. So when smartphones arrived, people jumped to apps that sent messages over data. Above all, WhatsApp. And WhatsApp looks the same on an iPhone and on Android. No blue. No green. No fallback. In the United States, texting had become cheap, and often unlimited.

So there was little reason to leave the phone's built-in app. Americans kept texting, and more and more of them did it on an iPhone. The green bubble problem took hold where texting was cheap and iPhones were common.

4:30Why not iMessage on Android?

So why didn't Apple just put iMessage on Android? Inside Apple, people did suggest it. Emails that came out in a court case show executives discussing the idea in 2013. But one executive argued that iMessage on Android would make it easier for iPhone families to give their kids Android phones. iMessage wasn't just a feature. It was a reason to stay. And in 2022, when someone told Apple's CEO he couldn't send his mother certain videos, because she had an Android phone, the answer was: "Buy your mom an iPhone."

5:11Google and RCS

But Apple isn't the only reason this took so long. Android had no single answer to iMessage. Google launched one messaging app after another. Talk. Hangouts. Allo. And retired them, one after another. The real successor to SMS was supposed to be RCS. Rich Communication Services. A standard designed by the mobile industry in the late 2000s. But RCS depended on operators to switch it on, and for years, many didn't. In the end, Google ran much of it on its own servers.

5:51The turn: 2023 to 2024

And then, in November 2023, Apple made an announcement. The iPhone would support RCS. Apple didn't say why now. The timing lined up with a European competition investigation. Others pointed to China, where new phones were being pushed to support RCS. A few weeks later, a small app called Beeper Mini let Android phones send real iMessages. Apple shut it out within days.

Blue stayed Apple's. But in 2024, RCS arrived. iPhones and Android phones could finally share read receipts, typing indicators, and proper photos. In 2026, Apple and Google began rolling out encryption for those messages, first as a beta, and only with some operators. So the road between the two is finally paved.

6:49So why is it still green?

Which brings us back to the question. Why is the bubble still green? Because RCS is not iMessage. It's a newer, better road, but it isn't Apple's road. Blue is reserved for messages Apple carries itself. So the colour never really meant "this phone is worse". It meant "this message isn't ours".

7:15Who owns the road

And that's the quiet change behind the whole story. SMS belonged to the operators, and so it worked with everyone. The messengers that replaced it belong to the companies that make the phones and the apps. And a company that owns the road gets to decide who's allowed to use it. And what colour you are when you do.

So here's a question for you. If every bubble turned the same colour tomorrow, would anyone actually switch phones? Let me know in the comments.
