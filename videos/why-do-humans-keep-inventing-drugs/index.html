Why Do Humans Keep Inventing Drugs?

12 min2026-11-14

This video goes through caffeine-fed caterpillars and bees, how drugs led scientists to the brain's own opioids and cannabinoids, what dopamine really does, the mutation that let our ape ancestors break down alcohol, why most people who try a drug don't get hooked, coffee bans in Mecca and England, the pharmacist who isolated morphine, the Bayer chemist who made aspirin and heroin eleven days apart, the legal loophole of new psychoactive substances, and psilocybin on prescription in Australia.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Tobacco seeds by an ancient fire

More than twelve thousand years ago, in what is now Utah, someone sat by a fire and left behind a few tobacco seeds. About eight thousand years ago, in the Andes, people were chewing coca leaves with a pinch of powdered lime. And about nine thousand years ago, in a village in China, people were brewing a drink from rice, honey and fruit. These people never met. They didn't share a language, a religion, or even a continent. And yet each of them found a plant that changes how a human brain works. And kept coming back to it.

Nobody knows when humans first did this. And these are just three examples. Opium in the Bronze Age Mediterranean. Cannabis in the mountains of Central Asia. So why? Why does every human society, sooner or later, find a way to change its own mind? And there's a stranger question hiding underneath. Why does a plant contain a molecule that fits a human brain at all?

1:04Plants fight with chemistry

A coffee bush doesn't care whether you're awake. A tobacco plant has never met a smoker. The answer is that these molecules weren't made for us. They're weapons. Plants can't run away from the animals that eat them. So many of them fight with chemistry. In 1984, a researcher in Boston mixed ground coffee and tea leaves into the food of caterpillars. They ate less, trembled, and stopped growing. At higher doses, they were dead within a day. Nicotine is so good at this that farmers sprayed tobacco water on their crops for centuries. And some of today's most widely used insecticides are modelled on it.

Even cocaine seems to protect the coca plant. At the levels found in its leaves, it scrambles the nerves of the insects that chew them.

1:57Why it works on us too

And here's why the weapon works on us too. An insect's nervous system and yours run on many of the same chemicals. Dopamine. Serotonin. Acetylcholine. Insects use them too. Some of these signalling systems have been around for hundreds of millions of years. So when a plant evolves a molecule that jams an insect's nerves, it often jams ours as well. The same molecule can have very different effects in different animals. Dose matters. So does biology. The caffeine in your coffee is the same defensive chemical that helps protect the plant. Just in a very different dose.

2:40Caffeine for bees

And plants don't only use these chemicals as weapons. Coffee and citrus flowers put a little caffeine in their nectar. Not enough to harm a bee. Just enough to change it. In 2013, researchers found that bees given caffeine were three times as likely to remember a flower's scent a day later. Plants were already using chemistry to influence their visitors long before we came along.

3:08Why would a brain want a poison?

But that only explains why these molecules work. It doesn't explain why we want them. A poison should taste like a warning. So why would a brain go looking for it?

To answer that, scientists had to look inside the brain. And for a long time, the best map they had was the drugs themselves.

3:31A lock for a poppy

In 1973, three laboratories, working independently, found receptors in the brain that morphine locks onto. Which raised an obvious question. Why would a human brain have a lock for a poppy? Two years later, researchers in Aberdeen found the key. The brain makes its own opioids. We now call them endorphins. The name means "the morphine within". Cannabis led to the same discovery. First a receptor its active compound fits, then, in 1992, the brain's own version. Its discoverers named it anandamide, after the Sanskrit word for bliss.

And nicotine gave its name to a whole family of receptors, in your muscles as well as your brain.

4:18Hijacked systems

They don't need to invent a new system inside your brain. They hijack systems that were already there. Caffeine blocks a signal that builds up the longer you're awake, the one that makes you feel tired. Nicotine imitates a messenger the brain uses for attention. And cocaine stops the brain from clearing away dopamine, so it keeps working for longer than it should.

4:42What dopamine really does

Dopamine is often called the pleasure chemical. It isn't, really. It's closer to a teacher. Among other things, it helps the brain learn what's worth doing again. So a substance that pushes on it doesn't just feel good. It can teach the brain to treat it as something worth repeating.

5:03The alcohol mutation

Then why didn't evolution protect us from that? With one substance, it seems to have done the opposite. About ten million years ago, our ape ancestors picked up a single mutation in a gene that breaks down alcohol. It made them about forty times better at breaking it down. One explanation is fruit. Around that time, our ancestors were spending more time on the ground, where ripe fruit falls and ferments. Our ancestors may have gained an advantage from eating fermented fruit, millions of years before anyone brewed a drop.

5:41Who gets hooked?

And not everyone who tries a drug gets caught by it. In the early 1990s, a large American survey estimated how many people who had tried a substance went on to become dependent on it. For cannabis, about nine per cent. For alcohol, about fifteen. For heroin, twenty-three. And the highest of all wasn't heroin or cocaine. It was tobacco. Nearly a third. So the risk depends on the substance, and on the person. Genes, mental health, stress, and how often it's used all change the odds.

6:18Not chasing a high

And chemistry still isn't the whole story. The most widely used drug on Earth is caffeine. And hardly anyone drinks coffee for the thrill. They drink it to get through a morning. To stay awake on a night shift. To feel normal. That's what a lot of drug use is. Not chasing a high, but getting away from something. Pain. Fear. Exhaustion. A memory you'd rather not have.

Some cultures used psychedelic plants in ceremonies, where an altered state meant contact with something sacred. Others used alcohol to mark a wedding, or to turn strangers into friends.

6:59Coffee bans

And which of these substances a society accepts isn't decided by chemistry at all.

By the fifteenth century, coffee was spreading through Sufi communities in Yemen, where it kept people awake for night prayers. In 1511, the governor of Mecca banned it. The ban didn't last. In 1675, King Charles the Second tried to shut down every coffee house in England. He backed down before the order even took effect. And in the seventeenth century, the Pope threatened to excommunicate anyone who used tobacco in the churches of Seville. Today, coffee is breakfast. And tobacco, which, in that survey, hooked more of its users than heroin, is sold at the petrol station.

7:48From plant to pill

For most of history, all of this came straight from plants. You chewed the leaf. You drank the brew. You smoked the dried flower. Then, in the early 1800s, a young German pharmacist pulled a single compound out of opium. He named it after Morpheus, the god of dreams. Morphine. For the first time, one of opium's active compounds could be isolated, purified and measured. That changed medicine. And it changed the risk.

Around 1860, a German chemist did the same with coca. A coca leaf is typically less than one per cent cocaine. Purified, it's a hundred times more concentrated.

8:32Aspirin and heroin

And in August 1897, a chemist at Bayer prepared two compounds, eleven days apart. One became aspirin. The other became heroin. Heroin had actually been made more than twenty years earlier. But Bayer turned it into a medicine, and sold it for coughs.

8:53New molecules, new loopholes

And increasingly, chemists went beyond extraction. They began modifying molecules, and creating compounds no plant had ever made. Some became essential medicines. Modern anaesthetics. Antidepressants. Painkillers far stronger than morphine. Being new doesn't make a molecule more dangerous. What matters is what it does, how much of it you take, and how. But it did open a loophole. Laws usually ban specific molecules. Change one small piece of a banned one, and you may get something similar that, legally, doesn't exist yet.

At the end of 2024, the European Union's drugs agency was monitoring more than a thousand new psychoactive substances. Forty-seven of them were reported for the first time that year. When one is banned, another can appear. Faster than anyone can study what it does.

9:52What do we want our brains to do?

And now the question is turning around. It's no longer just what nature happens to contain. It's what we want our brains to do. Since 2023, specially authorised psychiatrists in Australia have been allowed to prescribe psilocybin, the compound in magic mushrooms, for depression that nothing else helped. Millions of people take pills to sleep, to focus, or to feel less afraid. The same molecule can be a medicine when it's used to treat a patient, or an illegal drug when it's possessed or sold unlawfully. The line between a drug, a medicine and a morning coffee isn't written in the molecule. We draw it. And we keep moving it.

10:37So why?

So, why do humans keep inventing drugs? Partly, we don't. The first ones were invented by plants, as weapons against insects. They work on us because our brains are built from the same ancient parts. And they pull us back because they push on the system that decides what's worth repeating. Everything else, the coffee houses, the ceremonies, the laboratories, is what a curious species does once it notices that.

We didn't just invent drugs. We found out that the world is full of chemicals that can change what it feels like to be human. And once you've found that out, it's very hard to stop looking.

So here's a question for you. If a pill could make you sharper, calmer or braver, with no side effects at all, would that still be a drug? Let me know in the comments.
