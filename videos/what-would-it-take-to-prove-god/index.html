What Would It Take to Prove God?

11 min2026-09-30

This video looks at what "proof" even means — an apple in a box, a mathematical proof and a scientific theory are three very different things. Then it goes through the ways people have tried to settle the question of God: the cosmological argument and fine-tuning, claimed miracles, the problem of evil, divine hiddenness, a God defined to be beyond any observation, and Carl Sagan's invisible dragon in the garage.

Along the way: why "there's a natural explanation" is not the same as "God doesn't exist", why the inability to disprove a claim is not evidence that it's true, and the same kind of mistake that atheists and believers sometimes make from opposite sides.

The full narration of the video.

0:00"Because I have proof"

Imagine someone tells you: “God exists.” Okay. So you ask: “How do you know?” And they give you an answer. “Because I have proof.” That sounds simple. But what would that proof actually have to prove? And what if someone else told you: “God does not exist.” Could they prove that? Could we ever definitively prove either one? Maybe the problem is deeper than the question of whether God exists. Maybe we first need to figure out what it would even mean to prove it.

0:35What counts as proof?

Suppose I tell you there is an apple inside this box. We can open it. If there is an apple inside, we can observe it. We can photograph it, weigh it, and let other people examine it. It's a claim we can test. Mathematical proof works differently. We don't have to observe anything. We can start with definitions and rules, and logically demonstrate that a particular conclusion must follow. Science works differently again. A scientific theory makes predictions that can be compared with reality. And ideally, there should be some possible observation that could show us that the theory is wrong.

1:18Which God?

And this is where the question of God becomes difficult. Because there isn't just one concept of God. Some people imagine a God who interacts with the physical world, answers prayers, and performs miracles. Others understand God as something outside space and time. And some philosophers describe God as the foundation of existence itself. These are not exactly the same hypothesis. So we can't simply say: “Science hasn't found God.” We first have to ask: What exactly are we supposed to look for?

2:01The cosmological argument

For centuries, philosophers have offered arguments for God's existence. Take the cosmological argument. It starts with two premises. The universe began to exist. And everything that begins to exist has a cause. So what caused the universe? Perhaps something outside the universe itself. And that first cause could be called God. But notice something important. Even if we accept that the universe has a first cause, it doesn't automatically follow that this cause is a personal, all-powerful, perfectly good God. That's another assumption.

2:40Fine-tuning

And the same problem appears in other arguments. Take fine-tuning. Some physical constants have values that allow stars, chemistry, planets, and eventually life to exist. Why? One possibility is that it's simply the result of chance. Another is that there are enormous numbers of universes with different physical parameters, and we happen to live in one that permits observers. Or perhaps a deeper law we haven't discovered yet makes these values unavoidable. And another possibility is that the universe was designed. But again: The observation itself doesn't say: “God did this.” That's an interpretation of the observation.

That doesn't necessarily make the philosophical argument worthless. It simply means that an argument is not the same thing as an empirical proof.

3:35Miracles and what we can test

Now we're getting closer to something we could potentially test. Suppose someone says: “God healed a person who had an incurable disease.” We can examine the medical records. We can look for alternative explanations. We can investigate whether similar cases occur without prayer. We can compare evidence from different cases. If we see something we can't explain, all we can conclude is: We don't know what caused it. History is full of things people once considered mysterious that later received natural explanations. But the reverse is also important. If science gives us a natural explanation for something, that doesn't prove that God doesn't exist.

“There's a natural explanation” is not the same statement as “God doesn't exist.”

4:28Can we disprove God?

Maybe this should be easier. We just need to find something that makes God's existence impossible. But again: Which God? Suppose someone claims that God is all-powerful and perfectly good. Now we encounter the problem of evil. The world contains enormous amounts of suffering. Wars. Diseases. Earthquakes. Children dying from illnesses. If an all-powerful and perfectly good God exists, why does this happen? This is a serious philosophical problem. A believer can propose different explanations. Free will. The possibility that suffering is necessary for some greater good.

The limitations of human knowledge. Or the possibility that our understanding of what is good is fundamentally different from God's. We might find these explanations unconvincing. We might think they fail to solve the problem. But if we want to be precise, we have to distinguish between: “This explanation doesn't convince me” and “This explanation is logically impossible.” Those are very different claims.

5:39Divine hiddenness

There is another interesting problem. Suppose God exists. And suppose God does not want his existence to be completely obvious. Then perhaps God could create a world where there are reasons to believe… but never conclusive proof. And this leads to the problem of divine hiddenness. If there is a God who wants humans to know him, why is it so unclear whether he exists? Why are there people who genuinely search for God and remain unconvinced? And why are there so many different religions, making different claims about what God wants?

These are legitimate questions. They may simply mean that a particular concept of God has difficulty explaining the world we observe.

6:25A God beyond any observation

What if we define God in a way that makes him impossible to observe? Imagine a being that: doesn't exist in space, doesn't exist in time, doesn't obey physical laws, leaves no measurable traces, and whose existence cannot possibly be disproved by any observation. Can we scientifically search for such a being? Not really. But there's an even more interesting question. If no possible observation could ever show that such a being doesn't exist… what would it mean to claim that it does exist?

6:59The invisible dragon

This is similar to a famous thought experiment by Carl Sagan. Imagine I tell you: “There is an invisible dragon in my garage.” You can't see it. You can't hear it. You can't weigh it. It leaves no footprints. And whenever you bring in a scientific instrument, the dragon somehow avoids detection. How could you prove that the dragon isn't there? You couldn't. But that doesn't mean the dragon is there. And this illustrates a very important principle of reasoning: The inability to disprove a claim is not evidence that the claim is true.

7:38Three different questions

Maybe there are three different questions. First: Can we prove that God exists? For particular concepts of God, we can construct philosophical arguments, historical arguments, or claims based on alleged empirical evidence. But we don't have a universal, indisputable proof of God's existence. Second: Can we prove that God doesn't exist? That can be just as difficult. If God is defined in a way that is compatible with every possible observation, there is no experiment that could definitively rule him out. And third: Can we even create a test that would decide the question?

And here, everything depends on how God is defined. If God interacts with the physical world in specific, predictable ways, some claims about God may be testable. But if God is defined as something fundamentally beyond any possible observation, science has no way to test the hypothesis.

8:38A mistake on both sides

And this is where people often make a mistake. An atheist might say: “You don't have proof, therefore God doesn't exist.” A believer might respond: “You can't prove that God doesn't exist, therefore God does exist.” Neither argument works by itself. The first would be wrong if it simply treated the absence of evidence as proof of absence. But if a particular kind of God should produce evidence that we would reasonably expect to find — and that evidence consistently isn't there — then its absence can be evidence against that hypothesis.

The important point is that this still doesn't automatically prove that God does not exist. The second makes the opposite mistake: treating the absence of disproof as evidence of existence. It is perfectly possible to say: “I don't have sufficient reason to believe this.” while also saying: “I don't have proof that it is impossible.” There is no contradiction. It's simply an acknowledgment of the limits of what we know.

9:44Does God exist? Yes or no

At the beginning, we wanted a simple answer. Does God exist? Yes. Or no. But perhaps the evidence doesn't let us give such a simple answer. We can study the universe. We can study the human brain. We can investigate alleged miracles. We can analyse philosophical arguments. We can compare different religions. And we can ask what we would actually expect to observe if a particular kind of God existed. But eventually, we reach a boundary between claims that can be tested and claims that depend on philosophical interpretation or personal belief.

And perhaps the most intellectually honest answer isn't: “God exists.” Or: “God doesn't exist.” It's this: It depends on what we mean by God — and whether we've defined the claim in a way that reality can actually confirm or disprove. Because sometimes the hardest part isn't finding the answer. It's figuring out whether the question can have an answer at all.
