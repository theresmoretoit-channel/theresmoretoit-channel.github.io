Why Is Computer Stuff Called That?

12 min2026-10-06

Computers are full of words like this. Mouse. Desktop. Cloud. Bug. Some of them are obvious, some were chosen very carefully, and some have travelled so far that hardly anyone remembers where they started.

Why is the internet a "cloud"? What does a payload have to do with a truck? Why do computers "boot"? Was the first bug really a moth? And why does a background program have the name of a demon?

The full narration of the video.

0:00This is a log

This is a log. And so is this. Somehow, a piece of wood became something your computer writes all day long. And the story of how it got there involves a ship, a rope, and a sailor counting knots. Computers are full of words like this. Mouse. Desktop. Cloud. Bug. Words that meant something else long before there were computers. Some of them are obvious. Some were chosen very carefully. And some have travelled so far that hardly anyone remembers where they started.

0:36The obvious ones: the mouse

Let's start with the easy ones. The mouse. It's small, it sits on your desk, and the early ones had a cord coming out of the back. In the 1960s, the team that built it thought the cord looked like a tail. Nobody remembers who said it first. But the name stuck. It wasn't a plan. It just looked like a mouse.

1:00The desktop

The desktop is a different story. Your computer has files, inside folders, on a desktop. And when you're done with them, they go in the bin. None of that is an accident. In the 1970s, researchers at Xerox designed screens that deliberately looked like an office. Apple later added the bin. Instead of teaching people a whole new vocabulary, they gave them one they already knew. Desk. Folder. Document. Bin. There's no desk inside your computer. And there's definitely no tiny bin. But you know exactly what they mean. That was the whole point.

And that idea, using something you can see to explain something you can't, is behind most of these words.

1:56The cloud

Take the cloud. Long before anyone kept photos in it, engineers drawing network diagrams had a problem. Somewhere in the middle was a huge, complicated network they didn't need to show. So they drew a cloud. A fuzzy shape that meant: there's a lot going on in here, and you don't need to know what. That's still exactly what it means. Your files in the cloud aren't in the sky. They're on somebody else's computers, probably in a data centre, possibly on another continent. The cloud is simply the part you don't need to see.

2:34The web

The web is a picture too. When Tim Berners-Lee named the World Wide Web in 1990, he was describing a shape. Documents linked to other documents, with no centre and no edge. Like a spider's web, every thread leads to another thread. And, just like a fly, you can easily get stuck in it for three hours.

2:57Packets

Then there's the packet. In the mid-1960s, a British scientist, Donald Davies, needed a name for small pieces of data travelling across a network. He chose packet, a small parcel, partly because the word would translate easily into other languages. And it fits. When you watch a video online, you're not receiving it in one piece. You're getting hundreds of thousands of tiny parcels, each with an address on the front.

3:26Payload

Which brings us to another word from the world of deliveries. Load. A truck carries a load. But not everything a truck hauls around is cargo someone has paid to send. There's fuel, there's the driver, there's the truck itself. The part that actually earns money is the pay load. The payload. Rockets kept the word. A rocket is mostly fuel, and the payload is the small part on top that the whole launch is for. A network packet is no different. Every packet has a label, with an address and delivery instructions. And a payload. The bit you actually wanted.

4:09Stream and buffer

Now let's move from the road to the water. A stream flows. You don't have to wait for the whole river to arrive before you can drink from it. That's streaming. The film starts playing while the rest of it is still on the way. But water doesn't always arrive evenly, and neither does data. So we need a buffer. A buffer is the thing on the end of a railway carriage that takes the jolt when it bumps into the next one. A data buffer does the same job. It absorbs the bumps, so the video doesn't stop every time the connection stumbles.

4:44Pipeline

And when data has to pass through several stages, one after another, programmers call it a pipeline. That one was plumbing from the start. In 1964, Doug McIlroy at Bell Labs wrote that programs should connect like lengths of garden hose. Data flows, gets buffered, and runs through pipes. Somewhere along the way, computing quietly turned into plumbing.

5:10Padding

And then there's padding. Padding is what you put around something to protect it. A padded jacket. A padded envelope. A padded chair. On a web page, padding is the space between the edge of a box and the words inside it. Almost every button you've ever clicked has a little cushion around the text. And outside the padding is the margin, a word borrowed straight from the edge of a page.

So far, all of these words have something in common. You can see the picture behind them. Once someone points it out, it's obvious.

5:48Back to the log

But some words have travelled so far that the original picture has completely faded.

Remember the log? Five hundred years ago, a ship had no speedometer. Just the sea going past. So sailors threw a piece of wood overboard, tied to a long rope. The wood stayed put in the water while the ship sailed away from it. Later, knots were tied along the rope at regular intervals. A sailor counted how many knots slipped overboard while a small sand glass emptied. That number was the ship's speed. And that's why we still measure the speed of ships in knots. The piece of wood was called the log. Every reading was written down, first on a board, then in a book. The log book.

Over time, the log book grew into a full diary of the voyage. Speed, weather, position, anything that happened on board. And when computers needed a place to write down everything that happened, the name was already waiting. Every line in a server log is a distant descendant of a sailor counting knots on a rope. Just with less wood.

7:00Booting

Booting is even stranger. In the nineteenth century, there was a joke about pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps. The little loops at the top of a boot. It was meant to sound absurd. You can't lift yourself off the ground by pulling on your own boots. It was a way of describing something impossible. But a computer has to do something very similar. When you switch it on, it needs a program to load its programs. And loading programs is a program's job. So it starts with a tiny piece of code that loads a slightly bigger one, which loads a bigger one still, until the whole system is running.

The computer pulls itself up by its own bootstraps. Engineers called it bootstrapping, and then just booting. A joke about the impossible became the first thing every computer does when you switch it on.

7:56The bug

And then there's the bug. You've probably heard the story. In 1947, the team working on the Harvard Mark II computer found a moth stuck in a relay. They taped it into the computer's notes, with a comment: "First actual case of bug being found." Into the log book, of course. But look at that comment again. First actual case. It's a joke. And it only works if everyone already knew what a bug was. And they did. In 1878, Thomas Edison was writing about the bugs in his inventions, the little faults and difficulties that show up when you build something new.

So the moth didn't give us the word. It was just the first bug you could actually hold.

8:44Daemon

Now here's a word that sounds much more sinister than it is. Daemon. On many computers, little programs run quietly in the background, handling the mail, the printer, the clock. Programmers call them daemons. Not because they're evil. The name came from MIT in the 1960s, and it was borrowed from physics. In 1867, the physicist James Clerk Maxwell imagined a tiny being, later called Maxwell's demon, guarding a little door in a wall that split a box of gas in two. It let the fast molecules through one way, and the slow ones the other, quietly sorting them, never resting.

A tireless little helper, working in the background. That's exactly what the programmers at MIT wanted their programs to be. And in ancient Greek, a daimon wasn't a devil at all. It was a spirit that watched over you.

9:41Worm

And finally, the worm. A computer worm is a program that copies itself from one computer to the next, all on its own. The name came from a novel. The Shockwave Rider, from 1975, in which a programmer turns a tapeworm loose in a giant network. A few years later, researchers at Xerox wrote real programs that spread between the machines on their network. And they borrowed the name from the book. The same lab, as it happens, that gave us the desktop.

A log that was a piece of wood. Booting, which began as a joke. A bug that was older than computers. A daemon that was a physicist's helper. A worm from a science fiction novel.

10:28The answer

And that's really the answer to the question in the title. Almost everything a computer does is invisible. You can't see a packet, or a buffer, or a cloud. So we name the invisible world after the visible one. A desk. A river. A truck. A parcel. A rope with knots in it. And after a while, we forget they were ever metaphors. We didn't invent a language for computers. We borrowed one from the world we already understood. This turned out to be so much fun that it would be a shame to stop here.
