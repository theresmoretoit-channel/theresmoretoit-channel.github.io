Why Do Banks Still Run on 60-Year-Old Code?

11 min2026-10-25

COBOL is a programming language from 1959. So why haven't banks replaced it, when they have the money and the engineers? This video looks at why a rewrite is not a translation, how a meeting at the Pentagon gave business its common language, and three real cases: a British bank that tried to leave its old system, an Australian bank that managed it, and a pandemic crisis in New Jersey where everyone blamed COBOL.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Add one penny

Nine hundred and ninety-nine pounds and ninety-nine pence. Add one penny. The answer is zero. No error message. No crash. Just zero, written quietly into the account. In COBOL, when the field for a result is too small to hold it, the extra digit can simply disappear. Unless the program asks to be warned, the calculation carries on. It doesn't round, either. Two pounds, thirty-four and a half pence, stored to the penny, becomes two thirty-four. Write one extra word, ROUNDED, and it becomes two thirty-five. Neither of these is a bug. They're rules. And many of the world's banks still run on rules like these.

0:46A language from 1959

COBOL is a programming language from 1959. Not every program written in it is that old. But the language is, and so are its habits. So why haven't banks replaced it? They have the money. They have the engineers. To find out, we'll look at three real cases. A bank that tried to leave its old system, one that managed it, and a crisis where everyone blamed COBOL. But first, that missing half penny.

1:17Why not just rewrite it?

Say you rewrite that banking program in a modern language. You'd probably fix those rules. Overflow becomes an error. Half a penny gets rounded up. Cleaner. Safer. And now, wherever half a penny turns up, your program disagrees with the old one. Modern languages can do exact decimal maths. Java has a type built for it. But even there, the rounding rule is a choice. One common setting rounds the half up: two thirty-five. Another rounds to the nearest even digit: two thirty-four. Both are correct. Only one matches. And with ordinary floating point, the kind most code uses by default, it gets stranger. Zero point one plus zero point two isn't zero point three.

So replacing COBOL isn't translation. It's finding every rule like this, in millions of lines, and keeping each one exactly as it was.

How did it end up there in the first place?

2:22How COBOL got there

In May 1959, the US Department of Defense called a meeting at the Pentagon. Computer makers, government agencies and big companies came, all with the same problem. Every computer had its own language. A payroll program written for one machine was useless on another. A committee designed a common language for business. Its biggest influence was FLOW-MATIC, from Grace Hopper's team at Remington Rand. And in December 1960, essentially the same COBOL program ran on two computers from rival manufacturers. That was the whole point.

It was designed so people could follow it. ADD PAYMENT TO ACCOUNT-BALANCE. You can probably guess what that does. And it stored money the way people write it: as decimal digits. The decimal point doesn't even have to be stored. The field's description says where it goes: the last two digits are pence. In 1964, IBM launched the System/360. Its design included instructions for decimal arithmetic. And IBM has designed its mainframes to stay compatible ever since. Many older programs can still run on today's machines without being rewritten.

So there was rarely a moment that forced anyone to move. Every year the code kept working, and every year it collected more rules. New products, new taxes, new regulations, new exceptions.

3:56How much is still running

Nobody has counted how much COBOL is still running worldwide. But IBM says forty-five of the world's fifty biggest banks still use its mainframes. And some owners have counted. In 2017, America's Social Security Administration said its systems held over sixty million lines of COBOL. In 2025, US government auditors listed the eleven federal systems most in need of replacement. They were between twenty-three and sixty years old, and cost about seven hundred and fifty million dollars a year to run. So the code is old, it's everywhere, and it's full of rules nobody wrote down. Surely the danger is in keeping it.

4:41TSB: a bank leaves

Let's see what happens when a bank leaves.

In 2018, a British bank called TSB had about five million customers, and a problem. Its systems were rented from Lloyds Banking Group, a competitor, and the fees were set to rise sharply. So TSB decided to move everything onto a new platform, built by its new owner, the Spanish bank Sabadell. One weekend in April. On Sunday evening, the new system was switched on. On Monday, there were about nine hundred thousand attempts to log in. Only about one internet banking login in ten succeeded. Payments failed. Some customers logged in and saw other people's accounts.

The worst lasted about a week. TSB didn't get back to business as usual until December. The fallout cost around three hundred and thirty million pounds in 2018 alone. Regulators later added nearly forty-nine million in fines. And here's the strange part. The customer data arrived intact. Both official reports say so. What failed was the new platform around it. Two data centres, set up differently. Performance tested on only one of them, with the targets lowered after the tests didn't pass. And there was no going back. Once the data had moved, returning to the old system was, in the regulator's words, "essentially impossible".

TSB wasn't escaping a broken system. It was leaving one that ran, because of who owned it and what it cost. Leaving was the dangerous part.

6:28Commonwealth Bank: leaving slowly

So is leaving always a disaster? In 2008, Australia's Commonwealth Bank set out to replace core systems that had served it for more than forty-five years. The plan: about five hundred and eighty million Australian dollars, and four years. It took five. Press reports later put the total at roughly one point one billion, as the project expanded. But it didn't move everything in one weekend. Deposit accounts went first, in 2010. Business accounts followed in 2011, and loans in 2012. Each step was something the bank could watch, check and learn from before it bet on the next one.

At the end, the bank says, it became the first major Australian bank with real-time banking, around the clock. So it can be done. But look at what it took. Five years, a much bigger bill, and patience. Modernisation isn't a purchase. It's a project, with risks of its own.

7:32New Jersey: everyone blamed COBOL

Now for the crisis where everyone blamed COBOL. March 2020. As the pandemic shut down New Jersey, new unemployment claims jumped from under ten thousand in a week to over a hundred and fifty-five thousand. Sixteen times as many, in seven days. The unemployment office couldn't keep up. In early April, the governor asked for volunteers. Not only health workers, but people who knew COBOL. The state's systems, he said, were more than forty years old. The story spread fast. Ancient code had collapsed under the pandemic. But when the state auditor looked into it later, it found no evidence that the delays had been caused by the computer systems.

Separately, reporters who looked at the error messages people were seeing traced many of them to the website in front, written in a much newer language, Java. The old code behind it kept running. So why did the state need COBOL programmers? Because Congress was changing the rules. New benefits, new amounts, new people who qualified, and every change had to go into the code, fast. The problem wasn't that the code was old. It was that few people could change it, and that the phone lines and the staff were overwhelmed as well.

8:57Three cases

Three cases. A bank that left a system that still ran, and whose migration failed. A bank that left carefully, and succeeded. And a crisis where the old code was blamed, and nobody could show it was the cause.

That doesn't mean old systems never fail. They do. They get expensive, the people who understand them retire, and waiting has a cost of its own. But age is easy to see, and the real risks aren't. Rules nobody wrote down. Knowledge in a few people's heads. And the moment you switch over.

9:34Wrap it, replace it piece by piece

That's why many banks don't replace the old core in one go. They wrap it. The app on your phone is new, but it may be talking to something much older. And they replace it piece by piece. In 2004, the software writer Martin Fowler named this after the strangler fig, a rainforest plant that grows around a tree until it can stand on its own. The old system keeps working while the new one grows around it, one job at a time, until nothing depends on the old one.

10:07Back to the half penny

Which brings us back to that half penny. Somewhere, a program may still drop it, because someone decided it should, decades ago. Replacing that program means knowing that, and everything like it.

The old system isn't necessarily what fails. Sometimes, it's what replaces it. Because replacing the technology isn't the same as removing the risk. Sometimes it just moves the risk into a single weekend in April.

So here's a question for you. If a system has worked for forty years, when is the right time to replace it? And have you ever had to replace one yourself? Let me know in the comments.
