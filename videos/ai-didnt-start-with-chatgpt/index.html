How Old Is AI, Really?

The full narration of the video.

0:00A box on a web page

At the end of November 2022, a web page appeared with a small box on it. You typed a question. And a computer answered. Not with a pre-written reply. With a sentence. It could write an email. Explain mathematics. Translate a page. Write code. And it could hold something that felt uncomfortably like a conversation. It was called ChatGPT. And for millions of people, this was the moment artificial intelligence began.

Except a computer had done something like this before. Fifty-six years earlier.

0:39ELIZA

In 1966, a researcher at MIT called Joseph Weizenbaum published a program called ELIZA. You could type: "My boyfriend says I'm depressed much of the time." And ELIZA would answer: "I am sorry to hear you are depressed." And you could carry on. And so would the program. ELIZA was not intelligent. It was not a neural network. It understood nothing at all. It matched keywords in what you typed and handed the rest back as a question. The whole script fitted in the appendix of a journal paper. People talked to it anyway. Weizenbaum's own secretary had watched him build it. She knew exactly what it was.

After a few exchanges with it, she asked him to leave the room. She wanted to be alone with it.

Which leaves a question that the next half century kept walking into. How little does a machine actually need to seem intelligent?

1:43Can machines think?

Somebody had already asked a version of that. Sixteen years earlier. In 1950, the British mathematician Alan Turing opened a paper with a question. "Can machines think?" And then he did something clever. He decided the question was too vague to answer, and swapped it for a different one. Put a person and a machine at the far end of a conversation. If you cannot tell which is which, does it still matter whether the machine is really thinking? It is an elegant argument. And ELIZA is what happens when you take it literally.

Because ELIZA convinced people in 1966, and there was nothing behind it at all.

2:28A phrase nobody had written down

Six years after Turing's paper, a group of scientists spent the summer at Dartmouth College. Their proposal used a phrase that nobody had written down before. Artificial intelligence. And their ambition was enormous. If we could describe human intelligence precisely enough, we could program a machine to reproduce it. A second idea had been around even longer, and it pointed the opposite way.

2:56A neuron on paper

In 1943, a neurophysiologist and a logician wrote down a simple model of a biological neuron. It took a few numbers in. Gave each one a weight. Added them up. And decided whether to fire. On its own it can do almost nothing. Its weights were fixed by hand. Two years after Dartmouth, a psychologist built one that could work them out for itself. And the idea underneath that was this. Maybe we do not program intelligence at all. Maybe we build enough simple units, wire them together, and let the system learn.

3:35None of it worked

None of it worked. Not because the ideas were wrong. Because the machines were nowhere near ready. Computers were slow. Memory was tiny. And there was almost nothing to learn from. Training a large network was not difficult. It was impossible. And there was a second problem, which was worse, because nobody had predicted it. A computer could do arithmetic faster than every human who had ever lived. But ask it to do something a five-year-old does without thinking about it, and it fell apart. Recognise a dog in a photograph. For a person, instant. For a computer, hopeless.

Eventually the money noticed. Funding was cut. The excitement faded. The field went through long stretches that later earned a name. AI winters. Artificial intelligence did not disappear. It just stopped being interesting.

4:35Then three things changed

What changed was not a brilliant new idea. From the 1990s onwards, computers got dramatically faster and storage got cheap. And the world began producing enormous amounts of digital data. Web pages. Photographs. Scanned books. Video. Text. For the first time there was something for a machine to learn from. And the question quietly turned over. Not: "How do we program all of the rules into the computer?" But: "What if we hand it the data and let it work out the rules?"

5:11Deep Blue

In 1997, a machine called Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov, the world chess champion. It was enormous news. And it is worth being precise about what it proved. Deep Blue could not hold a conversation. It could not recognise a dog. It could not learn anything. It was extraordinarily good at exactly one thing. ELIZA looked intelligent and was not. Deep Blue was superhuman and still was not. Both of them broke the same assumption. That if something looks like thinking, there must be thinking behind it.

5:49The dog

Now, the dog. In 2012, a neural network called AlexNet was pointed at more than a million photographs. It was not a new idea. Neural networks were older than most of the people working on them. What was new was everything around it. Enough data. Enough computing power. Better ways to train it. It did not just win. It won by a margin that made the older approaches look finished. The thing a five-year-old could do and a computer could not, a computer could suddenly do. And the question changed again. Not "can neural networks work?" But "how far do they go?"

6:32The transformer

In 2017, a paper came out of Google with an odd title. "Attention Is All You Need." It described a new architecture for neural networks, called the transformer. It let a model read a whole sentence at once, and work out which words belonged with which. Almost everything you have heard of since then is built on it. GPT. BERT. Claude. Gemini. And still, outside the field, nobody had noticed anything.

7:02GPT

OpenAI began building a family of models called GPT.

Each one was larger than the last and better at the same trick. Given some text, guess what comes next. By GPT-3 that trick had turned into something nobody quite expected. It could answer a question. Finish an argument. Write an essay. Generate code. And almost nobody used it. It was a research tool. You needed an account, some documentation, and a reason. The technology was there. The door was not.

7:4230 November 2022

On the 30th of November 2022, OpenAI put a text box on a web page. That is close to the whole story. It was not a new kind of artificial intelligence. It was not the first neural network. And it certainly was not the first chatbot. ELIZA was doing that job fifty-six years earlier. What changed was that you no longer had to understand any of it. You typed. It answered. Five days later, a million people had signed up.

8:17AI did not start with ChatGPT

When people say AI started with ChatGPT, it is a little like saying the internet started with Google. It did not. Google just made it enormously easier to use. And underneath ChatGPT there is a list, and every item on it took decades. Computing power. Huge amounts of data. Neural networks. Better ways of training them. Transformers. And finally, a simple interface.

8:47So when did it start?

So when did artificial intelligence actually begin? 2022? No. 2017? Not really. 1956? Closer. But even in 1956 nobody was starting from nothing. The models of neurons were already written down. Turing had already asked whether the question made sense. And people had been telling stories about artificial minds for a very long time before that. There is probably no first day.

9:23Back to a secretary at MIT

Think about that secretary at MIT in 1966, asking to be left alone with a few pages of pattern matching. The machines have become unimaginably better. We have not become much harder to convince.

So perhaps ChatGPT was not the beginning of the age of AI. Perhaps it was simply the moment everybody finally noticed. And perhaps the interesting question was never when we invented this. It is how long it takes us to work out what we have built.
