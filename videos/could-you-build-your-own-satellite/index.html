How Do You Build Your Own Satellite?

13 min2026-09-28

But building the box turns out to be the easy part. The hard part is everything else: what it costs, fitting it all into ten centimetres, surviving a rocket launch and the vacuum of space, running on its own with no one there to fix it, talking to the ground legally — and, in the end, clearing itself out of orbit.

CubeSats didn't make space easy. They made it standardised. And that turns out to be a surprisingly big change.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A crazy idea

Imagine you have a crazy idea. You want to build your own satellite. Not a billion-dollar communications satellite. Something small. A box about the size of a loaf of bread. Inside: a computer, solar panels, a radio, a few sensors. Maybe even a camera. You build it in your garage. And then somehow… you put it into orbit. Sounds impossible. But it isn't.

0:27Meet the CubeSat

People have been doing almost exactly this for years. They're called CubeSats.

And the surprising part is that the hardest thing isn't building the satellite. It's everything else.

0:41Standard sizes

The basic unit is called 1U — roughly a ten-centimetre cube. Stack units together and you get 2U, 3U, 6U and larger. The idea is simple. Instead of designing a new spacecraft from scratch, you reuse standardised sizes and standardised interfaces. And suddenly, space is open to universities, start-ups, and maybe even you.

1:15What is inside

So what would our little satellite actually contain? First, it needs a computer. Something has to run the software, control the hardware, and decide what to do. Then there's power. Solar panels make electricity. Batteries store it for when the satellite slips into Earth's shadow. Then communication. A radio. An antenna. And, back on Earth, a ground station listening. Then sensors. Temperature. Voltage. Current. Acceleration. Magnetic field. And some way of telling which way the satellite is pointing. Because in orbit, saying "up" is surprisingly hard.

1:57What it costs

But before we build one, there's an awkward question. How much does this little box cost? The answer is… more than it looks like it should. A simple 1U CubeSat can easily cost tens of thousands of dollars — just in flight hardware. The power system. Solar panels. Batteries. The computer. The radio. The attitude control. The structure. And the payload. Then come the things that aren't on the parts list. Testing. Integration. Documentation. Ground-station operations. Licensing. And finally… the launch. A rideshare for a tiny CubeSat can cost tens of thousands. Bigger missions and bigger CubeSats reach hundreds of thousands. And that's just getting the thing into space.

2:47Fitting it all in

And now an even more ridiculous problem. Remember all those parts? How on earth does all of that fit inside one tiny box?

In space, there's no room for a random pile of electronics. Every centimetre matters. Every gram matters. Every watt matters. The satellite is a three-dimensional puzzle that has to survive a rocket launch.

3:15Off-the-shelf parts

So where do you get all these parts? You don't have to design everything yourself. There's a whole industry selling components for small satellites: computers, power systems, radios, antennas and solar panels. There are even marketplaces built specifically for CubeSat parts. And the parts are standardised, too. Many share one board format and simply stack together, so pieces from different makers fit into one unit. Companies like EnduroSat and GomSpace sell them, and a single subsystem can cost anywhere from a few thousand to tens of thousands of dollars.

And these aren't exotic processors. One current CubeSat computer uses an ARM Cortex-M7, with familiar interfaces — SPI, I²C, UART and CAN. And it isn't a custom space chip. It's a real, off-the-shelf board you could hold in your hand. A spacecraft needs something more important than raw speed: predictable behaviour, low power and reliability. More demanding missions can use ARM Cortex-A processors — and even Linux. But it isn't fast. A typical CubeSat computer runs at a couple of hundred megahertz. Your laptop runs at four or five gigahertz, with many more cores. It trades raw speed for using almost no power.

4:39Writing the software

And if you're a programmer, this is where it gets interesting. You don't write some mysterious "space language". You write embedded software. Often in C or C++. It might run directly on the hardware, or on a real-time operating system like FreeRTOS, Zephyr or RTEMS. There are even open frameworks built for spacecraft. NASA's F Prime, for example, is a flight-software framework for embedded and spaceflight systems. So your code might look something like this. But there's one huge difference from a normal computer. You don't get to SSH into it. You can't attach a debugger. And if your software crashes… your computer is a few hundred kilometres up.

So it needs watchdogs, safe modes, fault detection and automatic recovery. It has to fix some problems without you there to help.

And one big reason it needs all that is radiation. In space, cosmic rays can flip a bit in memory, or even knock out a chip. There's no heavy shielding — that would weigh far too much. Instead, the computer watches itself, corrects errors, and resets when something goes wrong.

5:56Surviving space

But if you actually want to launch it… things get serious. A part that works on your desk isn't automatically ready for a rocket. It has to survive vibration, vacuum, temperature extremes and radiation.

6:12Which way is up?

Now, back to that problem of knowing which way the satellite is pointing. Picture it tumbling through space. No floor. No ceiling. GPS can tell you where you are… but not which way you're facing. So satellites combine gyroscopes, magnetometers and sun sensors to work out their attitude. And then they have to change it. One common trick is a magnetorquer. It pushes against Earth's magnetic field and makes a tiny torque. It's not a rocket engine. It doesn't move the satellite. It just turns it. And that's enough to point the satellite exactly where you want.

6:55The mission

But none of this is the reason we built the satellite. We need a mission. That's the payload. Measure radiation. Photograph Earth. Study the atmosphere. Test a new sensor. Or simply prove a new technology works. The satellite itself is mostly there to keep the payload alive. Power it. Point it. Keep it warm. Store its data. And send it home. When your desktop locks up, you restart it. When your satellite locks up… you have a very expensive object silently orbiting Earth.

7:33Testing, a lot of testing

So let's say we built it. Everything works… The computer boots… The radio talks. The panels make power. The payload runs… Can we launch it? …Not yet. A satellite has to prove it can survive the launch. That means testing. A lot of testing. First, bolt it to a giant shaker. Then shake it, hard… That's vibration testing. Rocket launches are not gentle. It has to survive noise, vibration and shock without anything coming loose or breaking. Every screw matters. Every connector matters. Every piece of cable matters.

Then comes vacuum. On Earth, air carries heat away. In space, there is no air. One side can get extremely hot, another extremely cold, and the electronics still have to stay inside their limits. So the satellite needs thermal straps, coatings, insulation and carefully chosen materials. Satellites don't have air conditioning. They have physics. And the battery gets interesting. Charge it with sunlight. Then the satellite slips into Earth's shadow, and the panels make almost nothing. The battery keeps it alive. Then back into sunlight. Charge. Discharge. Charge. Discharge. Again and again. For years. One mistake in power could quietly end the mission.

9:00Talking to it

And now, how do you talk to it? You need a radio. An antenna. And a frequency you're legally allowed to use. And a ground station. The satellite doesn't have the internet. It just flies overhead and transmits. Your ground station catches the signal. The computer decodes it. And suddenly you have data from your own spacecraft. Imagine opening a terminal and seeing this. Except the machine printing those numbers… is hundreds of kilometres over your head.

9:34Getting to orbit

So how do we actually get it up there? You could build a rocket… Please don't… You don't need one. CubeSats fly as secondary payloads. A rocket is already going up. It has spare room. Your satellite rides along in a standard deployer. No personal rocket required. And now it's sitting on top of a rocket. You can't touch it. You can't open the box. You can't plug in a cable. The rocket launches. About nine minutes later, you're in orbit. The deployer releases your satellite. It springs free and begins to tumble. But how does it know it's out? … Spring-loaded switches, pressed in while it sat in the deployer, pop free the moment it's ejected, and the computer boots. Then, by the rules, it stays silent for about half an hour. Only then does it power up its radio, unfold its antennas, and finally call home. And somewhere on Earth… your ground station hears it.

You built a machine. And now that machine is orbiting the planet.

10:37The end of the mission

Except, we're not done. A dead satellite can't just stay up there forever. Left in orbit for centuries, it becomes space debris. That means modern missions increasingly have to plan for the end of the spacecraft's life. Ideally, the thin upper atmosphere slowly drags it down. Its orbit decays. It re-enters. And it burns up. The end of the mission… is part of the mission.

11:13So — could you?

So… could you build your own satellite? Technically, yes. You don't need to be NASA. You don't need to own a rocket. You don't even necessarily need a huge company. The satellite has to survive launch. Survive space. Communicate legally. Run on its own. And, in the end, clear out of orbit. All of that has to be proven before anyone puts it on a rocket.

CubeSats didn't make space easy. They made it standardised. Instead of building everything from scratch, you can build something that fits an existing standard. And that's a surprisingly big change. So somewhere, right now, there are tiny machines orbiting Earth. Built by universities, research teams, companies and students. Some carry cameras. Some measure radiation. Some test brand-new technology. And some are just experiments asking one question: "can we make this thing work in space?" And that's what makes building your own satellite so fascinating. Because the distance between you and space isn't a rocket anymore. It might be… a ten-centimetre box. And a very, very long checklist.
