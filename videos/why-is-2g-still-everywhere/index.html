2G Is Ancient. So Why Are Lifts, Cars and Alarms Still Using It?

14 min2026-09-02

Operators want to switch it off, and they have good reasons: radio spectrum is finite and modern networks use it far better. But the machines that depend on 2G were built to last twenty or thirty years, and nobody replaces a device that still works.

A study commissioned by the European Commission and published in May 2026 estimates that 64 million vehicles across the EU could lose eCall if the old networks are switched off without coordination. It recommends that each Member State keep at least one circuit-switched network alive until at least 2030.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Stuck between floors

Imagine you're in a lift. You press the button. The doors slide shut. The lift starts moving. Everything is perfectly normal. Until, one day, it isn't. The lift stops between floors. So you press the emergency button. You expect someone to answer. But nobody does. Not because the button is broken. Not because the lift has no electricity. But because the mobile network that the emergency phone uses… has been switched off. Welcome to the surprisingly complicated problem of turning off 2G. Because 2G isn't just something your old Nokia used twenty years ago. It is still quietly sitting inside an astonishing number of things.

Things we generally expect to work for decades. Things like lifts. Alarms. Industrial equipment. Meters. Cars. And, rather awkwardly… emergency systems.

1:012G is ancient

Let's start with the obvious question. Why are we still talking about 2G? GSM first appeared commercially in the early 1990s. It is ancient. By technology standards, it is practically a fossil. Today we have 4G. 5G. Gigabit mobile connections. We can stream 4K video from a phone while sitting on a train. So surely it makes sense to get rid of this ancient technology? Well… For your smartphone, probably. For a lift? Not so much.

1:38What a lift actually needs

A lift doesn't need 5G. It doesn't need Netflix. It doesn't need a gigabit connection. It doesn't even need the internet. It just needs to be able to say this. "Help. I'm stuck between floors." That's basically it. An emergency communicator in a lift might simply establish a voice call with a service centre. And GSM is perfectly capable of doing that. The same applies to many alarm systems. Some industrial equipment. Some tracking devices. Some meters. They don't need enormous bandwidth. They need a tiny, reliable communication channel. And that's exactly the sort of thing GSM was rather good at.

2:26Remember GPRS?

And then there was GPRS. If you were around in the early 2000s, you may remember it. GPRS was basically mobile data over GSM. And it was… slow. Very slow. We are talking about tens of kilobits per second. Today, a teenager would probably describe that as a network outage. But some of us actually used it. There were mobile data packages that allowed you to use GPRS as your internet connection. Yes. As your actual internet connection. Some of us knew no better. And somehow… we survived. The interesting thing is that GPRS is still perfectly adequate for some machines. A device might only need to send a few hundred bytes.

Something like this. Temperature, 21.4. Pressure, 1012. Status, OK. That's it. You don't need 5G for that. You barely need what we'd normally call the internet.

3:31Machines don't get replaced

And this is where things get interesting. 2G isn't just used by old phones. It is used by machines. And machines have a very annoying habit. If they work… nobody replaces them. A lift can stay in service for twenty or thirty years. An industrial machine can last even longer. An alarm system might be installed and then forgotten about. A meter can sit somewhere for fifteen years, quietly reporting its readings. And nobody is going to replace a perfectly working device just because the mobile industry has moved on to another generation of technology. Why would they? It works.

4:17The car problem

Now let's talk about cars. Modern cars contain an extraordinary amount of electronics. And some older cars have emergency communication systems based on 2G or 3G. This includes older versions of eCall. The European emergency call system can automatically contact 112 after a serious accident and transmit information about the vehicle. That's rather useful. But if the car's communication module only understands an old mobile network… then switching off that network creates a rather strange situation. The car still works. The engine works. The brakes work. The airbags work. The radio works.

You can drive it perfectly normally. But its emergency communication system may no longer be able to communicate. The car hasn't become obsolete. The network it depends on has.

5:17Just replace the module?

Now, the obvious answer is this. "Fine. Just replace the old module." And sometimes that is exactly what will happen. But it isn't always that simple. Imagine thousands of lifts. Thousands of alarm systems. Industrial equipment installed fifteen years ago. Vehicles that are perfectly roadworthy. Meters installed across an entire country. Who replaces all those devices? The manufacturer? The building owner? The lift maintenance company? The car owner? And what happens if the manufacturer doesn't even make the device anymore? Suddenly, a very cheap GSM modem has become a rather expensive engineering project.

6:06Why operators want it gone

Now, there is a perfectly good reason for wanting to switch off 2G. Radio spectrum is limited. Operators have only so much of it. Modern networks can use that spectrum much more efficiently. LTE and 5G can provide vastly more capacity and much better performance. So from the operator's point of view, keeping an entire old network running forever doesn't make much sense. Why maintain old equipment and dedicate spectrum to a technology that most people no longer use? That's a perfectly reasonable question. But there is another question. How much 2G do we actually need?

6:51How much 2G do we need?

Maybe the answer isn't this. "Keep the entire 2G network forever." Maybe it's this. "Keep a tiny part of it." We don't need 2G to carry millions of smartphone users. We don't need it for streaming video. We don't need it for normal mobile internet. We don't even need huge amounts of bandwidth. We might only need a small number of GSM channels. Enough for voice, SMS, basic GPRS data and emergency systems. Most of the spectrum could still go to 4G and 5G. The old network would become a tiny legacy layer.

And it wouldn't even have to belong to one particular operator. It doesn't have to be Vodafone. Or O2. Or Deutsche Telekom.

7:42EU Legacy

Imagine there was a shared European legacy service. Let's call it… EU Legacy. A lift doesn't care who owns the tower. It just needs its old modem to find the network and make the call. So perhaps the real problem isn't this. "We don't have enough capacity." Perhaps it's this. "We don't want to reserve any spectrum for it."

8:08This isn't hypothetical

And here's where it gets strange. This isn't entirely hypothetical. Regulators are aware of the problem. In May 2026, the European Commission published a study on exactly this. What happens to the machines when the old networks go. And the numbers are not small. If the networks are switched off without coordination, 64 million vehicles across the EU could lose eCall. 64 million. So the problem isn't this. "Somebody wants to keep their old Nokia." We're talking about infrastructure. Safety systems. Vehicles. Industrial equipment. And millions of devices whose owners may not even realise which mobile technology they use.

And what does that study recommend? Keep at least one circuit-switched network alive until at least 2030. In other words… keep a legacy network alive.

9:10We've done this before

And here's the funny thing. We've solved this sort of problem before. Think about the transition from black-and-white television to colour. When colour television arrived, we didn't say this. "Black-and-white televisions are obsolete. Throw them away." The colour television standards were designed so that older black-and-white receivers could still display the original image. New televisions could extract the additional colour information. One broadcast. Old equipment still works. New equipment gets the new features. That is what good backward compatibility looks like.

9:51Why mobile networks are harder

Now, to be fair, mobile networks are much more complicated. GSM and LTE are not simply two versions of the same technology. You can't just add a little bit of LTE to a GSM signal and expect a 1998 Nokia to understand it. It won't. But that doesn't mean we have to switch the old system off overnight. We could run both. Until the old equipment is finally gone.

10:21Legacy isn't bad

And perhaps that's the most important point. Legacy technology isn't necessarily bad technology. Sometimes it's simply old technology that is still doing its job. A GSM modem doesn't become dangerous because it is twenty years old. A lift doesn't stop being useful because its emergency telephone uses GSM. An industrial machine doesn't suddenly become obsolete because the telecom industry has invented 5G. We have a tendency to think of technology as a sequence. Old. Replace. New. But real infrastructure doesn't work like that. Real infrastructure overlaps. For decades.

11:062028 is not far away

And this is why the current timetable is interesting. Some European operators are planning to retire 2G around the end of this decade. That sounds like a long time. Until you remember what we're talking about. A smartphone from 2021 is already old by 2026 standards. But a lift from 2011? That's barely middle-aged. An industrial machine from 2005? It might still have another twenty years of useful life. That's the mismatch. Mobile networks change much faster than the infrastructure that uses them.

11:47The boring solution

And perhaps the best solution isn't some revolutionary new technology. Maybe it's something incredibly boring. Just one question. "Does it still work?" Yes. Good. Leave it alone.

12:01One tiny message

Technology doesn't always have to mean destroying the previous generation. Sometimes the smartest thing you can do is build the new system… and leave a tiny door open for the old one. Because somewhere out there is a lift. There is probably an alarm. There is probably an industrial machine. There is definitely an old car. And inside all of them there may be a little GSM modem made fifteen years ago. Nobody thinks about it. Nobody talks about it. It doesn't stream video. It doesn't browse the web. It doesn't download apps. It has one job. To send a tiny message when something goes wrong. Something like this. "Help. I'm here."

And honestly… I think that's a pretty good reason to keep a few GSM channels alive. Because the most impressive technology isn't necessarily the one that replaces everything that came before. Sometimes… it's the one that lets the old stuff keep working until we're actually ready to let it go. 2G may be ancient. But perhaps the right question isn't this. "Why are we still using it?" Perhaps it's this. "What happens if we turn it off before we're ready?"
