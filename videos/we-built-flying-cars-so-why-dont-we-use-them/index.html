We Built Flying Cars. So Why Don't We Use Them?

9 min2026-11-04

This video starts with just one flying car: why a car and a plane want almost opposite things, the 1947 flying car whose wing you were meant to rent at the airport, how much more power it takes to hover than to drive, why a helicopter gets away with it, what batteries can and can't do, and who would actually fly the thing. Then it puts a few thousand of them over one city, and keeps going.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A car that's allowed to fly

This car has a certificate that says it's allowed to fly. Slovak aviation authorities issued it in 2022, after seventy hours in the air and more than two hundred take-offs. And it's not the first. In 1956, the American government certified a car that could fly. Six were built. Not enough people wanted one, and it never went into production. We were promised flying cars. The strange thing is, we built them. Again and again. So why doesn't anyone use them?

Building a flying car was never the hard part. The hard part starts the moment you have more than one.

0:44Start with one

But let's start with one. A car and a plane want almost opposite things. A car has to be strong and heavy enough to survive a crash. A plane has to be as light as possible. A car has to fit in a lane. A plane needs wings wider than the road. So every flying car is a compromise. The wings fold, or come off, or get left behind. And it still needs a runway. The Dutch flying car that's road-legal needs a hundred and eighty metres of it just to take off. So to fly to work, you'd first drive to an airfield.

In 1949, the aircraft maker William Piper put it in one sentence. The roadable plane, he said, is neither a good plane nor a good automobile.

1:36Rent the wing at the airport

Two years earlier, an American company had tried to get around that. Its flying car came in two halves. You'd buy the car, and rent the wing at the airport. It flew for the first time in November 1947. Three days later, it ran low on fuel and had to make a forced landing on mud flats near San Diego. The story goes that the pilot had checked the wrong fuel gauge, the one for the car. Whether or not that's true, it shows the problem. Two machines in one body means two of everything to get right.

2:13Straight up, from a street or a roof

So engineers had a better idea. Skip the runway. Take off straight up, from a street, or a roof. That's what most flying cars today really are. Not cars with wings, but small aircraft that rise vertically.

2:28Where physics sends the bill

And that's where physics sends the bill. At a hundred kilometres an hour, an ordinary car needs about thirteen kilowatts. Its weight is carried by the road. The engine only has to keep it moving. A machine that hovers has to carry all of that weight itself, by throwing air downwards, every second. For something the size of a car, that takes somewhere between twenty and forty times as much power. And the smaller the rotors, the worse it gets. A helicopter does the same job with about ten times the power of a car. But only because its rotor is ten metres across.

So it's efficient exactly because it could never fit on a road. To keep a car in the air cheaply, we'd need something completely new. Something like antigravity. And that's far beyond anything we know how to build.

3:23Batteries

And if the power comes from batteries, there's a second problem. A kilogram of petrol holds about fifty times as much energy as a kilogram of today's battery packs. Electric motors waste much less of it, but even then, the battery still delivers only about a fifteenth as much. A fuel tank gets lighter as it empties. A battery doesn't.

3:48Who's flying it?

Then there's the question the old illustrations never asked. Who's flying it? A private pilot's licence takes at least forty hours in the air, and in practice usually more. In the United States, there are around fourteen hundred licensed drivers for every private pilot. So there are really only three ways to give everyone a flying car. Everyone learns to fly. Someone on the ground flies it for them. Or it flies itself. The second one runs out of people. In a NASA simulation, one person on the ground could safely look after three aircraft.

Which leaves the third. In China, a pilotless air taxi has been certified since 2023. Even so, it hasn't carried a single paying passenger yet. So the flying car may need more than better engines. It needs software we'd trust with the sky above our heads. And so far, we've only talked about one machine.

4:52A few thousand over one city

Now put a few thousand of them over one city. When a car breaks down, you pull over. In the air, there's nowhere to pull over. Antigravity would help here too. You could pull over onto a hard shoulder in the sky. You just couldn't get out and walk. Per hour of travel, private flying is about fifty times deadlier than driving. And over a city, it isn't only the people on board who are at risk. It's everyone underneath, who never chose to fly at all.

5:26Noise, and somewhere to land

Then there's the noise. The newest air taxis really are much quieter than helicopters. But even a quiet aircraft, times a thousand, over the street where you live, every morning and every evening.

And every one of them has to land somewhere. São Paulo has one of the biggest helicopter fleets of any city, with about four hundred helicopters and around two hundred and sixty helipads. That's what it takes for just a few hundred machines.

5:58Make it millions

So now, the last step. Make it millions. London has more than two and a half million cars. If just one in a hundred became a flying car, that's over twenty-six thousand aircraft over one city. That's about as many as are in the air over the entire planet at any one moment. At its busiest, the whole of the United States handles about five and a half thousand flights in the air at the same time. It takes fourteen thousand air traffic controllers to do it. And every one of those flights has an assigned route and altitude, and a plan filed in advance.

A city full of flying cars would need all of that, for trips to the supermarket.

6:43The real answer

So that's the real answer. A flying car is a perfectly good idea for one person. It just stops working when everyone has one.

6:55A smaller dream

Which is why the biggest companies building them today have quietly changed the plan. They're not selling flying cars. They're building air taxis. A small number of aircraft, flown by professional pilots, on a few carefully chosen routes. In Dubai, Joby plans to fly passengers between four landing sites, one of them at the airport. But even this smaller dream is hard. Two of Europe's best-known air taxi companies, Lilium and Volocopter, filed for insolvency within two months of each other. And Airbus paused its own project, saying battery technology wasn't ready yet.

So far, nowhere in the world is an air taxi like this carrying paying passengers.

7:42Where the flying car belongs

If they do take off, they'll look less like the future we were promised, and more like something we already have. A helicopter service. Just quieter, and maybe cheaper.

Maybe that's where the flying car belonged all along. We imagined it as a replacement for the car. But its real place may be somewhere else entirely. Not in every garage, but on a handful of routes, for the trips where flying really is worth the cost. We didn't fail to build flying cars. We built them, and found out what they're good for.

8:21A question for you

So, would you actually trust a flying car above your city? And if not, what would it take? Let me know in the comments.
