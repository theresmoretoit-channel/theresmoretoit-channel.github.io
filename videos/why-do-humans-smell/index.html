Why Do Humans Smell?

The full narration of the video.

0:00Your sweat does not smell

Your sweat does not smell. Fresh out of the gland, what comes out under your arms has almost no smell at all. What you smell is what happens to it afterwards. And your body does not make it. You have two kinds of sweat gland. The ones spread over your whole skin are there to cool you down. That sweat is almost entirely water. It is not the problem. It never was. The other kind sits in your armpits and your groin. They switch on at puberty. And what they push out is a large, heavy molecule with no smell whatsoever. It is a package. A sulphur atom, wrapped up so that it cannot fly.

And you cannot smell a package. A smell has to reach your nose, which means it has to travel. Then a bacterium finds it. It lives all over your skin. Here it has something to work on. And it carries the right tools. One enzyme trims the wrapping. A second enzyme cuts the bond holding the sulphur in place. And the thing that floats away is the smell. So your body makes a component. The bacteria on your skin finish the job. You are not the factory. You are the supplier.

The finished article travels well.

1:25A few molecules in every billion

Some odour molecules register at a few parts per billion. That is a ratio, not an amount. A few molecules in every billion, and your brain says: something smells. Hold on to that. We are coming back to it.

1:42Why broadcast at all?

That leaves an awkward question. Why would a body run a process whose entire output is thrown into the surrounding air? Because smell is older than soap. Older than cities. Older than words. For most of life on Earth it was never decoration. It was the message.

2:09What a smell tells other animals

Watch almost any other animal. A smell tells it who you are. Where you are. Which group you are from. Whether you are ready to mate. Whether that is food. Whether you are ill. That last one sounds like a stretch. It is not a stretch at all.

2:28The nurse who smelled Parkinson's

A Scottish nurse called Joy Milne noticed that her husband had begun to smell different. Woody. Musky. She mentioned it. Nothing came of it. Twelve years later he was diagnosed with Parkinson's. She only made the connection at a meeting of patients, where the whole room smelled of him. So researchers tested her. Twelve shirts. Six worn by patients, six by people without the disease. She picked out all six. And then she picked out a seventh, and would not back down. That person did not have Parkinson's. They were diagnosed nine months later.

The smell was traced to skin oil on the upper back. It had been sitting there for years, on an ordinary shirt, and nobody knew how to read it.

And she is only an extreme case of something completely ordinary.

3:29Everybody carries a different mixture

Every person carries a slightly different mixture of molecules. Part of it is your own chemistry. Part of it is the particular bacteria that happen to live on you. And part of it is what you ate, where you live, what you are wearing, and what you did today. So it is not really a smell. It is a signature.

3:51What the dog reads

And something out there is reading it. People reach for the word pheromone. In humans, not one has ever been found. A dog does not need one. Four dogs in Belfast were given breath and sweat from one person, calm, and then under pressure. They found the stressed sample in ninety-four per cent of seven hundred and twenty trials. And it goes further than detection. Human fear sweat raises a dog's heart rate and sends it looking for its owner. It never saw a face. To the dog, none of that is a failing. It is simply what today smells like.

And this is not a human trick. It is not even an animal trick.

4:37Sagebrush warns the plant beside it

Cut the leaves of a sagebrush and it releases chemicals into the air. A wild tobacco plant growing beside it then takes measurably less damage from grasshoppers and caterpillars. It was warned. No nerves. No brain. No nose. Just a molecule leaving one organism and altering another. The range is tiny. Under fifteen centimetres. It is a whisper, not a broadcast. But it is communication. It simply has no words in it.

5:11Why any of it disgusts us

So why does any of this disgust us? Because a molecule carries no opinion. Nothing inside it means "revolting". That part is added by you. And usually it is added for good reason. Rot. Waste. Infection. If something smells like it is decaying, not eating it is excellent advice. Disgust is a warning system, and it has kept an enormous number of people alive. Then something happened to that system.

We took the warning system and turned it into a rule of manners.

5:521919: the year somebody sold the problem

And unlike most rules of manners, we know who wrote this one. We know the year. In 1919 an advertising agency had an awkward client: a sweat-blocking liquid called Odorono. Sales were flat. A copywriter called James Webb Young went to find out why. The answer was inconvenient. Two women in three did not think it was about them. So he stopped selling the liquid and started selling the problem. The advertisement ran in Ladies' Home Journal. "Within the Curve of a Woman's Arm." Underneath, a subtitle. "A frank discussion of a subject too often avoided."

Two hundred readers cancelled their subscriptions. Colleagues stopped speaking to him. Sales rose by a hundred and twelve percent in a single year.

None of which invented the shame. People had minded the smell for centuries. Baths. Perfume. Soap. Long before anybody had a product to shift. What 1919 invented was the sale. An old, vague worry became something you could buy your way out of. And once smelling is a failure rather than a fact, there is no obvious place to stop.

7:11Washing, shaving, silver in the socks

We wash. We shave. We deodorise. We perfume. We wash a shirt that has been worn once. We build antibacterial metals into socks. An enormous amount of time and money, spent so that a body smells of nothing at all. Or, better still, of something that came out of a bottle.

7:33The pump, and the people it skips

And now remember the pump. The one that has to push the package out of the gland before a bacterium can get at it. In some people it barely works. It is inherited. And it is not rare. Hardly any package. Hardly any smell. Nothing to do with washing. Across much of East Asia that is not the exception. It is the majority.

8:00The test

So here is the test. If the smell itself were the problem, a place where most people hardly make it would not need the cure. Japan has a deodorant industry anyway. And Japanese has a word for offending somebody with your smell. Sumehara. Smell harassment. The pump behind all this comes down to a single gene. And that gene has a story of its own. That one is its own video. If it is up already, there is a link above and below.

Nobody's chemistry changed in 1919. The rule did. And the rule outran the biology. Rules do that.

8:47What you were taught

Think back to being very small. Things smelled the way they smelled. None of it was an accusation. Then you were taught. That one is lovely. That one is not. Go and have a shower.

9:02Hygiene and odour are not the same thing

Now, hygiene matters. Nobody here is arguing against washing. But two things got welded together that were never the same thing. Something can smell without being dangerous. And something can be dangerous and smell of nothing at all. Carbon monoxide has no smell. It kills people in their sleep. None of which means the rule is simply wrong. Nobody wants to sit next to somebody who has not washed in three weeks. But there is a great deal of room between that, and treating a body as a fault.

Smell is information. What we decided it means is another matter entirely.

9:46Back to the molecules

Now go back to the molecules.

A few parts in every billion, drifting through ordinary air. Enough to make you hungry. Enough to make you step back. Enough to put you in a kitchen you have not stood in for twenty years. An amount of matter too small to weigh, rearranging your opinion of a person. Because you are not reacting to a smell. You are reacting to information. Your body is running a system that is older than language, and it never got the message to stop. Underneath the soap, and the perfume, and the freshly washed shirt, something is still talking.

So the next time somebody complains about a smell, remember what is actually arriving. Not a substance called "bad". Just molecules. A few in every billion. Carrying information that we decided, in 1919, we would rather not receive. Maybe we would all live a little easier, with one less thing to worry about, if we had not turned information into a social problem. We are not smelling a person. We are smelling molecules.
