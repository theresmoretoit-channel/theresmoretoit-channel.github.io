Why We Do What We Do

Are You a Hacker Just Because You Opened a Terminal?

10 min2026-09-04

Maybe. Or maybe you are just trying to find out why the server is down.

This video is about why terminals and hacking became almost the same thing in popular culture — and what hacking actually looks like. The green phosphor that Hollywood borrowed. The progress bar that reaches one hundred per cent. The line "I'm in." And then the real version, where the server says permission denied and you spend twenty minutes discovering that you forgot one character in a configuration file.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Someone walks past

You open a terminal. Someone walks past. They see a black screen covered in mysterious text. And suddenly… "Oh my god! You're hacking!"

Well… Maybe. Or maybe you are just trying to find out why the server is down. Today we are going to look at why terminals and hacking have become almost the same thing in popular culture. And, more importantly… what hacking actually looks like.

0:29The magical black screen

For most people, a computer has buttons. You click something. Something happens. Then there is the terminal. No buttons. No friendly messages. Just this. And that is terrifying. A graphical interface might say: delete user. A terminal says this instead. To an experienced Linux user, that is perfectly ordinary. To everyone else, it looks like you are about to launch a missile.

0:59Why it looks like that

There is actually a historical reason for the way it looks. Early computers had no graphical interface. People talked to them through text terminals. And those terminals had dark screens with green or amber phosphor text. That was not an attempt to look cool. It was simply how the technology worked. But then film and television noticed something useful. A black screen full of rapidly changing text looks technical. And, more importantly… it looks complicated. You do not need to understand what the characters mean. You just see this. And your brain decides: this person is doing something illegal.

1:43The Hollywood hacker

Hollywood has developed a remarkably consistent language for hacking. The hacker sits in a dark room. There are six monitors. The room is lit entirely by the monitors. Nobody has turned the lights on. The hacker starts typing at roughly nine hundred words a minute. Text flies across the screen. A progress bar appears. Ninety-seven per cent. Ninety-eight. Ninety-nine. And finally, one hundred. The hacker leans back. And says… "I'm in."

2:17The real conversation

That is not how most computer security incidents work. Let us imagine the real conversation. "I am going to hack this server." And the server answers: permission denied. "Oh." Tries again. Permission denied. Checks the password. Reads the documentation. Searches the error message. Eventually discovers that the administrator turned off password logins six months ago. "Right."

Computers are remarkably good at making you spend twenty minutes discovering that you forgot one character in a configuration file.

2:58A terminal is an interface

On one side of the screen: system administrator. On the other: hacker. The same tools can appear on both. That is because the terminal is not a hacking tool. It is an interface. You can use it to run a server, read logs, copy files, install software, or work out what broke at three in the morning. Or, depending on what you are doing and whether you have permission, you can use it for security testing. Or for an attack. The tool does not tell you which one you are doing. A screwdriver can repair a computer. It can also destroy one. The screwdriver is not the criminal.

3:41What a hacker actually is

So what actually is a hacker? The word has a much older history than modern cybercrime. Originally, a hacker was someone who liked to explore systems and could find unexpected solutions. Over time, the word became attached to computer security. And today, when someone says "he is a hacker", they might mean a security researcher, a penetration tester, a genuine attacker, or simply someone who knows an alarming amount about Linux. Usually the context helps.

4:16Back to the picture

Now let us look at our picture again. The caption says: you hacked it. And look at her screens. On one of them, something like nmap. On the other, something that looks even more suspicious. Slash etc slash passwd. To someone who does not know Linux, she is clearly breaking into the Pentagon. To a system administrator, she is checking the network and reading a list of accounts. And that is where the joke comes from. The woman may not be hacking anything at all. She might just be doing her job.

4:51What nmap really does

So what is nmap actually doing? It is a tool for network discovery and security auditing. In very simple terms, you point it at a machine and ask: what is this computer exposing to the network? It might tell you that certain ports are open. And ports correspond to network services. That is not automatically an attack. It is more like looking at a building and asking which doors are open. If it is your building, that is normal administration. If it is someone else's, and you are deliberately looking for a way in… well, that is a different story.

5:31The password file myth

And what about that path? Someone might think: she is reading the password file. It does contain information about accounts. But on a modern Linux system, the actual password hashes are stored somewhere else. They are in slash etc slash shadow, which is much more restricted. So seeing that file is not the same thing as discovering everyone's passwords. Hollywood, of course, would make this much more dramatic. A Linux administrator would say: it is literally a list of users.

6:05Cracking, slowly

Another classic scene cracks a password in approximately fourteen seconds. Real cracking is duller. You have a hash, you have a wordlist, and you let the computer try candidates. Maybe it finds one in a minute. Maybe in a week. Maybe never. And while it works, the attacker is not sitting there typing. They might be eating a sandwich.

6:29Why they type so fast

Which brings me to my favourite Hollywood tradition. Apparently computers do not accept commands unless you type them extremely fast. Real programmers and administrators do not type constantly. They think. They read. They stare at an error. Then they discover that someone solved it eleven years ago. And then they copy the solution. That is computer science.

6:57The most dangerous command

Sometimes the most dangerous thing you can type is not a mysterious hacking command. It is something perfectly legitimate. The famous example is this one. Which is a wonderful story, except that a modern system refuses. It answers that operating recursively on slash is dangerous, unless you add a flag saying you meant it. The commands that really destroy things are duller. A delete in the wrong directory. A disk written over the wrong disk. The terminal does not warn you about those, because as far as it can tell, nothing is wrong. It can only ask: are you absolutely sure?

And if you answer yes… it may believe you.

7:43What a real attack looks like

And here is the biggest problem with the Hollywood stereotype. A real attack might not look like hacking at all. It might involve a stolen password. Phishing. An unpatched server. A leaked API key. A password reused from somewhere else. The attacker might not even need a terminal. They might simply log in to a web interface. Username. Password. And now they are inside. No green text. No Matrix-style graphics. No dramatic music. Which is arguably much scarier.

8:22A hacker does not look like one

People imagine a hacker as a hoodie, a dark room and a glowing keyboard. None of that has anything to do with hacking. The person next to you on the train, with a completely ordinary laptop, might know considerably more about computer security. And the person sitting in front of six servers might simply be trying to work out why the database will not start.

8:47So was she hacking?

So let us return to our original picture. The man points at the woman. You hacked it. Was she hacking? Maybe. Maybe not.

She has a terminal. She has nmap. She has a list of accounts. But none of those things prove that she has hacked anything. She could be the administrator, or running a security audit. Or she could actually be hacking. We do not know. And that is the joke.

9:18What the difference really is

The real difference between ordinary administration and hacking is not the colour of your terminal. It is not how many monitors you have. It is not how fast you type. And it is certainly not whether you are wearing a hoodie. It is what you are doing, and whether you have permission to do it. So next time you see someone staring at a black screen full of mysterious text, do not assume they are hacking. They might just be fixing the server. Although… if they are typing at nine hundred words a minute while shouting "I'm in"…

you might want to ask what they are doing.
