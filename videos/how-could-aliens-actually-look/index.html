How Could Aliens Actually Look?

9 min2026-10-07

Earth has already made creatures stranger than most science fiction: fish that talk with electricity, worms with no mouth that never eat, a slime mould with no brain that solves mazes, and a forest in Utah that is one single living thing. So what could life elsewhere actually look like — and would we even recognise it?

The full narration of the video.

0:00What we imagine

When we imagine aliens, we usually imagine something strangely familiar. Two arms. Two legs. A head. Two eyes. A mouth. Maybe they're green. Maybe grey. Maybe they have a few extra fingers. But underneath, they're still… people. Yet evolution on another world would have no reason to build anything like us. So what could an alien actually look like? Here's the thing. We don't have to invent anything crazy to find out. Earth has already built creatures stranger than most science fiction. We just don't think of them as aliens.

0:39Senses we don't have

Take a flower. To you, it's yellow. To a bee, it has a dark target painted in the middle, pointing straight at the nectar. Bees see ultraviolet light. The pattern is really there. We simply can't see it. And that's just one extra colour. Pit vipers hunt in total darkness. Two small pits on their face sense the heat of a mouse, so they can strike at a warm body they cannot see. Sharks feel electricity. Every living fish gives off a tiny electric field, and a shark can find a fish buried under the sand. Some fish go one step further. They don't just sense electricity. They talk with it.

Pulses of electricity for courtship, for warnings, for "this is my territory". A whole conversation that we would never notice. Migrating birds sense Earth's magnetic field. And many bats see with sound. They shout, and listen for the echo. Now imagine an alien that has all of these. Or none of them.

1:45Black forests

And colour itself might not look like ours. Plants on Earth are green because chlorophyll soaks up red and blue light, and bounces the green back. But most stars in our galaxy aren't like the Sun. They're red dwarfs, smaller, cooler stars that shine mostly in red and infrared. Under a sun like that, plants might evolve to swallow every bit of light they can get. Which means their forests could be black.

2:14Life without eyes

Then there's the opposite question. If light isn't useful on a world, why have eyes at all? Earth has an answer to that too. In caves in Mexico, there's a fish whose ancestors had perfectly good eyes. After generations in the dark, its descendants are blind. Just skin where the eyes once were. Now picture a world under kilometres of ice, like the oceans that may exist on some moons of Jupiter. Eyes there would be about as useful as they are in that cave.

2:46Life without sunlight

And down there, life might not need sunlight at all. At the bottom of our own ocean, around volcanic vents, there are worms two metres long. They have no mouth. No stomach. No gut. Instead, they carry bacteria inside their bodies that turn chemicals from the vent into food. An animal that never eats. It would be hard to design something more alien than that. And it's here.

3:14Gravity changes the shape

Gravity would change the shape too. Make an animal twice as tall, and it becomes eight times heavier. But its bones only get four times stronger. That's why an elephant doesn't look like a big gazelle. On a heavy world, large animals would be short and squat. On a light one, they could be tall and spindly in a way that would collapse on Earth. So far, we've changed the senses, the colours and the proportions. But we've kept the basic plan. A body with a head.

3:47Do aliens need a head?

Do aliens even need a head? An octopus has around five hundred million neurons. And most of them aren't in its head. They're in its arms. Each arm can taste, touch and make some of its own decisions. A starfish has no brain at all. Just a ring of nerves, and no front or back. And then there's this. A slime mould. A single giant cell with no neurons whatsoever. Put it in a maze with food at two exits, and it finds the shortest path. Put oat flakes on a map, where the cities around Tokyo are, and it grows a network that looks remarkably like the Tokyo railway.

No brain. No neurons. And yet it keeps finding surprisingly efficient routes. So a head is one solution. It's not a rule.

4:41One creature or many?

Now let's go further. Do aliens even need to be one individual? This is a Portuguese man o' war. It looks like a jellyfish. It may not be one animal. It's a colony of many small bodies, each specialised for a single job. Floating, stinging, eating. None of them can survive alone. Together, they behave like one creature. So is that one organism? Or hundreds? Biologists still argue about it. And an alien intelligence could be the same kind of puzzle.

5:18Giant organisms

Then there's size. In Utah, there's a forest of around forty-seven thousand aspen trees. Except it isn't a forest. Every one of those trunks grows from the same root system, with the same genes. Biologically, it's a single organism. Tens of thousands of trunks. One genetic individual. It's called Pando, and all together it weighs around six thousand tonnes. You can walk right through it and never realise you're inside a single living thing. And Pando doesn't even cover the most ground. In Oregon, a single honey fungus spreads underground across nearly ten square kilometres.

On the surface, you only see a few mushrooms.

6:05Size changes time

And size changes time. In America, there's a mayfly whose females spend less than five minutes as adults. Five minutes to fly, mate, lay eggs and die. Now imagine intelligence evolving on that clock. Or the opposite. A giant, slow creature might take a year to finish a thought. To it, a human life could be a flicker.

6:30Life without a planet

And then there's the question we rarely ask. Does life need to stand on a planet at all? In 1976, Carl Sagan and Edwin Salpeter imagined life in the atmosphere of Jupiter. Giant gas bags, kilometres across, drifting in the clouds. Never touching the ground, because there isn't one. Nobody knows if anything like that exists. It was a thought experiment. But nobody has shown that it's impossible. And it shows how many of our assumptions come from standing on a rock.

7:06Landing on another world

Which brings us to the biggest problem of all. Imagine landing on another world. You see rocks. Dust. Hills. Nothing moves. "There's no life here." But we just saw a forest that is one living thing. A colony that acts like an animal. A cell that finds its way through a maze. What if that landscape is alive? What if it thinks once every thousand years, through chemistry we've never measured? We'd be standing in the middle of an alien… and see nothing but geology.

7:44So what would they look like?

So what would an alien actually look like? The honest answer is, we don't know. But Earth gives us a hint. With one chemistry and one planet, life has already made creatures we'd barely recognise. Another world wouldn't start from our history, our gravity or our sunlight. When we imagine an alien, we usually start with a human… and change the shape. Maybe the real question isn't what aliens look like. Maybe it's whether we'd recognise one if we were looking right at it.
