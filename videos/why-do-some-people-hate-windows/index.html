Why Do Some People Hate Windows?

12 min2026-09-16

And then there are people who can talk for an hour about why it is terrible.

This video is about what those people are actually objecting to — and why it might make no sense to you at all.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00Most people never think about it

Why do so many people hate Windows? Windows runs on more than a billion computers. For most of those people it is simply the thing that starts when they press the button. They open a browser. Watch YouTube. Write a document. Play a game. And they never think about what Windows is doing inside. Or about the fact that anything else exists. But some people can talk for an hour about why Windows is terrible. Some of them run Linux. Some of them have a Mac. Some simply don't like Microsoft. So what exactly bothers them? And why might none of it make any sense to you?

0:42The paradox: thirty-year-old programs

Let's start with something concrete. Some programs written in the 1990s still run on Windows today. Thirty years later, on hardware that didn't exist when they were written. Almost nothing else you own can say that. And here is the strange part. That is also one of the reasons people call Windows a mess. The thing they complain about and the thing that keeps it working are the same thing.

1:12The washing machine

But first, let's start with you. Imagine you buy a washing machine. You plug it in. You select a programme. It washes your clothes. You don't want to read its source code. You don't want to write your own spin cycle. You want clean clothes. And that is how most people see their computer. The computer works. Done. And there is nothing wrong with that.

1:39Forty years of building on top

But some people want something more. And to see why, we have to look at how Windows was actually built. Today's Windows was never designed as one system. It is the result of more than forty years of building. It started as a graphical shell running on top of MS-DOS. Almost everything since then has been added on top. And Microsoft had one enormous constraint. Compatibility.

2:07The building nobody can demolish

Imagine a building that has been repaired and extended for thirty years. The original wiring is outdated. Some pipes run in completely illogical places. Today you would design it differently. But people still live there. Businesses still operate there. So you can't demolish it and start again. You add a floor. You fit a lift. And you keep the old pipes, because nobody may touch them. Windows can be a bit like that. A program written twenty years ago may still expect a particular interface, a particular behaviour, a particular file format.

And Microsoft can't simply delete the old parts. Because somewhere in the world a company still depends on them. That is the paradox. Backward compatibility is one of Windows' greatest strengths. And one of the reasons it feels like a technological patchwork.

3:06Windows 95, 98, ME — and the reputation

And some versions of Windows really didn't help. Windows 95. Windows 98. Windows ME. Drivers. Conflicts. Crashes. Restarts. Blue screens. Installing a new piece of hardware could feel like a small engineering project. And Windows earned a reputation. Windows is buggy. Windows crashes. Windows needs reinstalling.

3:32Windows NT and XP

But underneath, Microsoft had a second, sturdier line. Windows NT. And in 2001, Windows XP finally brought it to ordinary home computers. Windows became a far more stable system. But the reputation stayed. If a restaurant serves you one genuinely terrible meal, you can come back ten years later and find it excellent. And you will still think: I once had the worst goulash of my life in here.

4:04Microsoft the company

And then there is Microsoft itself. Windows became the dominant operating system for personal computers. And with dominance comes power. Microsoft ended up in a long series of fights with its competitors. Most famously the antitrust case around Internet Explorer and Netscape. So there is a second reason people dislike Windows. Not because it crashed on them. But because of how the company behaved when it was winning. So you can dislike Windows without having any problem with its technology.

4:42"But the computer is mine"

But now we get to something deeper. Imagine you buy a car. It is yours. And then the manufacturer says: some of its functions can be changed by an update. Some parts have to be paired with the car's electronics. And some things simply aren't possible, because we decided not to allow them. Most people won't care. They will never open the bonnet. But some people will say: wait. I bought this. Why are you telling me what I am allowed to do with it? And that feeling gets sharper the day the manufacturer announces it is dropping a feature you were using.

You can complain. You can buy a different car. But you cannot take their software and keep the feature working. And this is where the philosophy of free software begins.

5:33Closed source

Windows is proprietary software. Closed source. Microsoft gives you the finished program. You don't get its complete source code. It is a car where the manufacturer says: this is how we built it. Trust us that it works properly. And most people will say: fine. Just as most people never open up their car's control unit. But some people will say: I would like to look. With open source you can read the code. Study it. Change it. Fix it. And if the licence allows it, build your own version. Closed source doesn't mean that software cannot be checked at all.

You can watch how a program behaves. You can watch its network traffic. You can take the binary apart, all the way down to processor instructions. But that is dismantling the finished car instead of reading the service manual. In theory, you can do it. In practice, it is much harder. So closed software asks you for a certain amount of trust in the company that made it. And that, far more than any crash, is what some people are actually objecting to.

6:47It was about who decides

Because the argument was never really about bugs. It was about who decides.

7:04Linux is a different philosophy

Which is why Linux isn't simply Windows for free. It is also a different philosophy. Windows tells you: this is how we designed it. Linux tends to say: here is the toolbox. You choose a distribution. A desktop environment. Your programs. And if you want to, the system itself. But you don't have to. Install a mainstream distribution today and you may never open a terminal at all. And if you are feeling brave, you can still break something you didn't understand five minutes ago… and then spend three hours finding out why.

That is Linux too. But it is an option, not the price of entry. For some people that freedom is the whole point. For everyone else it simply sits there until they need it.

7:56And what about Apple?

And now an interesting paradox. If closed source is your problem, you can move to Linux. But if convenience is what you want, you can buy an Apple product. Apple builds the hardware and the software together, so it has far more control over the whole ecosystem. And that has real advantages. Things work together. You don't have to think about the technical details. And that is exactly what some people want. They don't want to be the administrator of their computer. They want to be its user. To someone else the same sentence is the problem. The more decisions the manufacturer makes for you, the fewer are left to you.

And there is one more thing people hold against Apple. Every year, something is revolutionary again. For some people that is part of the fun. For others it is exactly why they can't stand it. They don't want a computer to become a lifestyle.

8:59Three approaches

Windows says: you want compatibility? Here is a system that has to work with an enormous number of things. Apple says: you want it all to fit together? We will decide how. Linux says: you want control? Here you go. As much or as little as you like. And none of those is automatically the right one.

9:22Modern Windows

There is one more reason people actively dislike Windows. And it has nothing to do with the 1990s. The feeling that the system is constantly offering you something. Updates. OneDrive. A Microsoft account. Edge. Notifications. Features you never asked for. And you might think: I bought this computer. Why does it keep trying to sell me something? There are obviously reasons behind those decisions. Cloud services. Security. One ecosystem. Money. But from where the user is standing, the result is very simple. Just let me use my computer.

10:05So who is actually right?

So who is actually right? Maybe everyone. Because people arguing about operating systems are usually not answering the same question. One of them says: I just want it to work. The other says: I want to know how it works. One wants somebody else to take care of it. The other wants nobody telling them what they can do. Convenience, against control.

And most people want neither. They want to turn the computer on, open a browser, and have it work. Which is a perfectly legitimate thing to want.

10:43What do you actually want?

So maybe the better question isn't which operating system is best. Maybe it is: what do you actually want from your computer? Convenience? Compatibility? Freedom? Control? Or do you just want it to launch your game when you turn it on? Because in the end, it is just a computer. Well… at least for most people.
