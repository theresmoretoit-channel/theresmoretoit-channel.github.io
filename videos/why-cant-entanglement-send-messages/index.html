Physics That Sounds Simple Until You Ask

Why Can't Entanglement Send Messages?

11 min2026-11-16

This video goes through what entanglement actually is, John Bell's argument with Reinhold Bertlmann's mismatched socks, David Mermin's two boxes with three-position switches, the experiments since 1972 and the 2022 Nobel Prize, what happens when Alice tries to send Bob a single bit, Steven Weinberg's tiny change to quantum mechanics and what Nicolas Gisin and Joseph Polchinski found in it, and a game where entangled particles win about 85 per cent of the time and never more. Then Popescu and Rohrlich's imaginary box that always wins, and the question nobody has answered yet.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Two photons, 1,200 km apart

In 2016, China launched a satellite called Micius. One of its jobs was to make pairs of photons and split them up. One photon of each pair went down to an observatory in one city, the other to an observatory in another, twelve hundred kilometres away. At each end, a detector measured the photon that arrived. And the results were linked more tightly than anything ordinary allows. Is something passing between them? In 2008, physicists near Geneva tried to time it, between two villages eighteen kilometres apart. Assuming the universe has a preferred frame, in which Earth moves slowly compared to light, they found that anything travelling from one photon to the other would need to go at least ten thousand times faster than light.

That's a limit on something hypothetical, not a speed anyone measured. But it's a strange one.

0:57A line to Mars?

So here's the obvious idea. Keep one photon of each pair, and give the other to a friend on Mars. Today a radio message to Mars takes up to twenty-two minutes. With entangled photons, you measure yours, and your friend knows. It doesn't work. Not with better detectors, not with better satellites. Not ever, as far as physics knows. And the reason is stranger than the link itself.

1:26What entanglement actually is

First, what entanglement actually is. A source makes two particles together and sends one to Alice, and one to Bob. Each of them measures a particle and gets one of two answers. Call them zero and one. Alice's answer is random. So is Bob's. But when they set their detectors the same way, the answers match. In the ideal case, every time. Einstein never accepted that quantum mechanics was the complete story. In a letter to Max Born in 1947, he called this spooky action at a distance.

2:03Are particles just like socks?

But hold on. Is that really so strange? The physicist John Bell had a colleague, Reinhold Bertlmann, who liked to wear two socks of different colours. See that one sock is pink, and you know the other one isn't. Instantly, at any distance. Nothing spooky about it. So maybe particles are the same. Maybe they carry their answers from the start, like socks chosen that morning. In 1981, Bell used those socks to explain why that idea fails.

2:36Mermin's two boxes

Here's the simplest version of the test, from the physicist David Mermin. Two boxes, far apart, each with a switch with three positions. A particle arrives, and the box flashes red or green. For every particle, Alice and Bob each pick a position at random, without knowing what the other picked. When they happen to pick the same position, the colours always match. So suppose each pair of particles carries instructions. A colour for each position, the same list in both. Take the list red, red, green. There are nine ways to set the two switches. In five of them, the colours match.

Try any other list. None gives fewer than five. So if the answers are written in advance, the colours match at least five times out of nine. Quantum mechanics predicts exactly half.

Five ninths against one half. Experiments have tested gaps like this since 1972, and they side with quantum mechanics.

3:40Delft, and a Nobel Prize

In 2015, a team in Delft closed the two biggest loopholes at once. And in 2022, three pioneers of these experiments, John Clauser, Alain Aspect and Anton Zeilinger, shared the Nobel Prize for their own work. So the answers can't all be written in advance, at least not in any way that stays local. That doesn't mean one particle sends a signal to the other. And that is exactly where our message runs into trouble.

4:12Trying to send one bit

Let's try to send one. Alice wants to send Bob a single bit. She measures her particle and gets whatever she gets. She can't choose. Zero or one, the result is random. Bob gets the matching answer, but Alice didn't put anything in it. And Bob can't even tell whether Alice measured at all. Say Bob measures a thousand particles. About five hundred zeros, about five hundred ones. Now Alice measures her side, any way she likes. Bob measures another thousand. About five hundred, and five hundred. Nothing on his side changes. The connection only becomes visible when Alice and Bob compare their records. And to compare them, they have to send them, at the speed of light or slower.

5:03The one thing Alice controls

But Alice does control one thing. She chooses how to set her detector. In Mermin's boxes, that's the switch. Her choice changes how her results line up with Bob's. But that only shows when they compare. So could Bob notice it? Position one means zero, position two means one? No. Whatever Alice does to her particle, measuring it, turning it, or leaving it alone, Bob still sees red half the time and green half the time. It's called the no-communication theorem, and physicists proved it around 1980. Now imagine Alice could force her result, and the link still held. Force a zero. Force a one.

Then Bob would get her bit every time he measured. That would be a message, faster than light. So the randomness isn't a flaw in our equipment. It's part of the reason the link can't carry a message.

6:01What if the theory is slightly wrong?

But that's standard quantum mechanics. What if the theory itself is very slightly wrong? In 1989, Steven Weinberg, who had won a Nobel Prize ten years earlier, asked exactly that. Weinberg wrote down a version with a tiny change to its mathematics, a so-called nonlinear correction. He wasn't claiming it was correct. He wanted experiments to test it. Experiments looked for it with extreme precision, and found nothing.

6:33Gisin, Polchinski and the Everett phone

Then came a deeper problem. In 1990, Nicolas Gisin showed that Weinberg's theory, with the usual rules of measurement, would let Alice signal Bob. Instantly. At any distance. Joseph Polchinski looked closer. You could block the signal between Alice and Bob, but the theory still allowed communication between branches of the wave function. In principle, between branches where an observer got different outcomes. He called it the Everett phone. Not every nonlinear theory has to fail this way. But the lesson was clear. The rule against signalling isn't an extra restriction. It follows from standard quantum mechanics.

7:18A signal that arrives before it's sent

Why does that matter? As our video on faster-than-light travel showed, a signal faster than light could arrive before it was sent. Entanglement doesn't let information cross that line. It creates correlations, but carries no signal. Even quantum teleportation, from our video on teleportation, needs two ordinary bits, sent the ordinary way. Without them, Bob's result is indistinguishable from noise.

7:48The second mystery: a game

So that's the first mystery solved. Entanglement can't carry a message. But there's a second one. The strength of the link can be measured, and it has a surprising limit.

Here's a game. Alice and Bob each get a random question, zero or one, and each answers zero or one. They can't talk. They win if their answers match, except when both get question one. Then the answers must differ. With any plan agreed in advance, the best they can do is win three times out of four. With entangled particles, they can win about eighty-five per cent of the time, and experiments keep beating three in four. But not by more. In 1980, Boris Tsirelson proved that quantum mechanics tops out at eighty-five point four.

8:40A box that always wins

In 1994, Sandu Popescu and Daniel Rohrlich asked a simple question. Why stop there? They imagined a box that wins the game every time. It isn't a real device, just mathematics. But here's the surprise. It still can't send a message. Each side, on its own, gives zero or one at random. Bob sees fifty-fifty, whatever Alice asks. Stronger links than nature allows, and still nothing faster than light.

9:13Why 85 per cent?

So why does nature stop at eighty-five? Not seventy-five. Not a hundred. Physicists have proposed principles. One result shows that above about ninety-one per cent, problems that normally need enormous amounts of communication could be solved with just one bit. Another, called information causality, gives the same eighty-five point four limit. But it doesn't fully explain why quantum mechanics works the way it does. So far, no agreed principle explains why nature allows exactly these correlations, and nothing stronger.

9:53Back to Micius

So, two photons, twelve hundred kilometres apart. Not one bit gets through. And no quantum link, however perfect, gets past eighty-five per cent.

We know why the link can't carry a message. We still don't know why it isn't stronger.

So here's a question for you. Is there a deeper rule behind eighty-five per cent, or is that just how our universe turned out? Let me know in the comments.
