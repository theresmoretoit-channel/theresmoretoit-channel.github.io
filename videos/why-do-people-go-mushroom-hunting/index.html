Why We Do What We Do

Why Do People Go Mushroom Hunting?

12 min2026-08-28

This is about why the difference exists — and why mushroom hunting turns out to be the last everyday survivor of something humans did for almost all of our history.

Along the way: why a mushroom is closer to you than to the tree it grows under, why most wild mushrooms cannot be farmed at all, why the deadliest one in Europe kills by waiting, and why drying a mushroom does not weaken it but makes it into a stronger ingredient than it ever was.

The full narration of the video.

0:00What on earth are they doing?

Imagine you come from a country where nobody picks wild mushrooms. You're walking through a forest, and you see someone carrying a basket. They spend a few minutes looking around under the trees. Then they suddenly get excited. They pull a strange brown thing out of the ground, brush the dirt off it, and drop it into the basket. And you think: what on earth are they doing? Then you find out they're planning to eat it for dinner. Which seems stranger still when you learn that the same forest holds hundreds of different species. Some are delicious. Some will make you ill. And some can kill you.

So why do people do this? Why is it completely normal in one country, and almost unthinkable in another? And is mushroom hunting a ridiculous thing to do… or a surprisingly sensible way of getting food?

0:54Not a plant, not an animal

Let's start with something that sounds like a technicality, and isn't. A mushroom isn't a plant. It isn't an animal either. Fungi are their own kingdom. But if you had to pick a side… fungi are closer to us than they are to the tree they're growing under. Animals and fungi went their separate ways about a billion years ago. Plants had already gone their own way, earlier still. So the thing in the basket is a distant relative. A very distant one. And what you can see in the forest isn't even the whole organism.

1:30What you see is not the whole organism

The part with a cap and a stem is just the fruiting body. Most of the organism is underground, or inside wood — a network of threads running through the soil. It's a bit like an apple. The apple isn't the tree. And the mushroom isn't the fungus.

1:47The largest living thing ever found

How much is hidden? In Oregon, one single fungus spreads across nearly ten square kilometres of forest. One organism. Thought to be thousands of years old. By area, the largest living thing anyone has ever found. And for most of the year you could walk straight over it and see nothing at all. Then, for a few weeks, it puts up something you can pick.

2:12Why eat them at all?

Which brings us to the obvious question. Why eat them? Because as food, mushrooms are a strange choice. They're mostly water. They aren't a good source of calories. So the honest answer isn't nutrition. It's that they're there. Humans don't have to grow all their food. Sometimes they can just… find it. Berries. Fruit. Nuts. Roots. And mushrooms. You only had to know where to look. And here's the part that turns the forest into something more than a free supermarket.

2:42Why most of them cannot be farmed

Most of these mushrooms cannot be farmed at all. Not because nobody has tried. Because of what they are. Underground, the threads of the fungus wrap around the roots of a tree and join with them. The tree hands over sugar that it made from sunlight. The fungus hands back water and minerals, drawn from far more soil than roots could ever reach. It's a trade. It's called mycorrhiza, and it is not a curiosity. The large majority of land plants do some version of it. The forest runs on it. Which is lovely — and a serious problem if you'd like to sell mushrooms. A fungus that has lived off a tree for millions of years has lost the ability to live on its own.

Give it a bag of sterile sawdust and nothing happens. It isn't looking for food. It's looking for a partner.

3:35What is actually on the shelf

Now look at a supermarket shelf. Button mushrooms. Oyster mushrooms. Shiitake. Every one of them is a species that feeds on dead material and needs no tree at all. They can be grown in a shed. And the ones that aren't on that shelf — the porcini, the chanterelles — are missing for exactly one reason. Nobody can grow them in a shed. So the forest isn't a cheaper version of the shop. For some of these mushrooms, it is the only shop there is.

4:08The dangerous ones

Which would be a happy ending, except for one detail. Some of them are poisonous. And the dangerous ones are not dangerous in the way you'd expect. Take the death cap. Its toxin doesn't attack your stomach. It jams the molecular machine that reads your genes. Cells stop making proteins. The liver, which processes almost everything you eat, takes the worst of it.

4:34Why the death cap kills

But that isn't what makes it deadly. This is. You feel completely fine. For six hours. Often twelve. Then you're violently ill. And then you get better. The symptoms fade. You appear to recover. Three to six days after the meal, the liver starts to fail. That one species, on its own, accounts for something like ninety per cent of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. Not because it's the strongest poison in the forest. Because by the time your body tells you anything, it has had half a day's head start.

5:08The rules that do not work

So you might hope for some simple rule. There are plenty of them. Poisonous ones turn a silver spoon black. Anything the animals eat is safe. Bright colours mean danger. None of them work. Silver doesn't react to the toxin. Plenty of animals happily eat things that would kill us. And some of the deadliest species are a modest, unremarkable brown. Nature doesn't label anything. So mushroom hunting isn't: I see a mushroom, I eat the mushroom.

It's: I see a mushroom. I work out which species it is. I am certain. Then I eat it.

5:49Not mushrooms — species

And that's the interesting part. Because a mushroom is not a category you can work with. An experienced picker doesn't recognise mushrooms. They recognise species. Dozens of them. Which trees they grow under. Which month they appear. What the underside looks like. What the flesh does when you cut it. It's a biological database, held in somebody's head. And traditionally, they didn't learn it from a book. They learned it from a person. Usually outdoors. Usually as a child.

Someone points at the ground and says: that one's good. That one you don't touch. And the child remembers.

6:30How the knowledge travels

Thirty years later, they say the same two sentences to somebody else's child. Do that for a few centuries and you get a country where taking a basket into a forest and coming home with dinner is completely unremarkable. In Czechia, a recent survey found that seventy-three per cent of adults go out and pick mushrooms. Not once in their lives. As a thing they do. Now take the same forest. The same trees. The same species, in the same places. And nobody picks anything. Not because it can't be eaten. Because nobody there sees it as food.

7:09The honeymoon that named it

Somebody noticed this on his honeymoon. In nineteen twenty-seven, an American and his Russian wife were walking in the woods in New York State. She saw mushrooms, ran off delighted, and started filling her arms. He found the whole thing revolting. Same forest. Same mushrooms. Same afternoon. Two completely different worlds. They spent the next thirty years trying to explain it, and gave the two attitudes names. Mycophilia. Mycophobia. Love of mushrooms, and fear of them. Neither one is about the mushrooms.

7:45Food is mostly an agreement

And that's a fascinating thing about food. What counts as normal is mostly an agreement. Somewhere, snails are dinner. Somewhere, frogs. Seaweed. Insects. Some people drink milk from another species their entire lives and never think about it once. Mushrooms are the same. One person looks at the forest floor and starts planning supper. Another looks at the same thing and thinks: that's a fungus.

8:14Why drying changes everything

Which is a shame, because they can be extraordinary. Fried in butter. In soup. In a sauce. In risotto. Pickled. Or with onion and eggs. And then there's drying, which sounds like the dull option and is the best one. Because drying doesn't only stop them going off. It changes what they are. Mushrooms are rich in the compounds behind umami — the savoury taste you get from meat, from broth, from aged cheese. As a mushroom dries, its own cells break down and release a second compound that does something strange. On its own, that compound tastes of very little. But put the two together, and the savoury hit isn't added. It's multiplied.

In tests with the pure compounds, mixing the two in equal amounts has been reported to raise savoury intensity around thirtyfold. So a dried mushroom is not a weaker version of a fresh one. It's a stronger ingredient than the mushroom ever was. Which is why, in the middle of winter, you can open a jar… and the whole kitchen smells of the forest.

9:22The part that is free

And then there's the other advantage. It's free. Well. Not counting the car. The fuel. The boots. The basket. The knife. And several hours of your life spent wandering about in the woods. But the mushrooms themselves? Those are free. You leave home with an empty basket, walk into a forest, and come back carrying several kilograms of food that nobody grew.

9:46One thing that matters

One thing, though, and it matters. Mushroom hunting isn't a competition to see who brings home the biggest basket. And picking something you can't confidently name is a genuinely bad idea. Some dangerous species look a lot like edible ones. Getting a blueberry wrong is disappointing. Getting a mushroom wrong is a different category of mistake. Which is why the knowledge still travels the way it does. From somebody who already has it, standing next to you, holding the thing.

10:20So is it ridiculous?

So is mushroom hunting ridiculous? Not really. It's the last ordinary survivor of something humans did for almost all of our history. Finding food, instead of making it. And it survived because industry couldn't take it over. Not for lack of money in it. The mushroom won't grow without its tree. And the knowledge won't fit on a label. So it stayed where it started. In forests. In families. Passed from one person to another by pointing at the ground.

Today we can buy strawberries in winter, and avocados from another continent. And mushrooms, in a plastic tray. But then you take a basket and walk into a forest. And you start looking at the world differently. You notice the trees. The moss. The soil. Where the water sits. You start reading the ground for places something might be hiding. Then you see it. A small brown cap under the leaves. You pick it. And you realise that a few hours from now, it might be on your plate.

So the next time you see someone disappearing into a forest with a basket, don't assume they've lost their mind. They're collecting dinner from a shop with no door, no shelves, and nothing written on anything. And if they know what they're doing, it makes a very good one.
