How Society Actually Runs

Does Copyright Hold Back Progress?

10 min2026-09-06

Copyright was built as a trade: a temporary monopoly in exchange for more creation. This video asks what happens when "temporary" turns out to mean the life of the author plus seventy years — and what that costs the people who want to build on what already exists.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00A recording nobody can use

Imagine you find a recording from nineteen fifty. It is exactly what you need for the documentary you are making. Nobody sells it any more. The company that released it vanished decades ago. The musician died long ago. You want ten seconds of it. And you may not be allowed to have them. Not because it is secret. Not because anyone else is using it. But because somebody may still own it. And nobody knows who. So here is the question. Can copyright get in the way of the thing it was built to encourage?

0:40Why copyright exists

Let's start somewhere important. Copyright is not a stupid idea. Imagine you spend three years writing a book, and publish it. The next morning somebody prints a million copies and undercuts you by half. You did the work. They did not. So the bargain is this. Society gives you a temporary monopoly. You get your chance to earn from it. And eventually, it belongs to everybody. That last part is the whole point. It was never the creator owns this forever.

1:15How long is "temporary"?

So how long is temporary? The Berne Convention, which most countries have signed, sets the floor at the life of the author plus fifty years. Britain, the European Union and the United States all went further than that. The life of the author, plus seventy years. Work out what that means. Someone writes a song at twenty-five. They live to eighty-five. Their song is locked up for a hundred and thirty years. Steamboat Willie, the first Mickey Mouse cartoon ever released, came out in nineteen twenty-eight. It became free to use on the first of January, two thousand and twenty-four.

Ninety-five years.

2:00Progress does not start from zero

And that number matters, because of the way progress actually happens. Nobody starts from nothing. Someone discovers something. Someone else studies it. Someone improves it. And someone builds on that. Which leaves us with a tension. Copyright says we want to encourage creation. But creating usually means: let me use something that already exists.

2:26Music: one song, many owners

Music shows this best, because a song is not one thing. There is the composition. The lyrics. The arrangement. The recording itself. The performance. And anything sampled from elsewhere. Every one of those can belong to somebody different. So you take two seconds of an old recording. You slow it down. You cut it into pieces. You bury it under a new beat. The original is barely audible. You have made something new. Legally, it may still count. And sometimes nothing was copied at all. There are only so many notes. Only so many chord progressions. Only so many rhythms. With millions of songs, resemblance is not suspicious. It is arithmetic.

3:14Blurred Lines and the dissent

In two thousand and eighteen an American appeal court upheld a verdict of over five million dollars against the writers of Blurred Lines. The dissent pointed out that the two songs differed in melody, harmony, and rhythm. It warned that the majority was effectively letting someone copyright a musical style. So the law cannot simply ask whether two things sound alike. It has to ask what was actually copied. A melody? A rhythm? A protected musical expression? Or something that belongs to everybody?

3:50Software has the same problem

Software has the same problem, only far more openly. Modern software is built out of other software: libraries, frameworks, operating systems, protocols.

4:02Reverse engineering

Now take the harder case. You buy a device. You take it apart. You work out how it does what it does. And you build your own version. You did not copy the product. You extracted the knowledge. In nineteen ninety-two an American court agreed. Copying a games console's code in order to understand it counted as fair use. Not because copying is harmless, but because there was no other way in.

4:30Google v. Oracle

In two thousand and twenty-one the American Supreme Court went further still. Google had reused eleven thousand five hundred lines of somebody else's interface. Four tenths of one per cent of the whole system. The court called it fair use, six votes to two, because it let programmers carry what they already knew into somewhere new.

Which takes us somewhere uncomfortable.

4:57Orphan works

Think of a photograph from nineteen thirty. The photographer may be dead. Their family may not know the photograph exists. The publisher may be gone. You want it for a documentary. You cannot ask permission, because you cannot find anybody to ask. That is an orphan work. It exists. It has value. Nobody is earning from it. And using it is still a risk. Europe tried to fix this in twenty twelve. You may use an orphan work, provided you first carry out what the law calls a diligent search. So the price of using an abandoned photograph is the price of proving that you went looking for its owner.

And so the work stays unused. Not because anyone wants to keep it from you. But because nobody knows who is allowed to say yes.

5:50Should we abolish copyright?

So should we simply abolish copyright? Probably not. Imagine a film that costs a hundred million dollars. Thousands of people. Years of work. And on the day it opens, somebody sells a perfect copy for almost nothing. That destroys the reason to make the expensive thing at all. "Everything should be free" does not solve the problem. It just moves it somewhere else. So the question is not copyright or no copyright. It is how much.

6:25Compulsory licensing, since 1909

And some of the answers already exist. If you want to record your own version of somebody else's song in the United States, you do not need their permission. You file a notice. You pay a fixed rate. That is the entire process. That system was written in nineteen oh nine. Compulsory licensing is not a thought experiment. It is older than almost everybody using it.

The same idea could cover sampling. You used two seconds? Report it. Pay the rate. Done. No months of negotiation. No hunting for whoever owns three seconds of a recording from nineteen seventy-four.

7:08Transformation is not copying

And the law could take transformation more seriously. Pirating a film is not the same thing as making a documentary about it. Uploading somebody's song is not the same thing as sampling two seconds of it. Copying a rival's code is not the same thing as building something that works alongside it. If you want the next one, there is a subscribe button.

7:33And then AI arrives

And then, of course, we built generative AI. Can a model learn from copyrighted books? From copyrighted music? Can it imitate somebody's particular style? And if it can, who owns what comes out? In twenty twenty-five two American courts answered part of it. Training a model on copyrighted books was transformative enough to count as fair use. But one of those same rulings found that keeping a library of pirated books was not. Which is exactly the distinction this whole argument has been circling. Not, did you use it. But, what did you do with it.

AI did not create that question. It just made it impossible to keep avoiding.

8:20The trade-off

Because there is no free option here. Strong protection gives people a reason to invest. It also makes existing culture harder to reuse. Weak protection makes building on the past easier. It also makes expensive work harder to pay for. Which is why the goal was never to maximise protection, and never to minimise it either. The goal was to maximise how much gets made. And those are not the same thing.

8:50What it leaves for tomorrow

So perhaps the real question is not how we stop people copying. It is how we stop free-riding without stopping building. And the most important thing about copyright may not be what it hands today's creators. It is what it leaves for tomorrow's. Because if we guard the past too carefully… we end up with a system so good at protecting what has already been made that making the next thing is unnecessarily hard. And that would be a very strange way to encourage progress.
