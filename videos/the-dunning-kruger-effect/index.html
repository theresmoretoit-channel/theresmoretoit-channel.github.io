Why Do We Think We Know More Than We Do?

10 min2026-09-09

In 1999, Justin Kruger and David Dunning gave students tests of humour, grammar and logic, then asked them how well they thought they had done. Across four studies, the people in the bottom quarter scored around the 12th percentile — and estimated themselves at the 62nd. They weren't just wrong. They were wrong about how wrong they were.

But the popular internet version gets it backwards, and there's a second problem that almost nobody mentions: several researchers have argued that the characteristic graph can appear partly for statistical reasons. Take simulated data with no such effect in it at all, plot it the same way, and you can still get a picture that looks much like the original. The debate isn't settled — which brings us straight back to the uncomfortable question the whole thing is about.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The feeling of getting it

You know that feeling when you learn something new… and suddenly, it seems incredibly simple? You watch a couple of videos. Read a few articles. Maybe you try it yourself. And then you think: "I get it." But here's the scary part. What if the thing you're missing is precisely the thing that would tell you that you're missing something? This is the basic idea behind the Dunning–Kruger effect. And it's much more interesting — and much more disturbing — than the popular internet version of it.

0:35Learning to program

Imagine you've never programmed before. Someone shows you a few lines of code. You learn what a variable is. You learn what a loop does. You write your first little program… and suddenly programming doesn't seem that difficult anymore. You might even look at professional programmers and wonder: "Why do they make such a big deal out of this?" But then you keep learning. You encounter data structures. Concurrency. Memory management. And suddenly you realise that programming isn't one problem. It's an entire landscape of problems you didn't even know existed.

And that's the key.

1:19The 1999 study

In nineteen ninety-nine, psychologists David Dunning and Justin Kruger published a study investigating something very similar. They were interested in people's ability to judge their own performance. They gave students tests of logical reasoning, grammar, and humour. Then they asked them how well they thought they had done. What they reported was that people who performed poorly on certain tasks often had difficulty recognising just how poorly they had performed.

1:5012th versus 62nd

And the numbers were striking. The people who performed in the bottom quarter estimated their performance as if they were around the sixty-second percentile. In reality, they were around the twelfth. They weren't just wrong. They were wrong about how wrong they were.

In other words, the problem wasn't only that they made mistakes. They also lacked some of the knowledge necessary to recognise those mistakes.

2:18Why it happens

And this creates a peculiar situation. You need a certain amount of knowledge to perform a task. But you may also need some of that same knowledge to accurately judge your performance. So when your knowledge is very limited… your ability to evaluate your own knowledge can be limited too. And this is where the phenomenon becomes genuinely interesting. Suppose I give you a complicated problem. You produce an answer. You are completely convinced it's correct. How can you tell whether you're right? You could compare it with an expert. You could test it against reality.

You could look for counterexamples. You could measure the result. But imagine that none of those things are available. Then you're relying largely on your own judgement. And if your judgement is based on the same incomplete knowledge that produced the original answer… you have a problem.

3:17Grading your own exam

Your brain is essentially grading its own exam. With an answer key it doesn't have.

This is also why expertise can look surprisingly different from confidence. A beginner might say: "This is easy." An experienced person might say: "It depends." Why? Not necessarily because the expert is less confident. The expert simply sees more variables. The beginner sees the problem. The expert sees the problem… and all the ways the problem can go wrong. Think about something as simple as fixing a computer. A beginner might say: "Just reinstall the operating system." Sometimes, that's exactly right. But an experienced administrator immediately starts asking questions.

Is the hardware healthy? Is the filesystem corrupted? Is the disk failing? Is there a backup? Is the problem actually software? Is there a network issue? Will reinstalling destroy useful evidence? What caused the problem in the first place? The beginner sees one solution. The expert sees a decision tree. And that difference can make expertise look like uncertainty.

4:39What it does not mean

But there's an important catch. The Dunning–Kruger effect does not mean: "The less intelligent you are, the more confident you become." And it doesn't mean that experts are always insecure. That's a caricature of the research.

4:55The graph itself

But there's another problem. What if even the famous Dunning–Kruger graph doesn't mean quite what we think it means? Several researchers have argued that the characteristic pattern can appear partly because of statistical effects. Things like regression to the mean. And the fact that almost everyone thinks they're above average. And a correlation created by the way the two measurements are plotted against each other. And here's the surprising part. If you take simulated data with no such effect built into it, and plot it the same way…

you can still get a graph that looks much like the original. In other words… the graph itself needs to be interpreted carefully.

And the debate isn't completely settled. There are responses to those criticisms, and responses to those responses. Which brings us back to the uncomfortable question. How do you know you're right?

5:57What they actually claimed

Dunning and Kruger's own claim was more subtle. People can have difficulty accurately evaluating their own competence, especially when they lack the skills needed to evaluate the task itself. And that can happen to almost anyone. In fact, that's what makes this effect so dangerous. You don't have to be stupid. You just have to enter a new field. Every expert was once a beginner. Every beginner initially has a very incomplete mental model of the subject. And that means every one of us has areas where we may be confidently wrong… without realising it.

6:37Are you doing it right now?

And here's the really uncomfortable question. How do you know you're not doing it right now?

You might be thinking: "Fortunately, I understand the Dunning–Kruger effect, so I'm not one of those people." But that doesn't prove anything. Knowing that the effect exists doesn't magically make you immune to it. You can understand the concept perfectly… and still overestimate your knowledge of economics. Or medicine. Or programming. Or history. Or physics. Or politics. Or literally anything else.

7:14Feedback from reality

So what can we actually do about it? The answer isn't simply: "Be more humble." Humility is useful. But humility by itself doesn't tell you whether you're right. A person can be extremely humble… and still be wrong. What we need is something better: feedback from reality. Test your assumptions. Look for counterexamples. Measure things. Ask people who know more than you. Try to predict what will happen before it happens. And, perhaps most importantly… make it possible for yourself to discover that you're wrong.

A good programmer doesn't trust their code because they feel confident about it. They run tests. A scientist doesn't trust an idea because it feels convincing. They test it against evidence. An engineer doesn't say: "I'm pretty sure this bridge will hold." They calculate it. Measure it. Test it. And build in safety margins. These systems work because they don't rely entirely on the human ability to judge itself. They let reality provide the answer.

8:30How would you find out?

And perhaps that's the most useful lesson of the Dunning–Kruger effect. The goal isn't to constantly doubt yourself. It's not: "Maybe I'm wrong about everything." The goal is to know how you would find out if you were wrong. That's a much stronger form of intellectual humility. Instead of saying: "I'm probably right." Ask: "What evidence would convince me I'm wrong?"

If you have a good answer… you're already doing something very valuable. Because the most dangerous ignorance isn't simply not knowing something. It's not knowing that you don't know it.

And sometimes… the first step towards becoming an expert… is discovering just how much there is left to learn.
