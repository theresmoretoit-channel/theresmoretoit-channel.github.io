Physics That Sounds Simple Until You Ask

Nothing Is Really Solid — What Happens When You Push a Steel Rod?

10 min2026-09-01

Not immediately. Not at the speed of light either. The answer is Wednesday afternoon — about fifty-five hours later — and the reason takes us all the way down to what matter is actually made of.

Along the way: why steel is not perfectly rigid, why a perfectly rigid object cannot exist in this universe at all, why your hand never really touches the table it is resting on, and why "solid" turns out to be a description of behaviour rather than a property of the world.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Push one end

Imagine a steel rod. You push one end. The other end moves immediately, right? It is steel, it is solid, the whole rod has to move together… yes? Well… No. Let's dig into it.

0:18The easy answer: light

Maybe you already know the answer. Nothing can move faster than light. So the other end obviously can't respond immediately. Light would need just over three seconds to travel one million kilometres. So that's our answer, right? Not quite. Because the really interesting question is this. How fast can a steel rod actually respond?

0:44A wave in the metal

Even steel isn't perfectly rigid. When you push one end, you slightly compress the material. Those atoms push on their neighbours. Those push on the next atoms. So the disturbance travels through the material as a wave. And the question is this. How fast does that wave travel? Well… steel is actually pretty good at transmitting it. But not that good.

1:10Five kilometres a second

About five kilometres per second. That's roughly the speed of sound in steel. Which sounds pretty damn fast. Until you remember how long our rod is. One million kilometres.

1:24Fifty-five hours

At five kilometres per second, the disturbance would need roughly… fifty-five hours to reach the other end. You push the rod on Monday morning… and the other end doesn't start moving until Wednesday afternoon.

1:40But steel feels solid

This sounds ridiculous. Steel feels solid. If you hit a steel bar with a hammer, it doesn't look like you're compressing it at all. So why doesn't the whole thing move together? What is actually travelling through the steel? To answer that, we have to zoom in. A lot.

1:59The rod is not one thing

A steel rod is, physically, an enormous collection of atoms interacting with one another. When you push one end, you don't somehow give every atom in the rod an order. "Move." The atoms closest to your hand are pushed first. They move slightly. The rod doesn't respond as one perfectly rigid object. It responds progressively, from one part to the next.

2:26Zooming in on an atom

So let's zoom in even further. Steel is made of atoms. And atoms are not tiny steel balls. They're quantum mechanical systems. A tiny nucleus sits in the middle, surrounded by electrons. And almost all of an atom's volume is not occupied by the nucleus. So when you look at a solid object, you're not looking at a collection of microscopic solid balls packed tightly together. You're looking at an enormous system held together by interactions between atoms.

2:59What holds it together

So what actually holds all of this together? The answer is mostly electromagnetism. Each positively charged nucleus interacts with negatively charged electrons. Electrons also interact with neighbouring atoms. And depending on the material, these interactions create different kinds of chemical bonding. In metals, electrons can spread out across many atoms. This is part of what gives metals their characteristic metallic bonding.

3:29Why don't atoms collapse?

But now we have another problem. If atoms attract each other… why don't they simply collapse together? Imagine two atoms. Move them closer. At first, there can be attraction. Move them even closer… and the energy rises very rapidly. The electron clouds begin to overlap.

3:50The Pauli exclusion principle

And quantum mechanics prevents identical electrons from occupying the same quantum states. This is where the Pauli exclusion principle becomes important. So matter has an equilibrium. Too far apart, and attractive interactions can pull atoms together. Too close, and quantum effects and electromagnetic repulsion push back. There is a preferred arrangement. And on a macroscopic scale, we have a name for the result. Solid matter.

The atoms in a solid aren't frozen in place. They're constantly moving. And when you push on it, you're disturbing that system.

4:33Your hand on a table

Now put your hand on a table. You can feel it. It feels like your hand has encountered a completely solid surface. But zoom in. There isn't a microscopic wall made of perfectly touching little balls. The atoms in your hand interact with the atoms in the table. Their electrons interact with each other. And quantum mechanics prevents the atoms from simply passing through each other. The result is a force that stops your hand from passing through the table. What you experience as "touch" is the macroscopic consequence of all these microscopic interactions.

5:11What touching really means

So when you put your hand on a table, what does "touching" actually mean? It's an interaction. Your hand interacts with the atoms in the material. The material pushes back.

5:25Could we make it faster?

And now the original steel rod makes more sense. Its response isn't determined by how "solid" it feels to us. It's determined by how quickly a mechanical disturbance can propagate through the material. Could we make that faster? Absolutely. Different materials have different sound velocities. Steel is around five kilometres per second. Diamond can exceed ten kilometres per second. But none of them are infinitely fast. And that's not just an engineering limitation. It's fundamental.

6:04A perfectly rigid rod

Imagine we somehow invented the stiffest possible material. A material that was perfectly rigid. You push one end… and the other end responds immediately. Sounds great. But now we have a problem. You could use the rod to transmit information. Push the rod. One. Don't push it. Zero.

6:27It would be a telegraph

If the other end responded instantly, you would have a way to transmit information instantaneously. That isn't allowed by relativity. Physical influences cannot propagate faster than light. So a perfectly rigid object cannot exist in our universe. Perfect rigidity would require instantaneous communication between different parts of the object.

6:53Even light takes time

Remember our imaginary rod? One million kilometres. Even light needs just over three seconds to cross that distance. So even if we replaced our steel rod with some completely hypothetical material that was as close as physically possible to the ultimate limit… there would still be a delay. Nothing can make the information arrive instantly.

7:19Look what we just did

Think about what we've just done. We started with something completely ordinary. A steel rod. Then we asked why the far end doesn't move immediately. That led us to mechanical waves. Mechanical waves led us to atoms. Atoms led us to electromagnetism. Then quantum mechanics. And suddenly we're talking about the structure of matter itself. All because someone pushed a stick.

7:50What "solid" actually means

The word "solid" sounds like a fundamental property. But "solid" is really a macroscopic description of how an enormous number of particles behave together. At the microscopic level, matter is dynamic. Atoms vibrate. Electrons occupy quantum states. Fields interact. Energy moves around. And yet, when billions upon billions of these interactions work together, we get something that feels completely solid. A table. A rock. A wall. Your hand.

8:24The same laws on both sides

And here's the part I find most remarkable. The same physical laws that make the table solid also make your hand solid. And those same laws allow your brain to detect the forces between them. Your brain receives electrical signals. It processes them. And it constructs an experience. The feeling of touching a solid object. So the thing experiencing the table is made from the same kind of matter as the table.

8:55Next time you touch a table

So the next time you put your hand on a table, you can think of it differently. It isn't a perfectly rigid block. It's an enormous quantum system. And when you push on it, the disturbance travels through the material as a wave. The table feels solid. But underneath that familiar solidity is something much stranger. A universe where almost nothing is quite what it seems. And somehow… it all holds together.
