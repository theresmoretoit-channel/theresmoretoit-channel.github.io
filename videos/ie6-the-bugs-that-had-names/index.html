Internet Explorer 6 — The Bugs That Had Names

14 min2026-09-07

In Internet Explorer 6 the bugs had proper nouns. Peekaboo. The Guillotine. The Three Pixel Jog. The Duplicate Character Bug. And that turns out to be the most telling thing about the whole era: you don't name a bug you meet once.

This is part two. It's about what living inside that platform actually meant — the named bugs, the invisible flag underneath most of them, the hacks written to reach one browser, the fix that was a typo on purpose, and the three words of HTML that could close a window on your own machine.

0:00Part two

This is part two. If you haven't seen part one, it's linked below. Here's the short version anyway. In two thousand and one Microsoft released Internet Explorer six, and it arrived with Windows XP. Within a couple of years it had more than ninety percent of the browser market. Microsoft used that to turn the browser into an application platform. It was a real idea. Web developers just happened to pay for it. That was part one. But I left something out. I left out the part where the bugs had names.

0:36A bug with a number

Normally a browser bug is a number in a tracker. Issue four one seven two. Nobody remembers it. Nobody talks about it at lunch. In Internet Explorer six the bugs had proper nouns. And that turns out to be the most telling thing about the whole era.

0:57The Three Pixel Jog

Start with a gentle one. You float an image to the left and put a paragraph beside it. The text should sit right next to the image. Instead it sits three pixels away. Not four. Not two. Three. Every time. It had a name. The Three Pixel Jog.

1:17The Double Margin Bug

Now float that image left and give it a left margin of ten pixels. Internet Explorer six gives it twenty. It doubles the margin, but only on the side you floated it to. The Double Margin Bug. And the accepted fix was to declare a floated element inline. Which is nonsense. A float is a block. Calling it inline means nothing at all. It worked, so people wrote it.

1:44Peekaboo

This next one is better. You load a page, and part of the content simply isn't there. You scroll down. You scroll back up. And it appears. Nothing loaded. Nothing changed. It was just shy. And it had the right name. The Peekaboo Bug. The remedy was to tell the missing element that its position was relative. Nobody could fully explain why that brought it back.

2:10The Guillotine

Then there is the one where you hover over a link, and the background of the box below it gets cut off in a straight line. The Guillotine Bug. There was a bug where text crept away from where you put it. Magic Creeping Text. There was one where borders came out in the wrong place, in the wrong colour, or not at all. Border Chaos.

2:32The Duplicate Character Bug

My favourite is quieter. You lay out a row of boxes, and letters from the last one turn up again underneath it. Not always. Not predictably. The Duplicate Character Bug. And what decided whether it happened to you, and how many letters came out doubled, was the number of HTML comments you had left in the markup. Comments. The one thing in the file the browser is supposed to throw away.

3:00The Expanding Box

There is one more, and it's different, because people used it on purpose. If you set a height and the content is taller, the box should overflow. Internet Explorer six grew the box instead. Which is wrong. It is also exactly what a minimum height does. And Internet Explorer six had no minimum height. So developers stopped reporting that one and started depending on it.

3:27You don't name a bug you meet once

Stop and look at that list. The Three Pixel Jog. The Double Margin Bug. Peekaboo. The Guillotine. You don't name a bug you meet once. These had names because everyone met them. And here is the part that makes it worse. Most of them had the same cause underneath.

3:48hasLayout

Inside Internet Explorer six, every element either did or did not have something called layout. It decided whether the element sized and positioned itself, or left that to its parent. And you could not set it. There was no property called layout, and no way to ask for it. It wasn't in any specification. It was an internal flag in the rendering engine. But you could flip it by accident. Setting a width did it. Setting a height did it. Floating an element did it. So half the fixes in this video were people flipping a switch they couldn't see.

4:27The Holly Hack

Which is why they went looking for the cheapest way to flip it on purpose. One was to set a height of one percent. Every other browser had to be kept away from that, so it went inside a hack we'll get to in a minute. And in Internet Explorer six it did no harm, because a height it can't fit is one it grows past anyway. The bug from two minutes ago is what made the fix safe. It had a name. The Holly Hack.

4:54zoom: 1

The other way was a Microsoft property called zoom, set to one. Zoom of one means do not zoom. It changed nothing you could see. That was the point. It sat in stylesheets purely to set an invisible flag that made the bugs go away.

5:14Talking to one browser

Which brings us to the hacks, and the hacks are where this gets strange. To fix one browser you first have to talk to only that browser. And Internet Explorer six behaved as though there was another element above the html element. No other browser did. It wasn't documented. It was simply how it acted. So you wrote a rule that matched the phantom, and only that browser could see it. Star, space, html. Every other browser looked at it, found nothing that matched, and moved on. That is the star hack, and for years it sat in almost every stylesheet on the web.

5:54The underscore hack

There was also the underscore hack. You put an underscore in front of a property name. A standards compliant browser throws the line away as invalid. Internet Explorer six ignores the underscore and applies it. So you deliberately broke your own stylesheet, and the breakage was the instruction.

6:15Conditional comments

And then there is the one Microsoft built on purpose. You could write a comment that only Internet Explorer obeyed. To every other browser it was an ordinary comment, ignored the way comments are. To Internet Explorer six it was a door into a second version of the page. Which means the vendor looked at the situation, agreed that this browser needed a stylesheet of its own, and shipped a feature to make that easier.

6:45Quirks mode

Some of the damage didn't need a bug at all. Internet Explorer six had two modes. In the modern one it followed the standard. In the old one, the width of a box included its padding and its border, which is a completely different number. Which mode you got depended on the first line of the document. And the version of HTML that everyone was moving to told you to put an XML declaration up there. That line pushed Internet Explorer six into the old mode. Following the newer standard was what broke it.

7:21Transparency

Then there is transparency. Internet Explorer six couldn't show a PNG with soft edges. Where the image should fade, it put grey. Microsoft's answer was a filter with a name like a serial number. It worked. But it painted outside the browser's normal rendering process, so links you put on top of it would often stop working. You fixed the picture and broke the link. And fading anything else was a second filter, with its own syntax, sitting in the stylesheet right next to the ordinary property that every other browser understood.

7:59Tables and the spacer gif

Some of this predates the bugs entirely. Before any of it, layouts were built out of nested tables. And to hold a gap open you used a transparent image one pixel across, stretched to whatever size the gap needed to be. It had a name too. The spacer gif.

8:19Hover, and a file next to the page

Hovering was another one. In Internet Explorer six, hover worked on links. Only on links. So a menu that opened when you moved the mouse over anything else needed a small program attached to the stylesheet. Not a script in the page. A separate file, referenced from CSS, holding code whose job was to teach the browser one selector. People passed that file around for years.

8:47JavaScript in a stylesheet

Part one mentioned that you could put JavaScript inside a stylesheet. What it didn't mention is how often that JavaScript ran. Every mouse move. Every keystroke. Every scroll. Over and over again, to work out one width.

9:04Ceilings nobody else had

And the browser had ceilings that nobody else had. A stylesheet stopped applying somewhere past four thousand rules. Anything after the thirty first stylesheet on a page was ignored. Which sounds like a number you would never reach, until your site is large enough that somebody has to write a build step whose only job is to merge stylesheets together before the browser sees them.

9:30The memory leak

And some of it wasn't cosmetic at all. Internet Explorer six counted its page objects and its script objects in two different ways, and neither side could see the whole loop. So if a script pointed at part of the page, and that part of the page pointed back at the script, neither was ever released. Leave that window open long enough and the browser ate the machine.

9:55The missing equals sign

But the best one isn't a layout bug at all. You write an input tag. You give it a type. And you forget the equals sign. That is a malformed attribute. Every parser ever written has an answer for that. Ignore it. Guess at it. Carry on. Internet Explorer crashed. Inside, it wanted to know whether the field was hidden, so it compared what you had typed against the word hidden. You hadn't typed anything. So it went to fetch the thing you hadn't typed from address zero. Which would be an ordinary browser crash, except that the code doing it was not in the browser.

10:34Out of the browser

It was in a shared system library. So those three words also took down the mail client, and the preview pane in the file manager. A window listing files on your own machine, closed by a web page. That is part one's argument arriving as a crash report. The browser wasn't a program running on the system. It was part of the system.

And you couldn't simply test the fix somewhere else. Internet Explorer was welded to the operating system, so one computer ran exactly one version.

11:12One machine, one version

Which is why a front end job in two thousand and six came with a shelf of spare machines. And later with a folder of virtual ones, each carrying a whole operating system so that one page could be opened inside it. And then somebody did the obvious thing. If you can teach the browser one missing feature, you can teach it all of them.

11:34A library to patch the browser

A developer called Dean Edwards wrote a library whose entire purpose was to make Internet Explorer six behave like a later version of itself. People shipped that. In production.

11:48What it cost

Which is where it stops being funny. For most of a decade, a serious share of the world's front end work went into one browser's mistakes. Not features. Not products. Compensation. And it lasted because of the thing part one was about. Companies had built real applications on it, the kind that only ran there, and those applications still had to work on Monday. So the browser that everybody wanted gone was the one nobody was allowed to remove. In two thousand and ten, Google announced that its own apps would stop working in it. And a design studio in Denver decided that deserved a funeral.

12:31The funeral

An actual one. A coffin. A eulogy. Between eighty and a hundred people came to watch a browser get buried. They had planned for thirty. And Microsoft sent flowers. The card said, thanks for the good times, Internet Explorer six.

12:48One year later

One year later, to the day, Microsoft launched a website. It tracked how much of the world was still running Internet Explorer six, country by country. At that point it was still twelve percent. The goal was to push it under one. The company that built the browser was running a public countdown to its own product's death.

13:12Why the bugs had names

So why did the bugs have names? Because you name the things you cannot get around. Nobody names the weather in a country they are passing through. You name it where you live. And for about ten years, that was where the web lived.
