Ordinary Things, Awkward Questions

How Many Genders Are There?

10 min2026-09-30

We separate the things the word "gender" often gets stretched to cover — reproductive roles, individual sex characteristics, social expectations, gender identity and gender expression — and look at what can and can't be counted in each of them. Along the way: Facebook's sixty custom gender options in 2014, what complete androgen insensitivity syndrome shows about chromosomes and anatomy, why an infertile man doesn't stop being male, and why three labels on a form don't automatically mean three genders.

This video isn't trying to settle every argument about sex and gender. It tries to make sure both sides are answering the same question.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Two, fifty-five, or hundreds?

Two genders. Fifty-five genders. Hundreds of genders. Before we start counting, what exactly goes on the list? Imagine a form with three options: woman, man, and non-binary. Now add another option: trans woman. Have we discovered another gender? Or added a more specific description? A trans woman can describe herself as both trans and a woman. Those answers overlap. Counting the labels as separate boxes already gives us a problem. Now imagine that someone replies: "But humans reproduce with sperm and eggs." That's a statement about biology. It doesn't tell us how many descriptions belong on that form.

This is how an argument about a number can hide an argument about a definition. One person counts reproductive categories. Another counts identities. Someone else counts words.

1:00Sixty options on Facebook

There is a real example of the problem. In 2014, Facebook announced sixty custom gender options for people using its German interface. Sixty options in a profile menu. The announcement described a way for people to express their identities. It wasn't the discovery of sixty kinds of reproductive system. But saying "it's just a menu" doesn't answer everything either. Why did people want those options? What were the original two boxes missing?

1:32Body, expectations, identity

To understand that, we need to separate three things: the body, the expectations around it, and the person living in it. They are connected. They aren't interchangeable.

1:52Sperm, eggs and reproductive roles

Let's start with the body. Human sexual reproduction involves two kinds of reproductive cell: sperm and eggs. These are called gametes. In reproductive biology, the male role is associated with sperm, and the female role with eggs. That distinction gives the categories a biological basis. It doesn't mean that every individual must be producing those cells right now. A boy doesn't become male at puberty. An infertile man doesn't stop being male. Equally, a woman doesn't stop being female when she reaches menopause. Reproductive organisation and current fertility are different things.

So the argument "some women can't have children" doesn't, by itself, make biological sex impossible to define. It shows why fertility alone is a poor test for an individual.

2:46How bodies develop

But the body has more going on than two labels on a diagram. Sex development involves chromosomes, genes, gonads, hormones, and the way tissues respond to them. Usually, these produce a familiar pattern. XY chromosomes, testes, and typically male anatomy. Or XX chromosomes, ovaries, and typically female anatomy.

3:10When chromosomes don't tell the whole story

Usually isn't the same as always. Consider a condition called complete androgen insensitivity syndrome. The name is a mouthful. The mechanism matters more. Someone with this condition has XY chromosomes and develops testes. But their cells do not respond to androgens, including testosterone. The signal is there. The cells cannot receive it. As a result, they are born with typically female external genitals. A different hormone from the testes prevents the uterus from developing. Chromosomes alone don't tell you all of that anatomy.

This is one example of a difference in sex development. Intersex is an umbrella term used for variations in sex characteristics. It doesn't describe one uniform body type. These variations don't introduce a third kind of human gamete. Two reproductive roles can coexist with variation in how bodies develop.

4:14Gender roles

Now move from the body to the expectations around it. Who is supposed to be tough? Who is supposed to do the caring? What counts as masculine or feminine? These are questions about gender roles. They concern what a society expects, encourages, or discourages. Those expectations can change between places and over time. A man caring for his children, or a woman who dislikes dresses, hasn't automatically changed gender. Breaking a stereotype and identifying as another gender are different things.

4:51Gender identity

Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their own gender. For some people, being a man or a woman fits the sex they were assigned at birth. For others, it doesn't. Cisgender describes that alignment. Transgender describes a gender identity that differs from the sex assigned at birth. These words describe a relationship. They aren't names for extra gametes. A definition tells us what a word means. It doesn't explain where gender identity comes from. Non-binary is an umbrella term for identities that aren't exclusively man or woman. It doesn't mean that everyone using it has exactly the same experience.

5:36Gender expression

Gender expression is how someone presents themselves through clothing, appearance or behaviour. A skirt tells you what someone is wearing. It doesn't tell you their chromosomes or their gender identity. So "gender" needs some context. Are we discussing social expectations, personal identity, or presentation? A conversation can slide between them without anyone noticing.

6:04Counting the labels

Now we can return to those long lists. Imagine writing "woman" on one card and "cisgender woman" on another. A third card says "transgender woman". We now have three cards. But the second and third are more specific descriptions within the first category. Three labels do not automatically mean three separate genders. So if someone says "fifty-five", ask to see the list. You can count its entries exactly. But to count distinct genders, you need to decide which labels overlap and which name separate categories. And there's no universally agreed rule for where one label stops and another begins.

This is also why "there are infinitely many" needs an explanation. An open-ended vocabulary isn't a measurement of infinity. It means the list hasn't been closed.

6:57Colours, and the limits of an analogy

You might compare this with colours. Is turquoise a separate colour, or a kind of blue or green? The answer depends partly on how finely you divide your categories. But that comparison has a limit. It illustrates a problem with naming. It doesn't establish that gender, or biological sex, is one smooth rainbow-shaped scale. An analogy isn't a biological model.

7:23"But people invented these words"

"But people invented these words." Yes. Money depends on human rules too. That doesn't make your rent imaginary. A description can matter in someone's life without naming a new reproductive category. Still, giving something a name doesn't automatically explain it. We can ask what a term includes, and whether it helps answer our question.

7:47What makes someone a woman?

That applies to short definitions too. Take the question: "What makes someone a woman?" Before choosing a test, notice which question you want it to answer. If you mean an adult human female, you are using a sex-based definition. You then need to say what you mean by female, and how you apply it to the individual in front of you. If you mean a gender identity, you are asking about the person's sense of being a woman. Answering that question with a chromosome count changes the subject being measured. This doesn't make every disagreement an innocent misunderstanding. People can understand the distinction and still disagree about which category should matter in a particular setting.

A vocabulary lesson won't settle all of those arguments. It can stop us treating an answer to one question as an automatic answer to every other one.

8:43So how many genders are there?

So how many genders are there? For human reproductive roles, we have the distinction between sperm and eggs. For individual sex characteristics, we also need to describe variation. For gender identities, there isn't a single universally agreed number of distinct categories. Man and woman don't describe everyone, and broader and narrower labels can overlap. For the options on a particular form, there is an exact answer. Count them. Just don't mistake the design of the form for a discovery about the whole species. The strange thing isn't that we have so many words to describe people. It's that we expect one number to describe bodies, identities, and the rules we build around them.

Before answering "two" or "fifty-five", finish the question. How many… of what?
