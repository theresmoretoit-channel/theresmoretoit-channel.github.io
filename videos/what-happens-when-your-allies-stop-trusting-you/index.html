What Happens When Your Allies Stop Trusting You?

11 min2026-10-17

So what happens to the most powerful country on Earth when its allies stop trusting it? Nobody storms out of an alliance. What happens is quieter: a second factory, a second supplier, a backup plan. This video follows that backup plan from Brussels to Madrid, Ottawa and Seoul, and then asks what the United States was getting from trust all along.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Article 5, invoked once

There is one sentence in the NATO treaty that every member has agreed to. An armed attack against one or more of them shall be considered an attack against them all. It is Article Five. In more than seventy-five years, it has been invoked exactly once. Not for Poland. Not for the Baltic states. Not for anyone in Europe. On the twelfth of September, 2001, the allies agreed to invoke it. A few weeks later, they confirmed it. The attack on the United States was an attack on them all. Over the next twenty years, soldiers from dozens of countries followed the US into Afghanistan. More than a thousand of them never came home.

Britain lost four hundred and fifty-seven. Canada lost a hundred and fifty-eight. Denmark, a country of six million, lost forty-four.

0:57An ally doing the maths

Twenty-five years later, a large survey asked Europeans how they see the United States. Only eleven per cent called it an ally. The most common answer was colder. A necessary partner. That is not hatred. In a way, it is worse. It is an ally doing the maths.

1:17The question

So what happens to the most powerful country on Earth when its allies stop trusting it? Not to them. To the United States.

The first part of the answer is not dramatic. Nobody storms out. What happens is quieter, and much harder to undo.

1:37Why trust is not soft

Trust sounds like something soft. Something for friends and families. Between countries, it is a working tool. Imagine two countries building a fighter jet together. It will take ten years, billions in shared factories, and parts that only fit each other. If both expect the partnership to survive the next election, that plan makes sense. But if one of them starts to wonder whether the other will walk away the moment it is convenient, the maths changes. It builds a second factory at home. It buys from two suppliers, not one. It keeps a backup plan it hopes it will never need.

Nobody announces anything. Nobody leaves an alliance. They just stop planning on the assumption that you'll be there, and start planning for the possibility that you won't.

2:27Reasons to wonder

And in the last few years, the allies of the United States were given a lot of reasons to do exactly that. Canada, which followed the US into Afghanistan, was hit with tariffs and told it should become the fifty-first state. Denmark, which lost forty-four soldiers there, was told the United States wanted Greenland, and for a year, would not rule out force. Ukraine, in the middle of a war, saw US intelligence switched off for a week. Today, Europe is Ukraine's largest source of military aid, and Europeans now pay for many of the US weapons that reach Ukraine.

And when diesel at home passed six dollars a gallon, Washington eased its sanctions so that Russian diesel could flow to the United States. Russia. The country that many US allies are spending billions to deter. Even if all of it came, it would cover less than two weeks of US demand. And the United States exports more diesel than it imports. Every one of these can be explained on its own. A negotiating tactic. An election. A price at the pump. But allies do not plan around explanations. They plan around patterns. And the lesson they drew was simple. A US promise could be taken back the next time something else mattered more.

3:51The backup plan

So the allies started building the backup plan. The European Union set up a hundred and fifty billion euros in loans to buy weapons together. And the rules favour Europe: at least sixty-five per cent of the cost of the parts has to come from Europe or its close partners. Spain shelved plans for the US-made F-35 fighter and is looking at European jets. Canada is reviewing its own F-35 order. In South Korea, a poll this year found that nearly two thirds of people want their own nuclear weapons. Read that one again. A country protected for seventy years by the US nuclear umbrella now wonders whether it needs a bomb of its own.

That is one of the risks when security guarantees lose their credibility. The question stops being who will protect us, and becomes how we protect ourselves.

4:48And then there is Canada

And then there is Canada. Canada sends about two thirds of its exports to the United States. The two share the longest border on Earth. Yet in 2026, Canada became the first country outside Europe to join the EU's joint arms-buying scheme. And in a poll this spring, when Canadians were asked whether their country should become a full member of the European Union, fifty-seven per cent said they would support it, at least somewhat. That is not government policy. Ottawa says full membership is not on the table. But ask yourself why so many Canadians are willing to entertain the idea.

5:30The part that should sting

Now here is the part that should sting in Washington. For decades, the United States asked its allies to spend more on their own defence. It was a fair request. And now they are. NATO allies have agreed to aim for five per cent of their economies by 2035. The US got exactly what it asked for. Except that some of that money now builds Europe's own industry, so that, over time, Europe depends less on US suppliers. There is a difference between asking an ally to carry more of the load, and convincing it that one day it may have to carry all of it.

The first makes an alliance stronger. The second makes it optional.

6:20What trust was buying

So that is what happens to the allies. They hedge. But the question was never about them. It was about the United States. To answer that, start with a question that sounds almost rude. What was the United States actually paying for? After the Second World War, it helped rebuild Western Europe, kept troops across the world, and promised to defend countries that could not defend themselves. That was never charity. Washington had its own interests, and it never hid them. But what it got back rarely showed up on a bill. Take the wounded and the sick from Iraq and Afghanistan. For two decades, tens of thousands of US and allied troops passed through one hospital. It was not in the United States. It was in Landstuhl, Germany.

The aircraft that flew them there landed at Ramstein, a US air base on German soil, under agreements already in place. Or take computer chips. The most advanced ones are made with machines that only one company in the world builds. It is Dutch. When Washington wanted to slow down China's chip industry, the Netherlands and Japan joined export restrictions on advanced chipmaking equipment. Or take money itself. Most of the world's foreign currency reserves are still held in US dollars. That helps the United States borrow more cheaply than it otherwise could.

A French finance minister had a name for that in the 1960s. An exorbitant privilege. Bases. Hospitals. Export controls. Sanctions that actually bite, when key partners join them. And an alliance that went to war when the US was attacked. None of that is free. But almost none of it is bought with money either. It is bought with trust.

8:16A country with options

So when the trust goes, the allies are left with options. And a country with options can say no. No to the next round of export controls. No to the next sanctions package. No to the base. No to the airspace. No to the vote at the United Nations. Not out of hostility. But because saying no no longer costs what it used to.

8:43Why it is hard to undo

And here is what makes it so hard to undo. Suppose the next government in Washington changes course. It apologises, renews every promise, and means it. The factory in Europe is already built. The new supplier has been tested. The contracts are signed. None of that disappears when relations improve. And the allies have learned something that cannot be unlearned. A promise from Washington may last only until the next election. So even the best promise now comes with an expiry date. That is the real cost. Not one bad deal, but every future deal worth less.

9:25Still the strongest

None of this means the United States is collapsing. It still has the strongest military, the deepest markets, and the world's most important currency. But power was never only what a country can do on its own. It was also what others did because it asked.

9:43The first call

For eighty years, when something went wrong in the world, the first call went to Washington. Not because anyone had to call. Because everyone assumed it would answer. The allies have not become enemies. They are still trading, still talking, still sitting at the same tables. They have just started keeping other numbers on speed dial. And in Denmark, forty-four families still remember the cost of answering that call. The call came from Washington.

10:16If you are watching in the United States

If you are watching this in the United States, this is not just a story about your allies. It is about what your country stands to lose. Some of these changes are already difficult to reverse. Others are still being decided. The United States is losing the advantages of trust, one quiet decision at a time. And that is still a choice.
