How Do We Find Exoplanets?

11 min2026-10-01

So how do astronomers find planets they usually cannot see? Mostly, they don't look for the planet itself. They look for what it does to everything around it.

A planet crossing in front of its star makes it slightly dimmer — for an Earth-sized planet and a Sun-like star, by about one part in twelve thousand. A planet makes its star move and shifts the lines in its spectrum. It can distort the light of a distant background star. And its atmosphere can leave a fingerprint in starlight. We go through the methods one by one, and look at what happens when two of them see the same planet.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A strange problem

There is a strange problem with finding planets outside our Solar System. They are everywhere. But we can barely see them. Imagine pointing the most powerful telescope ever built at another star system. You might expect to see something like this: A star. And next to it, a planet. Simple. Except that almost never works. A planet is incredibly faint compared with the star it orbits. And from dozens or hundreds of light-years away, the star and the planet appear almost like a single point of light. So how do astronomers find planets they usually cannot see? The answer is surprisingly clever.

They don't necessarily look for the planet. They look for what the planet does to everything around it. And there are several ways to do that.

0:53Direct imaging

Let's start with the most obvious one. Direct imaging. If we can somehow block the overwhelming light of a star, a sufficiently bright planet can appear next to it. This is not science fiction. Astronomers have directly imaged some exoplanets. But there is a catch. The planets we can image directly tend to be large, young, and still relatively hot. A young planet can glow strongly in infrared light because it is still radiating heat left over from its formation. Even then, separating the planet from its star is extremely difficult.

A telescope may need a special instrument called a coronagraph. A coronagraph blocks or suppresses the light from the star while allowing light from nearby objects to reach the detector.

1:50A single point of light

And even after all that, we are usually not looking at a detailed picture of a planet. We are looking at a tiny point of light. But that tiny point can contain an astonishing amount of information. If we split its light into a spectrum, we can look for the chemical fingerprints of molecules in the planet's atmosphere. So direct imaging can potentially tell us not just: "There is something there." But: "There is a planet there — and this is what its atmosphere may contain."

2:21Waiting for a transit

Still, direct imaging is difficult. Fortunately, there is a much easier trick. We can wait for the planet to get in the way.

Imagine looking at a star from far away. Now imagine a planet crossing directly between the star and us. The planet blocks a tiny fraction of the star's light. The star gets slightly dimmer. And then, when the planet moves away, the star becomes bright again. That's a transit. The change can be incredibly small. For an Earth-sized planet crossing a Sun-like star, the drop in brightness is only about zero point zero zero eight per cent. That's about one part in twelve thousand. A tiny change in a very bright signal. But modern telescopes can measure tiny changes in brightness.

And if the same dip happens again and again at regular intervals, we have something very interesting. A planet candidate. Other causes, such as an eclipsing pair of stars, have to be ruled out first. The time between the dips tells us the planet's orbital period. The depth of the dip tells us approximately how large the planet is compared with its star. And the shape and duration of the transit can reveal additional information about the orbit and the geometry of the system. This is one of the reasons missions such as Kepler and TESS have been so successful. They don't need to photograph thousands of planets individually. They simply watch enormous numbers of stars and ask: "Did any of them get slightly darker?"

But there is an important limitation. A transit only happens if the planet's orbit happens to be aligned so that the planet crosses the star from our point of view. A planet can be there and never transit at all.

4:13Without waiting for a transit

So what if we could detect a planet without waiting for it to pass in front of its star? We can. And this next method is particularly clever.

It is tempting to imagine a planet orbiting a stationary star. But that's not quite what happens. A planet and its star both orbit their common centre of mass. Usually the star is much more massive, so the centre of mass is very close to the star. But the star still moves. Tiny amounts. And that movement can be detected. How? With a spectrograph.

4:49Starlight and the Doppler effect

A spectrograph spreads starlight into its component wavelengths. Stellar spectra contain dark absorption lines produced by atoms and molecules in stellar atmospheres. If the star moves slightly towards us, those spectral lines shift towards shorter wavelengths. If it moves away from us, they shift towards longer wavelengths. This is the Doppler effect. So instead of seeing the planet, we watch the star's spectrum. If the spectral lines repeatedly move back and forth, something may be pulling the star around. And one possible explanation is a planet.

This technique is usually called the radial velocity method. It can tell us about the planet's orbital period and the size of the star's motion. And from that, with an estimate of the star's mass, astronomers can estimate the planet's mass — more precisely, its minimum mass unless the orbital inclination is known.

5:47Combining two methods

Now something really interesting happens when we combine the two methods. Suppose a planet passes in front of its star. The transit tells us the planet's size relative to its star. If we know the star's size, we can work out the planet's size too. The radial velocity method tells us how strongly the planet pulls on its star. That gives us information about its mass. And if we know both mass and size, we can calculate density.

6:17What is it made of?

Suddenly, the planet stops being just a mysterious dot. We can start asking what it might actually be made of. A planet with a relatively high density could be rocky. A much lower density could indicate a large atmosphere surrounding a smaller solid core, or a planet rich in water or gas. But density alone cannot tell us exactly what a planet is made of. And this is one of the most powerful ideas in exoplanet astronomy: Sometimes different methods don't just find the same planet. They answer different parts of the puzzle.

6:55Gravitational microlensing

But there is an even stranger method. One where we don't need the planet to cross its star. And we don't even need to watch the star wobble. Imagine a distant star behind another star. As the foreground star passes in front of it, its gravity bends the light from the background star. The foreground star acts like a gravitational lens. The background star temporarily appears brighter. Now add a planet orbiting the foreground star. The planet's gravity can produce an additional distortion. A brief anomaly in the brightening curve. That anomaly can reveal the planet.

This is called gravitational microlensing. And it is particularly useful for finding planets that can be difficult to detect by other methods. But microlensing has a strange disadvantage. The event may happen only once. After the stars move apart, the alignment is gone. So unlike a transit, where we can watch the same planet pass in front of its star again and again, a microlensing event can be more like catching a rare cosmic coincidence. One moment, something happens. Then it's gone.

8:11Reading an atmosphere

Finding a planet is only the beginning. Astronomers can sometimes study its atmosphere by looking at starlight that passes through it during a transit. Imagine the planet moving across the star. Most of the starlight travels directly to us. But a tiny fraction passes through the planet's atmosphere first. Different atoms and molecules absorb different wavelengths of light. So when astronomers compare the star's spectrum during a transit with its spectrum outside the transit, they may see tiny changes. Those changes can reveal the fingerprints of atoms and molecules in the atmosphere. Water vapour. Carbon dioxide. Methane. Sodium. And other substances, depending on the planet and the observations.

The signals can be incredibly faint. But modern instruments can measure them. So a planet that is completely invisible in an ordinary image can leave a fingerprint in the light of its star.

9:16Back to the original question

And that brings us back to the original question. How do we find exoplanets? Sometimes we actually image them. But much more often, we detect their effects. A planet can make its star slightly dimmer. It can make its star move. It can distort the light from a distant background star. Its atmosphere can alter the spectrum of starlight. In other words, astronomers have developed several ways of detecting something without directly seeing it. And that is perhaps the most fascinating part.

9:50What "discovered" really means

When you hear that astronomers have discovered an exoplanet hundreds of light-years away, it does not necessarily mean that a telescope took a photograph of a little blue or green world. Often, the evidence is much more subtle. A tiny dip in brightness. A microscopic shift in a spectral line. A brief gravitational distortion. A few missing wavelengths in a spectrum. And from these tiny signals, astronomers reconstruct an entire world. Its orbit. Its size. Its mass. Its density. And sometimes even clues about its atmosphere.

All from light that has travelled across the galaxy to reach us. The planet itself may be almost impossible to see. But it cannot completely hide the consequences of being there. And that's how we find worlds we cannot see.
