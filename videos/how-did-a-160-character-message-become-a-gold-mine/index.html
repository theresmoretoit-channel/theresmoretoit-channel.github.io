How Did a 160-Character Message Become a Gold Mine?

10 min2026-10-31

This video follows the text message from a typewriter in 1985 to the phone in your pocket: where the number 160 really came from, why a single accented letter could cut your message by more than half, what the very first text said, and how a message could wait for a phone that was switched off.

Along the way: free texting from operators' own websites, why MMS was the awkward teenager of messaging, how internet messaging apps bypassed the gold mine, and why SMS still carries your login codes when nothing else works.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Data at an absurd price

In the early 2000s, mobile operators were selling data at a price that sounds absurd today. Not mobile internet. Text messages. A single text message carried at most 140 bytes. At ten cents a message, one megabyte of texts would cost you more than seven hundred dollars. And people paid it. Billions of times a day. So how did a tiny side feature of the mobile network become a gold mine? And why was every message limited to exactly 160 characters?

0:36Where 160 came from

Let's start with that number, because it has two parents. In 1985, a German engineer called Friedhelm Hillebrand sat down at a typewriter. He typed out ordinary sentences and questions, and counted the characters. Almost all of them fit into 160. He checked postcards and telex messages too. Most of those were shorter than 160 as well. So the first parent is human. 160 characters was enough to say most things people actually say.

1:12The network's spare capacity

The second parent is the network. Engineers were designing GSM, the new European standard for digital mobile phones. And a phone doesn't only carry your voice. It constantly exchanges small messages with the network. I'm here. I've moved to another cell. A call is coming in. That's called signalling. And most of the time, those channels sit almost empty. So the idea was modest. What if short text messages could ride along in that spare capacity?

1:46140 bytes, 160 characters

But a signalling message is small. The room left for the text was 140 bytes. 140 bytes is 1120 bits. Simple text doesn't need eight bits per character. GSM used seven. And 1120 divided by seven is exactly 160. It wasn't a coincidence. The first design had room for only about 146 characters. So the engineers stretched it until 160 fitted.

2:22One letter can halve a message

There's a catch, though. Seven bits only give you a hundred and twenty-eight characters. That covers English, and a handful of accented letters. But not the Czech ř. Not the Polish ł, the Hungarian ő, or the Cyrillic alphabet. Use one of those, and the phone switches to an encoding with sixteen bits per character. And the same 140 bytes now hold only 70 characters. One letter could cut your message by more than half. Which is why, for years, millions of people wrote their texts without accents at all.

3:00The first text message

The service itself was called SMS. Short Message Service. And nobody expected it to become the way people talk. It was a small extra built into the network. Something like a pager: a short note for someone on the move. On the 3rd of December 1992, the first text message was sent across a British network. It said: "Merry Christmas." It was typed on a computer, not a phone. The phone that received it could display the message. But it had no way to send one back.

3:38Store and forward

Getting a message to someone worked like a post office. Your phone handed the text to a server in the network, the SMS Centre. If the recipient's phone was switched off, the message waited. Out of coverage? It waited. And when the phone came back, the network tried again. Engineers call it store and forward. No internet, no account, no app. Just a mobile signal.

4:06Texting takes off

Then phones learned to send texts, and people discovered something the designers hadn't really planned for. A phone call needs both people at the same time. A text doesn't need that. You could send "Where are you?" and carry on with your day. The other person could answer in a minute, or in an hour. And on a keypad with three or four letters on each key, people got astonishingly fast. The phone stopped being just a telephone. It became a personal messaging device.

4:40The gold mine

And that's where the gold mine opened.

Think about what a text message costs the network. The towers were already there. The signalling channels were already there. And each message was at most a hundred and forty bytes. But customers didn't pay for bytes. They paid per message. Or for a bundle of a few hundred messages a month. Prices varied a lot from country to country. In much of Europe, you paid for every single text. In the United States, texting caught on later, and you often paid to receive a text, too. But the basic arithmetic was the same almost everywhere.

A tiny amount of data, sold as a separate service. And sold billions of times a day. For operators, it became one of the most profitable things they had ever sold.

5:37Free texting, one window away

And then it got a little strange. Some operators offered free texting from their own websites. Type a number, type a message, send. Programmers noticed. If a website can send a text, a program can use that website. Soon there were apps that sent texts from your computer, through the operators' own gateways. The service people paid for on their phones was sometimes free one window away.

6:06MMS

But 160 characters had another limit. What if you wanted to send a picture? Enter MMS. Multimedia Messaging Service. Pictures, sound, short videos, longer text. And here's the clever part. An MMS didn't travel like a text. First, your phone received a small hidden message. Something like: there's a picture waiting for you. Then the phone went online and downloaded it. Which meant MMS needed mobile data. It needed the right settings from your operator. And it had size limits that squeezed photos into a blurry little rectangle.

It was often expensive. And sometimes, it simply didn't arrive. MMS was the awkward teenager between the text message and everything that came next.

6:59Messaging over the internet

And what came next was the internet on your phone. Once phones had a reliable data connection, a message didn't need the operator's messaging service at all. It could travel as ordinary data. Apps like WhatsApp and Facebook Messenger sent texts, pictures, voice messages and videos the same way. The economics of messaging flipped. Instead of one message, one charge, thousands of messages became a rounding error inside your data plan. The gold mine didn't run dry. It was simply bypassed.

7:38Why SMS never went away

But SMS didn't disappear. Because it still has one trick that most internet messengers don't. Mobile signal: yes. Wi-Fi: no. Mobile data: no. Even then, SMS still works. That's why it carries the things that can't wait for a good connection. Login codes. Bank alerts. Delivery updates. And the occasional "Call me."

8:05RCS

The mobile industry did try to give the old system a modern successor. It's called RCS. Rich Communication Services. Typing indicators. Read receipts. Group chats. Proper photos. Reactions. Basically, everything people had been getting from messaging apps for years. But RCS needs an internet connection. So when the data is gone, the most modern system steps aside, and the phone can fall back to the oldest one.

8:39Back to 160 characters

A message designed in the 1980s, squeezed into the gaps of a signalling channel, limited to 160 characters. It became a gold mine. It was bypassed by the internet. And it's still the thing your phone reaches for when nothing else works.

RCS has one more twist, though. For years, the best-known phone in the world simply didn't speak it. But that's a story for another video. So here's a question for you. Was the 160-character limit a handicap texting survived, or part of the reason it caught on? Let me know in the comments.
