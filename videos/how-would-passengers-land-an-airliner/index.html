Ordinary Things, Awkward Questions

How Would Passengers Land an Airliner?

10 min2026-10-09

So what if they hadn't been? In a 2023 survey, almost half of American men said they could land a passenger plane with only air traffic control to help. One airline pilot put their chances at almost zero. What would a cabin full of passengers actually have to do to get an airliner down?

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00Dubai to Tel Aviv

At the end of September 2026, a Boeing 737 took off from Dubai, heading for Tel Aviv. Somewhere along the way, the co-pilot attacked the captain with the cockpit's emergency axe. The airliner dropped more than four kilometres in about a minute. The injured captain, lying on the floor, managed to unlock the cockpit door. Passengers rushed in and overpowered the co-pilot. And there was one more thing. Two spare flydubai pilots were sitting in the cabin. They took the controls and landed in Tabuk, in Saudi Arabia. They were only there because the airline expected a long detour. So here's the uncomfortable question. What if they hadn't been?

What if everyone behind that door was just… a passenger? How would they get the plane down?

0:54Could you land it?

In a 2023 survey, almost half of American men said they could land a passenger plane, with only air traffic control to help. One airline pilot put their chances at almost zero. In a way, both are right. An untrained passenger almost certainly can't fly an airliner. But they might not have to. Because a modern airliner can already land itself. Not in the future. Today. So the passengers don't need to learn how to fly the plane. They need to work out how to make the plane fly itself. That sounds simple. It isn't. And the first problem has nothing to do with flying.

1:37The door

It's a door. Since the attacks of 2001, cockpit doors have been reinforced and locked from inside. They're built to keep people out. But the cabin crew know an emergency code. If nobody in the cockpit denies access within a set time, often under half a minute, the door unlocks.

And once the passengers are inside, there's some good news.

2:03Don't touch anything

At cruising altitude, the plane is almost certainly flying on autopilot already. It holds its height and speed, follows its route, without anyone touching anything. So the most important rule is the one that sounds the laziest. Don't touch anything. The autopilot won't make any decisions. But it will keep the plane flying. And that buys them the most valuable thing in this whole story. Time.

2:33When time runs out

But time doesn't last forever. In 2005, a Helios Airways 737 climbed without its cabin pressurised, and both pilots passed out. The autopilot flew on to Athens, and circled there, perfectly, for over an hour. A flight attendant with a pilot's licence finally got into the cockpit. But by then, the fuel was running out. A hundred and twenty-one people died. So the autopilot gives you time. It doesn't give you a plan.

3:08The radio

For that, the passengers need someone who actually knows this aircraft. And that means the radio. Not a phone. Eleven kilometres up, a phone often has no signal at all. The radio is probably still set to the last controller the pilots were talking to. And the button to talk is right on the control column. Press it, and say: "Mayday, mayday, mayday. The pilots are incapacitated."

3:36A Cessna over Florida

That works. In 2022, a passenger called Darren Harrison did exactly this, in a small Cessna over Florida. His pilot had collapsed. Harrison had never flown a plane. "I have no idea how to fly the airplane," he told the controller. Controllers found Robert Morgan, a controller who was also a flight instructor. And Morgan talked him down, step by step, onto the runway at Palm Beach.

But a Cessna isn't a 737. An airliner lands about twice as fast, and can weigh nearly twenty times as much. Flying that onto a runway by hand, with no experience, is where the optimism of that survey runs out.

4:24Many hands

So the voice on the radio isn't going to teach them to fly. It's going to help them use the plane's own automation. And this is where having several passengers helps. One sits in the seat and talks. One reads out what the screens say. And if the plane has wi-fi, a third might even get a video call through to the airline, and point the camera at the instruments. Nobody has to understand the whole aircraft. They just have to describe it, one instrument at a time. On the ground, the controller can bring in an airline pilot who flies this exact type.

5:03The instrument landing system

Then comes the part most passengers don't know exists. Many large airports have an instrument landing system. It tells a plane two things: where the runway is, and at what angle to descend towards it. And an autopilot that's equipped for it can follow those signals, all the way down. On many airliners, it doesn't stop there. Set up correctly, it can raise the nose just before touchdown, pull the engines back to idle, and put the wheels on the runway. Pilots call it autoland. It was built for fog, for landing when the crew can barely see the runway.

The first airliner to land itself with passengers on board did it in 1965. So can an airliner land itself? Yes. It's been doing it for over sixty years.

But it can't decide to. Somebody still has to tell it to.

6:02The best case

And that's the best case. The runway needs the right equipment. The plane needs the right systems. And if anything goes wrong, nobody in that cockpit knows what they're doing. Worse, the plane may not be able to land itself at all. The runway might have no landing system, or something on board might have failed. Then someone has to fly it down by hand. The voice on the radio can explain every control. But it can't move a single one of them.

6:32The simulator test

So how would that actually go? In 2023, the Washington Post tried it. Four people who had never flown, an Airbus simulator, and instructors on the radio. Two of them switched off the autopilot, and crashed. One touched down, and ran off the runway. One refused to land on the runway at all, and headed for a lake. The only good landing came after the instructors stopped the simulation and coached her in, step by step.

7:03Runway or field?

Which leads to a choice that feels wrong. If the landing might go badly, why not aim for something soft? A field. A desert. The sea. Because a runway isn't hard. It's forgiving. It's kilometres long, flat, and clear of obstacles. Fire crews can be waiting beside it. And if you land too fast, there's room left to stop. A field has ditches, fences and trees. And water can rip an airliner apart if the plane hits at the wrong angle. Even the famous landing on the Hudson in 2009 was the work of a captain with almost twenty thousand hours of flying.

So if a suitable airport is within reach, the answer is almost always the runway.

7:58Why not fly it from the ground?

Which leaves one last question. If an airliner can land itself, why can't someone just take over from the ground? Because a link that lets someone fly an airliner from the ground would be a security problem almost as big as the emergency itself. So aviation is looking in a different direction. Not a pilot on the ground. A plane that doesn't need one.

8:23Emergency Autoland

Since 2020, some small private planes have had a system called Emergency Autoland. One button, meant for passengers. The plane picks an airport, tells air traffic control what's happening, flies there. Then it lands, and brakes to a stop. In December 2025, it was used for real for the first time. A King Air lost cabin pressure over Colorado. Both pilots were on oxygen and chose to let the system fly. It landed near Denver, and everyone walked away.

Nothing like that is fitted to the airliners most of us fly on. But Airbus has tested the idea on an A350. In flight tests, the plane responded to simulated pilot incapacitation, planned a new route, and talked to air traffic control.

9:17So, could passengers land it?

So, could passengers land an airliner? Probably not by flying it. But they might not have to. If the plane is stable, the autopilot is working, and they can reach someone on the ground, their job changes completely. They don't have to become pilots. They have to become the hands and eyes of the people on the ground. And the plane can do the flying. The safest airliner isn't one that never needs a pilot. It's one that knows what to do when the pilot is gone.
