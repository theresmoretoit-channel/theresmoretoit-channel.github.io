Why Do Drones Fly on a Wire?

8 min2026-10-10

In the age of satellites and wireless everything, why would anyone fly a drone on a wire? How does a drone weighing a few kilograms manage kilometres of cable, what does a 1980s US Army missile have to do with it, and what happens to all the fiber left lying in the fields?

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00A drone on a thread

Imagine a small drone flying low over a field. It looks like any other drone. But behind it, something is unwinding. A thread about as thick as the thinnest string on a guitar. And that thread can be twenty kilometres long. Some are now forty. The drone isn't talking to its pilot by radio. It's connected by a strand of glass. In the age of satellites and wireless everything, why would anyone fly a drone on a wire?

The answer starts with a number.

0:34Ten thousand drones a month

In 2023, a British defence think tank estimated that Ukraine was losing around ten thousand drones a month. And a big part of the reason wasn't guns. It was jamming. A radio drone runs on two signals. Video goes back to the pilot. Commands come forward to the drone. And a jammer doesn't need to hack either of them. It just shouts louder on the same frequencies. The drone can no longer hear its pilot. It drifts, or it lands, or it falls out of the sky. Designers moved to new frequencies. The jammers followed. Then they moved again.

1:15Light instead of radio

So someone asked a much simpler question. What if the drone didn't use the air at all?

An optical fibre carries light, not radio. Pulses of light, bouncing along inside a strand of glass. Commands go out through the fibre. Video comes back through the same fibre. A jammer can fill the sky with noise. But this drone isn't listening to the sky.

1:43Twenty kilometres of cable?

There's just one part that sounds impossible. Twenty kilometres of cable, dragged behind a drone that weighs a few kilograms? It isn't dragged. The spool rides on the drone, and the fibre unwinds from it as it flies. Whatever is already laid out just lies still on the ground. The drone never pulls its tail. It only lays it down, the way a spider leaves a thread. That's how something this fragile survives the flight at all.

2:22An old idea

And the idea isn't new. For more than half a century, anti-tank missiles have unspooled a thin wire behind them. One of the best known, TOW, literally ends in "wire-guided". In the 1980s, the US Army replaced that wire with fibre and put a camera in the nose. The missile sent its picture back down the fibre, and the operator could steer it from behind a hill, seeing the target only on a screen. It was cancelled. Then revived. Then cancelled again. Mostly, it was too expensive.

2:57What changed

What changed is the price of everything else. The telecom industry now makes hundreds of millions of kilometres of optical fibre every year. And a small racing drone costs a few hundred dollars. Put the two together, and an idea that was too expensive for a missile programme becomes cheap enough to throw away after one flight. By 2025, both sides of the war were using fibre drones in large numbers.

3:27More than one advantage

And it turned out that beating the jammer was only the first advantage. A radio drone isn't just listening. It's also transmitting. And so is its pilot. Every signal can be picked up, and traced back to where it came from. A pilot on a fibre is silent. There's nothing in the air to trace. And radio struggles when there's something in the way. A hill. A forest. A concrete wall. Light inside a fibre doesn't care. Wherever the drone can go, the signal goes with it. Unjammable. Silent. Works behind hills and inside buildings.

4:08Why isn't every drone on a wire?

So why isn't every drone on a wire?

Start with the weight. The fibre carries information, not electricity. The drone still runs on its own battery. And the spool has to come along for the whole flight. A ten-kilometre spool weighs about a kilogram. That's weight the drone can't carry as anything else. They're described as sluggish, and easily pushed around by wind.

Then there's the length. When the spool is empty, the drone has reached the end of its leash. And it's not a leash you can wind back in. Each fibre is laid once.

And then there's the tail. Imagine flying through a forest while laying a thread behind you. It can catch on branches. It can snag on wires and buildings. So the pilot has two questions, not one. Can the drone get there? And can the fibre?

5:09The thread points back

But the strangest problem is that the thread points. It lies on the ground, from the drone all the way back to the pilot. Where it hangs in grass and branches, it glints in low sunlight. And from the air, a line like that can be followed back to the pilot. So the defence changed too. If you can't jam a drone, you have to stop it physically. Along some supply roads, both sides now stretch nets over the road itself, for kilometres. A tunnel of netting, because the airwaves no longer protect you.

5:44The problem moved

That's the pattern here. The fibre didn't solve the drone problem. It moved it. An invisible link that could be jammed became a physical one that can be tangled, cut and followed.

5:57Fiber on the ground

And there's one more place the problem moved to. The ground itself. One flight leaves kilometres of fibre lying across the landscape. Now multiply that by every fibre drone flown on this front. In some places, fields and tree lines shimmer with threads, like a spider's web the size of a valley. Glass, in a plastic coating. It doesn't rot. At most, it crumbles into microplastic.

6:27A nest

And in June 2026, a photo from eastern Ukraine spread around the world. A bird's nest, knocked out of a tree by a bomb. Woven out of grass… and drone fibre. That same month, the army sent two nests like it to a war museum in Kyiv. Nobody knows yet how much harm the fibre does to birds. But long thin threads can wrap around legs and wings. Owls have already been found hanging in them.

6:57A wire again

For a century, we've been cutting wires. Phones, networks, aircraft. Wireless everything. And in the most crowded radio sky on Earth, the answer turned out to be a wire again.

When the war ends, the jammers will be switched off, and the noise in the air will stop that same day. The threads will still be there.
