Physics That Sounds Simple Until You Ask

Why Haven't We Found a Fourth Spatial Dimension?

7 min2026-10-18

The best clue may be hanging on your fridge. A small magnet holding up a sheet of paper beats the gravity of the entire Earth. Physicists call this the hierarchy problem, and in 1998 three of them proposed that gravity only looks weak because part of it spreads into dimensions we can't see. That idea makes predictions, and physicists have checked them three ways: a twisting disc in a lab in Seattle, particle collisions at the Large Hadron Collider, and a ripple in space from two neutron stars that merged 130 million light-years away. Here's what each test looked for, and where a fourth dimension could still be hiding.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Why doesn't the air leak out?

Close the door of your room. Shut the windows. The air inside stays inside. It doesn't seep away in a direction you can't point to. That sounds too obvious to say. But last time, we imagined a fourth direction of space, one that would let you reach into a locked safe without opening it. So if that direction is real, why isn't everything leaking into it? The air from your room. The heat from your coffee. You. Part of the answer, we already have. A big, open fourth dimension would wreck the solar system. Planets couldn't hold their orbits.

So if there's one at all, it has to be hidden. Curled up too small to notice, or out of reach, because we're stuck to our three.

0:48A clue on your fridge

And for the second idea, there may be a clue hanging on your fridge. A small magnet holds up a sheet of paper. Pulling down on that paper is the gravity of the entire Earth. And the magnet wins.

1:02A trillion trillion trillion times

Take two protons. The electric push between them is stronger than their gravity by about a trillion trillion trillion times. Physicists call this the hierarchy problem. Why is gravity so absurdly weak? Nobody knows.

1:18An idea from 1998

In 1998, three physicists offered a startling answer. Maybe gravity isn't weak at all. Maybe it only looks weak to us. Picture our universe as ink on a page, like last time. Atoms, light and people are stuck to the page. Gravity isn't. It spreads into the space around the page as well. So by the time it reaches us, it's diluted. It feels feeble, because it's shared with directions we can't see.

1:57What it predicts

It's only an idea. But it's a useful one, because it makes predictions. And predictions can be checked. And that gives us a test. Far apart, gravity follows the old rule. Double the distance, and it gets four times weaker. But bring two objects closer than the size of those hidden dimensions, and gravity would suddenly grow stronger than it should. In 1998, nobody could rule it out, because nobody had measured gravity at distances that small.

2:28Test one: gravity on a lab bench

It's brutally hard to do. At that scale, gravity is drowned out by almost everything else. Stray electric charges. Vibration. A change in temperature. In Seattle, a team at the University of Washington hangs a thin disc from a wire, and turns another disc beneath it. If gravity changed at short range, the hanging disc would twist in a telltale rhythm. In 2020, they reported that gravity still follows the old rule down to fifty-two micrometres. That's thinner than a human hair. So any hidden dimensions that gravity leaks into must be smaller than that. And the version from 1998 that needed them a millimetre wide is ruled out.

3:16Test two: the Large Hadron Collider

If gravity really is strong at tiny distances, then smashing particles together hard enough could let gravity take over. Theory said the result could be a microscopic black hole. At the Large Hadron Collider, physicists went looking for those black holes on purpose. Finding one would have meant gravity leaks into hidden dimensions. They also looked for something subtler. A collision where some energy seems to vanish, as if something had slipped off the page and carried it away. But missing energy isn't a photograph of another dimension. Ghostly particles called neutrinos slip out of the detector too. So every event like that has to be checked against everything we already know.

So far, the books balance. No black holes. No missing energy beyond what known physics explains.

4:11Test three: two neutron stars

The third test came from a hundred and thirty million light-years away. In August 2017, detectors on Earth picked up a ripple in space itself. Two neutron stars, the collapsed cores of dead stars, had spiralled into each other and merged. Less than two seconds later, a space telescope caught a burst of gamma rays from the same patch of sky. For the first time, the same event had been seen in gravitational waves and in light. And that let physicists ask a very simple question. If gravity leaked into extra dimensions along the way, the ripple would arrive fainter than it should. The galaxy would seem further away in gravitational waves than in light.

It didn't. The two distances agreed. Across a hundred and thirty million light-years, the ripple faded just as it would if gravity had only our three dimensions to spread through. If any of it leaked, it was too little to see.

5:14The scorecard

So here's the scorecard. On a lab bench, no leak. In the collider, no leak. Across the universe, no leak. That doesn't mean there's no fourth dimension. It means the hiding places we've checked are empty, and the ones left are smaller, or stranger.

5:32The oldest hiding place

The oldest hiding place is still open. A dimension curled up far smaller than any experiment can reach. Even then, it might leave a trace. In some theories, a particle moving around that tiny circle would show up to us as a heavier copy of a familiar particle. That doesn't mean an electron is secretly a tiny four-dimensional ball. It's a specific prediction: new particles, at particular masses. None has been found.

6:02String theory

String theory offers one reason physicists still take this seriously. Its consistent versions require extra dimensions. But a mathematical requirement is not evidence that they're real.

6:18Footprints, not visitors

Remember the square in Flatland. He learned about the third dimension because a sphere came to visit. We're not waiting for a visitor. We're looking for footprints. A pull that's slightly too strong at the width of a hair. A collision that doesn't add up. A ripple that arrives too faint. Meanwhile, the magnet on your fridge is still beating the whole planet. And nobody knows why. So the question has changed. It's no longer what another dimension would look like. It's what it could do that would finally give it away.
