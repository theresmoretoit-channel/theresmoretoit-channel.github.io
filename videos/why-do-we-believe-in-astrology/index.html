Why Do We Believe in Astrology?

13 min2026-08-27

That is not the strange part. The strange part is that a description written for everybody can still feel like it was written for you. This video starts with a thirteenth constellation the zodiac quietly leaves out, works through what a sign actually is once you notice it is not a constellation, and ends up somewhere more interesting than astrology: the machinery in your head that finds meaning in noise.

Along the way: a psychologist who handed thirty-nine students the same personality profile and got an average score of 4.26 out of 5, a double-blind test in Nature that the astrologers themselves helped design, and why one experiment is never the last word — including that one.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A question about your star sign

You know your star sign. Maybe you are a Leo. Maybe a Scorpio. Maybe a Capricorn. And you have probably read your horoscope at least once and thought: "Okay. That actually sounds like me." But here is a strange question. Why? Why should the position of Mars, Venus or Jupiter on the day you were born have anything to do with who you are? And if the stars really do decide what you are like, why can't astrologers even agree on which sign you belong to?

0:29The thirteenth constellation

Because there is a rather large problem hiding in the sky. It is called Ophiuchus. The zodiac supposedly has twelve signs. Aries. Taurus. Gemini. Cancer. Leo. Virgo. Libra. Scorpio. Sagittarius. Capricorn. Aquarius. Pisces. Nice and tidy. Twelve signs. Twelve months. Everything fits. Except the Sun does not actually pass through twelve constellations. It passes through thirteen. The thirteenth is Ophiuchus. The Serpent Bearer. And yes, the Sun really does move across it every year, for nearly three weeks, from the end of November.

So if your personality is decided by the constellation behind the Sun on the day you were born, what happens if you were born while the Sun was in Ophiuchus? Are you a Scorpio? A Sagittarius? Or the forbidden thirteenth sign? Western astrology has an answer, and it is a perfectly good one. Its twelve signs were never based on the boundaries of the constellations. There is no thirteenth sign to add, because the signs of astrology are not the constellations in the sky.

1:41Signs are not constellations

Because the real constellations do not sit in twelve equal slices of thirty degrees each. They are different sizes, and the Sun spends different amounts of time in them. So the real sky looks something like this. And the astrological zodiac looks like this. The second one is wonderfully neat. The first one is what the sky really does.

2:05So what is a sign?

So what is a sign, if it is not a star? It is an angle. Thirty degrees of a circle that starts where the Sun stands on the first day of spring. Pinned to our seasons, not to anything out there. And that matters, because the Earth is wobbling. Very slowly. Its axis of rotation precesses, like a spinning top, and one full wobble takes roughly twenty-six thousand years. So the first day of spring creeps along the sky, about one degree every seventy-two years, away from the stars it once lined up with. Western astrology does not mind. Its signs follow the seasons, and the seasons have not moved.

2:47Two schools, two answers

But the astrology practised across India made the opposite choice. It kept its signs tied to the stars, and it corrects for the drift. That correction has a name and a number. Today it is about twenty-four degrees. Which is most of a whole sign. So take one person. One birth, one sky, one set of planets. Western astrology calls them a Leo. Indian astrology calls them a Cancer. Work out how often that happens and it comes to roughly four people out of every five. Four in five would get a different sign depending on which astrologer they asked.

Which gives the question we started with a much sharper edge. If two systems cannot even agree on which thirty-degree slice is yours, the real question is why thirty degrees of anything should describe a person at all.

3:43How a horoscope is calculated

Fine, says astrology. The signs are only the frame. The important thing is the exact position of the planets. All right. Let us calculate a horoscope. Give an astrologer your date of birth, your exact time of birth, and the place, and they can calculate your birth chart. Where the Sun, the Moon and the planets stood is worked out astronomically. That is the easy part. A computer can tell you where every planet was that morning, to a fraction of a second of arc. But then comes the interesting bit. The astrologer interprets them. Mars means one thing. Venus means another. Saturn means something else.

Then you add houses. And aspects. And the ascendant. And suddenly a list of coordinates has become a personality profile. The planets are real. The calculations are real. The interpretation is something else. It is a system of symbolic meanings. And here is the question. Why should those meanings match a human personality? What connects the position of Saturn, more than a billion kilometres away, with your temperament?

4:55Gravity carries no message

There is no known mechanism. And gravity does not help. Jupiter does pull on a newborn, of course. But gravity is just gravity. It carries no message saying this child will be optimistic and that one will be ambitious. And even if it somehow did, it still would not explain why astrologers give each planet a completely different psychological meaning.

5:21Why the exact moment of birth?

That is a rather serious problem for a system that claims to tell you who you are. And there is another wonderfully arbitrary part. Why the exact moment of birth? Imagine two babies. One is born a minute before midnight. The other a minute after. Two minutes apart. And suddenly the planets have moved just enough to give them different horoscopes. But what about the moment of conception? What about the first heartbeat? Or the moment the doctor actually wrote the time down? Which one is cosmically important? Nature does not appear to contain a magical switch labelled: "Personality. Calculate now."

6:04Your horoscope is always accurate

And yet astrology treats that one moment as enormously significant. Fine, you might say. But my horoscope is always accurate. All right. Let us try something. Here is a horoscope. You are generally a thoughtful person, but you sometimes doubt yourself. You value close relationships, although you also need time alone. You can be very determined when something matters to you. And at times you are rather too critical of yourself. How accurate is that? Pretty good? Maybe surprisingly good? Here is the problem. That description was not written for you. It could have been written for almost anyone.

And that is not an accident. It is called the Barnum effect. Or the Forer effect, after the psychologist who tested it in nineteen forty-eight. He handed thirty-nine students what he told them were personal personality profiles. He had assembled the text out of a newsstand astrology book. Every student got exactly the same one. Then he asked them to rate it, from zero to five, for how well it described them. The average was four point two six. Only then did he tell them the truth. And many of them still said: "But that is me." So are horoscopes just random? Not exactly. This is where we have to be fair.

7:29Being fair to astrologers

A professional astrologer is not sitting there pressing a button marked "random horoscope". The positions really are calculated. The chart really is built according to an elaborate set of rules. But those rules rest on one assumption. That the positions mean something about a person. And that is the assumption that needs evidence. Because you could replace the whole system with a text generator. Pick a tendency. Add some ambiguity. Mention relationships, or a career, or personal growth. And you get something like this. "The coming weeks may bring some uncertainty in your relationships, but they could also open an opportunity for personal growth."

Did Mars cause that sentence? No. I just wrote it. And yet, does it sound like it could be your horoscope? This is where the argument stops being philosophical. Because we can actually test it.

8:29Putting it to the test

If astrology works, then an astrologer should be able to match a birth chart to a person better than chance can. In nineteen eighty-five, a double-blind test of astrology was published in Nature. Astrologers helped design it, and they said in advance what would count as success. Their own figure was one in two. Each of them was handed a birth chart and three personality profiles, and asked which of the three belonged to that chart. Guessing alone would get one in three. The original analysis found nothing better than chance. Forty right out of a hundred and sixteen.

9:07One experiment is never the last word

But the statistics were later disputed. Add up the answers a different way and a faint signal appears, right on the edge of meaning anything. Which is why one experiment is never the last word. Including this one. So the question is not what a single study found, but whether astrologers beat chance when you keep testing them. More than forty such tests exist. Nearly seven hundred astrologers, over a thousand birth charts. Pooled together, the effect is indistinguishable from nothing. And there is a second measurement that needs nobody to be right. Give one chart to two astrologers and ask them what it says.

Across twenty-five studies, their agreement comes out at about one tenth, on a scale where one would be perfect. For a test you would actually use on a person, psychologists want eight tenths or better. Before you can ask whether astrology is true, it has to say the same thing twice. And that is the key distinction. It does not matter whether an astrologer can produce an explanation that sounds convincing. What matters is whether it predicts something before we know the answer. That is how you tell a real effect from a good story.

10:26Why we are so good at this

And here is where this gets much more interesting. Because people who believe in astrology are not stupid. Astrology appeals to something our brains are extremely good at. Finding patterns. Finding meaning. Remembering the hits. Forgetting the misses. And building a coherent story out of incomplete information. If your horoscope says you may meet a difficult situation this week, and something annoying happens on Thursday, you remember it. If nothing happens, you do not. And if it says you may go through a period of emotional uncertainty, that cannot be wrong. You can always find something.

11:09What we found

So let us go back to the beginning. You were told that your personality was written in the sky on the day you were born. But the signs are not the constellations. There are thirteen of those on the Sun's path, not twelve, and they are not equal pieces of sky. Two whole schools of astrology disagree by nearly a sign about which one is yours. No known force carries a personality from Saturn to a delivery room. And a description written for everybody can feel like it was written for you. So does that prove that every astrologer is a fraud? No. It proves something much simpler. The claims of astrology need evidence.

And when they are tested carefully, astrology does not behave like something that can predict a person.

11:59The fascinating part

And perhaps that is the most fascinating thing about astrology. Not that Mars controls your personality. It does not. Not that Saturn knows what will happen to your career. It does not. The fascinating part is that your brain can look at a handful of vague sentences and say: "That is me." We are pattern-recognition machines. We see faces in clouds. We see shapes in random noise. We connect unrelated events. And sometimes we see meaning in the planets. The stars are real. The planets are real. The mathematics is real. The question is whether the story we have built around them is.

So the next time your horoscope tells you that something important is about to happen, remember. The universe probably is not sending you a message. Your brain is.
