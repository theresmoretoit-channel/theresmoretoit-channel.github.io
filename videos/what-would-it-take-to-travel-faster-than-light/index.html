What Would It Take to Travel Faster Than Light?

10 min2026-10-17

Physics has two stranger ideas. In 1994 Miguel Alcubierre found a solution to Einstein's equations that looks a lot like the warp drive of science fiction: don't move through space, move space instead. And in 1988 Kip Thorne and Michael Morris asked what it would take to hold a wormhole open, a serious physics question that started with Carl Sagan's novel Contact. So what would we actually have to invent?

The full narration of the video.

0:00One kilogram at 99% of light speed

Take one kilogram. A bag of sugar. Now push it to ninety-nine per cent of the speed of light. The energy you'd need is about a hundred and thirty megatons. More than twice the largest bomb ever detonated. For one kilogram. A spacecraft weighs tonnes. Push a single tonne to that speed, and you'd use nearly a year of all the energy humanity consumes. And ninety-nine per cent isn't the limit. Go to ninety-nine point nine, and the bill is three and a half times higher. Every extra nine costs more than the last.

0:37The last three metres per second

This isn't a guess. At the Large Hadron Collider, protons are pushed so hard that they fall short of light by only about three metres per second. Roughly the speed of a jogger. And no amount of extra energy will close that last gap. The closer you get, the more it costs, without any limit at all. So a bigger engine won't do it. The equations of relativity don't give a ship a way past the speed of light. And we'd need one. The nearest star beyond the Sun is more than four light-years away, so even light takes four years to get there. Crossing our own galaxy takes light more than a hundred thousand years.

Our actual spacecraft are far slower, as we saw in an earlier video. Relativity does offer one consolation. At ninety-nine per cent of light speed, time on board runs slower. The crew would reach the nearest star in about seven months of their own time.

1:31Time runs slower on board

But back on Earth, more than four years would pass. Cross the galaxy that way, and by the time you arrived, more than a hundred thousand years would have passed back home. So here is the question. What would we have to invent to travel faster than light? The answer, it turns out, probably isn't an engine.

2:05Space itself can stretch

Let's start with something odd that the universe already does. Space itself can stretch. The universe is expanding, and galaxies more than about fourteen billion light-years away are moving away from us faster than light. We can still see some of them. Nothing is breaking the speed limit. Those galaxies aren't racing through space. The space between us is growing. So what if a ship didn't move through space, but let space move it?

2:36Alcubierre's warp bubble

In 1994, a young Mexican physicist, Miguel Alcubierre, was working on his doctorate in Wales. He found a solution to Einstein's equations of gravity that looked a lot like the warp drive of science fiction. In front of the ship, space contracts. Behind it, space expands. The ship sits in a calm bubble in the middle. Inside the bubble, the ship isn't moving fast at all. Nobody on board would feel a thing. But the bubble itself could cross the distance to a star faster than light. Instead of driving faster, you move the road.

But a solution to an equation is not a machine. It tells you what space would have to look like. It doesn't tell you how to make it look that way.

3:23Less energy than nothing

And to make it look that way, Alcubierre's bubble needs something very strange. Negative energy. Less energy than empty space. Less than nothing. Quantum physics does allow a little of it. Put two metal plates a tiny fraction of a millimetre apart, and they pull towards each other. Physicists explain this force by saying the gap between the plates holds slightly less energy than the empty space outside. It was predicted in 1948 and measured precisely in 1997. But the amounts are minuscule. Take plates a metre across, a thousandth of a millimetre apart. They pull on each other with the weight of about a tenth of a gram.

And the energy missing from the gap is equal to the mass of about three protons. Quantum theory also seems to limit it. The more negative the energy, the smaller the region it can fill, and the shorter it can last.

4:23How much the bubble needs

And Alcubierre's bubble needs a lot. In 1997, two physicists worked out the minimum for his original design, with a bubble two hundred metres wide, moving at the speed of light. Under their assumptions, the negative energy came out at about ten billion times the mass of the entire visible universe.

Later designs brought that number down enormously. A few even avoid negative energy altogether. But so far, none of those has been convincingly shown to go faster than light. And there's another catch. The front edge of the bubble is out of reach for anyone inside. So the crew couldn't switch the bubble on, or steer it. The road would have to be laid before the trip ever began.

So maybe we shouldn't move the road. Maybe we should skip it.

5:19Sagan, Thorne and a novel

In 1985, Carl Sagan was finishing a novel called Contact. His heroine had to cross twenty-five light-years to the star Vega, and come back. In his draft, she flew into a black hole and came out near Vega. Sagan asked a friend, the physicist Kip Thorne, to check the physics. Thorne's answer was that a black hole wouldn't work. Anyone who fell in would never come out. But there was another shape in Einstein's equations.

5:49Folding the paper: wormholes

Imagine two points on a sheet of paper. The shortest way between them is a straight line. Now fold the paper, so the two points touch. Nothing moved faster. The geometry changed. That's a wormhole. A tunnel through spacetime, connecting two distant places. Wormholes appear in some solutions to Einstein's equations. But the simplest ones collapse faster than anything could get through.

6:18Holding a wormhole open

So Thorne and his student, Michael Morris, asked a different question. Forget whether wormholes exist. What would it take to keep one open, wide enough for a person? Their paper came out in 1988. A serious physics paper, which started with a question about a novel. The novel later became a film, with Jodie Foster. And the answer looked familiar. The throat of the tunnel would have to be held open by exotic matter. Negative energy again, like the gap between those metal plates. But not three protons' worth. In the simplest models, a throat just two metres wide needs negative mass roughly comparable to Jupiter.

Cleverer shapes need far less. But every version anyone has found still needs some. In the novel, Sagan got around that. The tunnels were built by an ancient civilisation, long since gone. We don't have that option. And no traversable wormhole has ever been observed. We don't know if one could exist, let alone how to build one.

7:28Three things we'd have to invent

So, what would we actually have to invent? Three things, at least. First, a way to reshape spacetime itself. Not to move through it faster, but to bend it, on purpose and on demand. Second, the energy to do it. For the original warp drive and for wormholes, that means energy of a kind we can barely make in a laboratory, in amounts that, in some estimates, dwarf everything in the universe. And third, a reason to think it's allowed. Relativity has passed every test we've ever given it. The clocks on GPS satellites run fast by about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day, exactly as relativity predicts. Without correcting for that, GPS navigation would quickly become unreliable.

That same theory is the one that sets the speed limit. And nothing in it hands us a way around it. So faster-than-light travel doesn't look like an engineering problem. It looks like a physics problem.

We have two ideas, the warp drive and the wormhole. Neither one is a blueprint. We don't know if either can be built.

8:40A problem deeper than energy

But there's a problem even deeper than energy, or exotic matter, or engineering. In that same year, 1988, Thorne and two colleagues showed that a wormhole built for travel could, in principle, be turned into a time machine. And if faster-than-light signals obey the usual rules of special relativity, they can be arranged to arrive before they were sent.

So what if the universe doesn't just make faster-than-light travel difficult?

What if it makes it incompatible with cause and effect itself?

That's where we're going next.
