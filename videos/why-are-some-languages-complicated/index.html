Why Are Some Languages So Complicated?

10 min2026-09-16

You don't need an ancient language to see how strange this gets. English says "go" and "went", and "went" never belonged to "go" at all. It was the past tense of a different verb, "wend". At some point English quietly took the past tense of one verb and handed it to the other. Nobody voted on it. Nobody wrote it down as a rule. It happened, and then it stayed.

This video is about why that is ordinary rather than odd. How one language becomes many without anyone deciding to start a new one. Why Old English losing its case endings did not make English simpler, but moved the work into word order and into a small army of auxiliary verbs. Why "how complex is this language" has no single number behind it. And why Proto-Indo-European — a language nobody has ever found a text in — still looks elaborate when linguists reconstruct it by comparing the languages that came out of it.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A simple life, a complicated language

Imagine someone alive five thousand years ago. No electricity. No books. No schools. By our standards, an extraordinarily simple life. So you might expect an extraordinarily simple language. Something like: I hunt. You hunt. Tomorrow we hunt. That is not what we find. Latin, Old English, Old Norse. All of them had grammatical machinery that modern English has mostly lost. And you don't need an ancient language to see how odd this gets. Modern English will do.

0:36Why isn't it "goed"?

The verb is "go". The past tense is "went". Why isn't it "goed"?

Because "went" never belonged to "go". It was the past tense of a different verb. "Wend". To turn, to make your way. You can still wend your way home. Two verbs. Two meanings. And at some point English quietly took the past tense of one of them and handed it to the other. "Go" lost its own past. "Wend" grew a tidy new one: "wended". Today several hundred million people simply learn it: go, went, gone. Nobody stops to think about the history in the middle of a sentence. Nobody voted on that. Nobody wrote it down as a rule. It happened, and then it stayed.

Which is this entire video, hiding inside one irregular verb.

1:31Language is not technology

Because the first mistake we make is treating language as technology. When we meet a complicated machine, we assume a simpler one came first. The earliest computers were crude. Later ones were not. Every version adds capability. So it's tempting to tell the same story about speech. Primitive sounds first. Grammar bolted on afterwards. But nobody designed English. Nobody designed Latin. There was no committee sitting around three thousand years ago, agreeing that the language needed more endings. Languages don't get released. They get used.

And every generation alters them slightly, without meaning to. Sounds shift. Words drift. Endings wear down. Nobody is in charge of any of it.

2:25How one language becomes many

Which is also how one language turns into many. Take a population that all speaks the same way, and split it. Half of them move somewhere else. At first, nothing happens. They still speak the same language. Then the small changes begin. A sound shifts on one side of the mountain and not the other. A word picks up a new meaning here and keeps the old one there. An ending survives in one group and quietly dies in the second. And because the two groups are no longer talking, none of it ever gets compared. After a few generations, you notice an accent.

After a few hundred years, you notice the grammar. After a thousand, you may need a translator. And at no point did anyone decide to start a new language.

3:18So why don't they wear down?

So if languages are always moving, why don't they simply wear down into something easy? Sometimes they do. English is the usual example. Old English had grammatical gender. Its nouns changed their endings depending on the job they were doing in the sentence. Almost all of that is gone. A modern English noun barely changes at all. Which really is simplification. In that corner of the grammar. But the information didn't evaporate. It moved.

3:52The dog and the man

"The dog bit the man." "The man bit the dog." Same words. Same endings. Entirely different afternoon. Old English had more freedom in word order, because the endings carried information about who did what to whom. Modern English has far less of it. Word order is holding that job now, and it never gets a day off. Dropping one mechanism and leaning harder on another is not the same as getting simpler.

4:22A small army of helper verbs

And English didn't only lose things. While the endings were wearing away, it was assembling something else: a small army of helper verbs. "I go." "I am going." "I have gone." "I had been going." "I am going to go." Modern English does that work with auxiliary verbs, where Old English leaned on endings. The endings left through one door, and the auxiliaries walked in through another.

4:49Complexity moves house

Complexity rarely disappears. It moves house.

5:03There is no dial

So how would you even decide that one language is simpler than another? There is no dial somewhere reading: language complexity, seventy-three per cent. Look at the range humans have produced. English. Arabic. Mandarin. Hindi. Japanese. Swahili. A language can have punishing verb endings and a word order you could set your watch by. Another can have almost no endings and a syntax that forgives nothing. One may have a plain tense system and an elaborate way of marking how you know what you are claiming. And some will pack into a single word what another language would spread across half a sentence.

Whatever you measure, you are measuring one room of a very large house.

5:49Back to our ancient human

Which brings us back to our ancient human. Latin nouns had a set of case endings. Six of them. Or five, or seven, depending on which grammar book you happen to open. And when linguists compare the descendants and work backwards, the ancestor they arrive at looks elaborate too.

6:09A reconstruction, not a record

Proto-Indo-European. Nobody has ever found a text in it. It is a reconstruction, not a record. Assembled by comparing the languages that came out of it.

So why would anyone that long ago need grammar like that?

6:27A society without technology

Because a society without technology is not a society without complications. Imagine having to say: "The man you saw by the river yesterday took my spear." Who. Which man. When. Where. Whose spear.

6:45Did you see it, or were you told?

And a question that some languages oblige you to answer every time: did you see it happen, or did somebody tell you? Get that wrong in some languages and you have not been impolite. You have been ungrammatical. Language is not mainly a tool for describing technology. It is a tool for handling other people. And people were never simple. We make promises. We tell stories. We negotiate. We remember. We gossip. We lie. No smartphone required.

7:19Less like engineering

All of which makes language look much less like engineering, and much more like evolution. Evolution doesn't design anything. It edits whatever is already there. It keeps the compromises. It carries the leftovers. It is stuck with decisions made by creatures that died long ago. Languages behave the same way. They inherit structures, bend them, mislay a few, and keep the strange ones entirely by accident. "Went" is a leftover. Silent letters are leftovers. Irregular verbs are a museum that every child has to memorise.

And that leads to the part people find hardest to accept.

8:04There is no direction

There is no direction.

No rule says a language ought to get simpler. No rule says it ought to get richer.

8:15The Slavic example

The Slavic languages show it neatly. Most of them kept a rich case system. Bulgarian lost the old one on its nouns almost completely, and grew something else instead: a definite article stuck on the end of the word. Same ancestor. Opposite directions. Neither one is the upgrade.

And the same thing happened right across the world. People separate. Languages drift. Sometimes they meet again and start borrowing from each other.

8:47Never a ladder

It was never a ladder. It is a tree, and it is still growing.

Latin was not complicated because the Romans had discovered a superior way to communicate. And modern English is not simpler because we are cleverer. They are different outcomes of different histories.

9:08Why did we expect a simple one?

So perhaps the question was wrong from the start. Instead of asking why some languages are so complicated, we could ask why we ever expected a simple one.

Language was never designed. No plan. No final version. No committee tidying up the mess. It only has to work well enough for people to understand each other. Everything after that is history. Language doesn't evolve towards perfection. It evolves towards whatever people keep using.
