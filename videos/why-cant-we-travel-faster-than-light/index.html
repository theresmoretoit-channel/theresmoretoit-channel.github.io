Why Can't We Travel Faster Than Light?

11 min2026-10-17

In 1964 William Bertozzi gave electrons thirty times more energy, and they still wouldn't pass the speed of light. In 2011 the OPERA experiment seemed to catch neutrinos doing exactly that. And in 1988 Kip Thorne and two colleagues showed how a wormhole could be turned into a time machine. So why do physicists think the universe might not allow any of it?

The full narration of the video.

0:00A reply before the message

Imagine you could send a message faster than light. You send it to a friend on a distant spaceship. They read it and send a reply, just as fast. And the reply reaches you before you've sent the message. That's not a trick of the clocks, and it's not science fiction. It's what the equations of relativity say could happen. In our last video, we asked what it would take to travel faster than light. Warp drives. Wormholes. Exotic matter. This time, the harder question. Why do physicists think the universe might not allow it at all?

0:40Measuring the speed limit

First, the speed limit itself. Because it isn't a rule of thumb. It has been measured. In 1964, at MIT, William Bertozzi fired electrons down a tube and timed them over about eight metres. Then he gave them more energy. And more. In the end, thirty times as much. At the lowest energy, the electrons moved at about eighty-seven per cent of the speed of light. With thirty times the energy? So close to the speed of light that his timing couldn't tell the difference. Newton's physics had a different answer. By his rules, the fastest of them should have been moving several times faster than light.

And the energy hadn't gone missing. He caught the electrons in an aluminium disc and measured the heat. It was all there. The extra energy went in. The electrons crept closer to light speed, and stopped short. That's the wall. But a wall isn't a reason. Why should there be a limit at all?

1:47Why should there be a limit?

The answer hides in something we never think about: the word "now". Picture a long train racing past a station. Lightning strikes both ends of it. A woman on the platform stands halfway between the strikes. Both flashes reach her together. For her, the strikes were simultaneous. A passenger sits in the middle of the train. But she's moving towards the front flash and away from the back one. So the front flash reaches her first. And because light travels at the same speed for everyone, the passenger concludes, correctly, that the front was struck first.

Neither of them is wrong. This was Einstein's own example. Whether two distant events happen at the same time depends on how you're moving.

2:40Can an effect come before its cause?

That sounds dangerous. Could someone see an effect before its cause? No. And this is the key. The order can only flip for events so far apart that not even light could get from one to the other in time. If one event can affect the other, everyone agrees which came first. In relativity, the light-speed limit is what keeps cause before effect.

3:06Alice, Bob and a faster message

Now let's break it. Alice is on Earth. Bob is on a spaceship, one light-year away, flying further out at ninety per cent of the speed of light. Alice sends him a message that travels at twice the speed of light. For Alice, nothing strange happens. The message leaves, and almost eleven months later, it reaches Bob. But the two events are so far apart that light couldn't connect them. So Bob and Alice don't have to agree on their order. And in Bob's frame, the message arrives before Alice sent it. That's unsettling. But it isn't a paradox yet.

Now Bob replies. Same kind of signal, twice the speed of light, measured from his ship. Work it through, and his reply reaches Earth about five months before Alice sent her message. Alice receives an answer to a question she hasn't asked yet.

4:03The tachyonic antitelephone

Einstein himself noticed this in 1907. In 1970, three physicists gave it a name: the tachyonic antitelephone. And here's the problem. Suppose the reply says: "Don't send the message." If Alice obeys, there's nothing for Bob to answer. So where did the reply come from? An effect has prevented its own cause.

Combine faster-than-light signals with the usual rules of relativity, and you can construct a message that arrives before it was sent. That is a serious problem for cause and effect. Not proof that every imaginable shortcut through spacetime must fail. And it doesn't matter what the signal is made of. Radio, particles, anything. All that counts is that it's faster than light, and that the usual rules hold.

4:58Could relativity be wrong?

Now, physics never proves that something is impossible. A new theory could change the rules. But it would have to explain why relativity has passed every test we've given it. And the tests are very good. Modern experiments have searched for any difference in the speed of light between different directions. Down to about one part in a billion billion, they found none.

5:21OPERA's faster-than-light neutrinos

And once, it looked as though something had broken through. In 2011, the OPERA experiment sent neutrinos from CERN, near Geneva, to a lab under a mountain in Italy, seven hundred and thirty kilometres away. They arrived about sixty billionths of a second early. Faster than light. And it wasn't a fluke of statistics. The result was far too strong for that. Whatever was wrong was wrong every single time. The team didn't claim a revolution. They published the result and asked others to find the mistake. Within months, the investigation found problems in the timing system, including a faulty fibre-optic connection.

Once the timing was corrected and the measurement repeated, the faster-than-light result disappeared. The lesson isn't that relativity can never be wrong. It's that an extraordinary result has to survive checking. This one didn't.

6:23Turning a wormhole into a time machine

So signals can't simply outrun light. But in our last video, we met a different idea. A wormhole. A shortcut between two places, where nothing has to outrun light, because the path is shorter. And in 1988, Kip Thorne and two colleagues showed how a wormhole could be turned into a time machine. It takes one trick we've already seen. A clock that travels close to light speed runs slow compared with one that stays at home. Start with a wormhole whose two mouths sit side by side. Through the tunnel, their clocks agree. Now take one mouth on a long trip, at ninety-nine per cent of the speed of light, and bring it back.

On Earth, ten years have passed. For the travelling mouth, less than a year and a half. But through the tunnel, the clocks at the two mouths still agree. So step into the mouth that travelled, and you come out of the other one more than eight years earlier.

A time shift between the mouths isn't yet a time machine. That happens when the shift is longer than light needs to get from one mouth to the other the ordinary way. Then you can step out in the past, walk back to the first mouth, and arrive before you left. None of this tells us how to build such a wormhole, or keep it open. It's a solution on paper. But it shows that the paradox isn't only about signals. Any shortcut through spacetime may lead to the same place.

7:56Does the universe forbid it?

So does the universe forbid it? There are two famous answers. In 1992, Stephen Hawking proposed that the laws of physics themselves prevent time machines from forming. His idea was that as a wormhole came close to becoming a time machine, the restless energy of empty space would build up inside it without limit, and stop the time machine from forming. He called it the chronology protection conjecture. And he pointed out, half jokingly, that we have not been invaded by hordes of tourists from the future. It was a joke, not a proof. In 2009, he threw a party for time travellers, and sent out the invitations only afterwards.

Nobody came.

8:44Novikov and the billiard ball

The Russian physicist Igor Novikov answered differently. Maybe the past can be visited, but it can't be changed. The physicist Joseph Polchinski challenged the idea with a billiard ball. Send it into a wormhole, so that it comes out in the past and knocks its younger self off course. Then it never enters the wormhole. A paradox. But when Thorne and two colleagues worked through the equations, they found that the collision could also happen differently. A glancing blow. The younger ball is nudged, not stopped, and still enters the wormhole.

For every starting point they tried, they found at least one self-consistent history. For some, there was more than one.

9:30Neither answer is proven

Neither answer is proven. Hawking's is a conjecture. Novikov's is a principle, not an observation. So for now, the speed of light isn't just a limit on our engines. It may be what keeps causes before their effects. Either the universe never lets a time machine form… or it allows only histories that never contradict themselves. We don't yet know which, if either.
