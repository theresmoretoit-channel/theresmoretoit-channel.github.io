Inside Your Computer

How Did ARM Take Over Computing?

10 min2026-10-08

The usual explanation is that ARM is simple and efficient, and efficient wins. But a modern ARM core is anything but simple, and since the mid-1990s Intel's chips have looked surprisingly similar inside. So how did a chip from a British home-computer company end up everywhere?

The full narration of the video.

0:00The chip that ran without power

In 1985, engineers in Britain switched on a brand-new processor for the very first time. It worked. Which was already a small miracle. But then they noticed something strange. The chip's power supply wasn't connected. And yet it was running. It was getting by on tiny currents leaking in through its input pins, from the signals of the chips around it. When they measured it properly, the processor needed about a tenth of a watt. Nobody had aimed for that. The engineers just wanted a chip that was cheap to make. That chip was the first ARM processor.

Forty years later, its descendants are in almost every smartphone. In Apple's computers. In some of the largest data centres in the world. Hundreds of billions of chips. So how did a processor from a British home-computer company take over computing? The answer isn't simply that it used very little power. That part was almost an accident.

1:10Acorn, 1983

To understand what really happened, we have to go back to 1983. The company was Acorn Computers. Its BBC Micro was in classrooms all over Britain. The BBC Micro ran on an older, eight-bit chip, the 6502. For its next machine, Acorn needed something much faster. So the team tested the new processors on the market. None of them did what Acorn wanted. They were complicated, and none of them really fit what Acorn wanted to build. Then two Acorn engineers, Sophie Wilson and Steve Furber, went to Arizona, to a firm started by one of the 6502's original designers.

They expected a big chip company. Instead, they found a handful of people working out of a suburban house. And they came home with a simple conclusion. If they can design a processor… so can we.

2:11The RISC idea

At the time, processors kept gaining more and more elaborate instructions. A single instruction could fetch a number from memory, do arithmetic with it, and write the result back. Convenient for the programmer. But every instruction like that makes the chip harder to build. It needs more logic just to work out what each one means. More transistors. More design time. Researchers at Berkeley and Stanford had asked a radical question. What if a processor only had simple instructions, and the compiler did the rest? They called it RISC. Reduced Instruction Set Computer.

Sophie Wilson designed ARM's instructions along those lines. Arithmetic only works on the chip's own registers, small, fast slots inside the processor. Memory gets its own instructions. So adding a number from memory becomes three steps. Load it. Add it. Store it. It's called a load-store architecture. More steps for the program. But each step is regular, predictable, and easy to decode. And ARM had plenty of registers to work with. Sixteen, each thirty-two bits wide. The 6502 had three small ones.

3:34The ARM1

The result was the ARM1. It had about 25,000 transistors. Intel's 286, launched a few years earlier, had about 134,000. Fewer transistors meant a smaller chip. A cheaper chip. And, almost as a side effect, a chip that barely used any power. That's where the tenth of a watt came from. Acorn wanted a cheap plastic package, which meant staying under one watt. So they designed carefully… and landed ten times below.

4:09The Archimedes

In 1987, a faster version of the ARM went into Acorn's new computer, the Archimedes. It was one of the fastest personal computers of its day. And it barely mattered. The world was buying IBM-compatible PCs with Intel processors. That's where the software was. That's what businesses bought. The Archimedes stayed mostly in British schools. And Acorn itself was struggling. It had already needed rescuing by Olivetti, back in 1985.

4:46Apple and a new company

Then help came from Apple. It was building a handheld computer called the Newton. It needed a processor that was fast, cheap, and could run on batteries. The ARM fitted perfectly. But Apple didn't want to depend on a chip owned by a rival computer maker. So in 1990, the ARM team was spun out of Acorn into a company of its own. Acorn, Apple and the chip maker VLSI owned it together. Twelve engineers. An old barn near Cambridge.

5:17Selling the design

And a business model that turned out to be the real secret. Intel designed its processors, made them in its own factories, and sold them. ARM would make nothing at all. It would sell the design. Any company could license it, build the ARM core into its own chip, and pay a small fee on every chip it sold. That mattered, because a chip doesn't have to be just a processor. Around the ARM core you can put a radio, a graphics unit, a camera processor. One chip, designed by whoever needs it, with ARM at the centre. ARM didn't have to beat Intel in personal computers. It could end up inside anything.

6:00Phones

And in the 1990s, anything increasingly meant mobile phones. A desktop computer can use a hundred watts and a big fan. A phone runs on a battery in your pocket. By the end of the decade, ARM cores were inside some of the best-selling phones, from Nokia and others. Then came the iPod. Then the smartphone. Billions of people suddenly carried a computer that had to last all day on a battery. And the processor that was built because it was cheap turned out to be exactly what that world needed.

6:35Is simple the answer?

So that's our answer, right? ARM is simple, simple means efficient, and efficient wins. Not quite.

Look inside a modern high-end ARM core. Huge caches. Several instructions running at once. Branch prediction, guessing which way the program will go before it gets there. Out-of-order execution, running instructions in whatever order is fastest. Billions of transistors on the chip. Nothing about it is simple. So where did the simplicity go?

7:13Two different things

The trick is that a processor is really two different things. The instruction set is the language the software speaks. Which instructions exist, and what they mean. The microarchitecture is the machine that actually carries them out. The language can stay simple, while the machine underneath becomes enormously complicated.

7:36What happened to x86

And the same thing happened on the other side. Intel's x86 instructions are famously complicated. But since the mid-1990s, Intel and AMD processors break them down inside into small, simple operations. Then they run those on an engine much like the one inside a modern ARM core. Underneath the labels, the two have grown surprisingly alike. ARM's cleaner instructions still help. They're easier to decode, which can save a little power. But they're not a magic advantage that x86 could never match. So ARM didn't take over because it was simple. And it wasn't designed for low power.

It won because three things lined up. An efficient design. A business model that let anyone build with it. And a world that suddenly needed billions of battery-powered computers.

8:34Back to Apple

And then, in 2020, the story came back to Apple. Apple had sold its stake in ARM years earlier, when it badly needed the money. Now it put its own ARM-based chips into the Mac. The same kind of cores as the iPhone, scaled up for a computer. Fast cores, efficient cores, graphics and much more, in a single chip. They were faster than the Intel chips they replaced, and the laptops ran far longer on a charge. ARM was no longer just the phone processor.

9:07Data centres

And ARM didn't stop there. It moved into data centres too, where every watt costs money. Amazon now designs its own ARM server chips.

The first ARM chip had about 25,000 transistors. Today's have billions. Almost everything about how they work inside has changed.

9:30What they really built

A small team wanted a processor they could actually build. They didn't build it for phones, for Apple, or to beat Intel. And in the end, the most important thing they made wasn't the chip. It was a simple foundation that everyone else could build on.
