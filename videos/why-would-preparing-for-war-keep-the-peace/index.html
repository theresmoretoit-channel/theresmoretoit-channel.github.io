Why Would Preparing for War Keep the Peace?

8 min2026-10-11

But if the goal is never to fight, why build tanks, missiles and warships? Can weapons actually keep the peace, or do they just make everyone more afraid of each other? From a Roman writer's complaint about a neglected army to the Dreadnought race, seventy thousand nuclear warheads, the Falklands and one night in 1983 when a Soviet warning system reported incoming missiles.

Military spending: SIPRI (April 2026). Nuclear warheads: Federation of American Scientists. Falklands: the Franks Report (1983).

The full narration of the video.

0:00Want peace? Prepare for war

Do you want peace? Then prepare for war. It sounds almost absurd. If the goal is never to fight, why build tanks, missiles and warships? And yet, in 2025, the world spent nearly two point nine trillion dollars on the military. The most ever recorded. Most governments that spend it say the same thing. It's not for starting a war. It's for preventing one. So is that a contradiction? Or is preparing for war one of the reasons war sometimes doesn't happen?

0:35Deterrence in its simplest form

Start with the simplest version. Two countries. One is thinking about attacking the other. If the target is weak, the attack looks cheap. A few weeks. A quick victory. Done. Now change one thing. The target can fight back, and make the war long, bloody, expensive. The attacker's question changes. It's no longer "can we win?" It's "is winning worth the price?" That's deterrence. You don't have to defeat your enemy. You have to convince them that attacking you is a terrible deal.

1:11An old Roman idea

The idea is old. Its famous Latin version is si vis pacem, para bellum. It's usually credited to Vegetius, a Roman who wrote about warfare about sixteen hundred years ago. He never wrote those exact words. What he wrote was longer: let him who desires peace prepare for war. It wasn't a boast, either. His book was a complaint that Rome had let its army decay. Soldiers, he wrote, had stopped drilling, and even stopped wearing their helmets and body armour.

1:52The security dilemma

Weakness invites attack. That part of the idea holds up well. There's a catch, though. Your enemy can see your preparations too. Say Country A is worried about Country B, so it builds more tanks. From the inside, that's pure defence. Country B can't see inside A's head. It only sees the tanks. So it builds tanks of its own. Now A is worried again. More tanks. Then B. Then A. Neither side wants a war. Both think they're only defending. Both end up more armed, and less safe. In 1950, the political scientist John Herz gave this a name: the security dilemma.

2:37The Dreadnought race

It isn't just theory. In 1906, Britain launched HMS Dreadnought, a battleship so advanced it made every other one outdated. Including Britain's own. Its huge lead in older ships suddenly counted for much less. Germany, already building a fleet, raced to match the new design. Each side said it was only for defence. The race didn't cause the First World War on its own. It certainly didn't prevent it.

3:09Nuclear weapons and MAD

Then came nuclear weapons, and the logic got even stranger. At the peak, in 1986, the world had around seventy thousand nuclear warheads. Almost all of them belonged to just two countries: the United States and the Soviet Union. Far more than either side needed to devastate the other. Why would that make anyone safer? Because even after a first strike, the other side could still strike back. Attacking would be suicide. It became known as mutually assured destruction. Or, fittingly, MAD. I won't destroy you, because you can destroy me. You won't destroy me, for the same reason.

The weapons weren't useful because they could be used. They were useful because using them would be a catastrophe.

3:59When it works, nothing happens

Which gives military power a strange property. When it works, nothing happens. No battle. No victory. No headline. Just… nothing. Since 1949, no outside power has invaded the territory the NATO treaty protects. Is that deterrence? Or would nobody have tried anyway? We can't know. History doesn't let us run it twice. We only ever see the wars that happened.

4:28When deterrence fails

Deterrence does fail, though. It doesn't depend on weapons alone. It depends on what the other side believes. In 1981, Britain announced it would retire its only regular warship in the South Atlantic. It was meant to save money. In Argentina, it was read as one more sign that Britain was losing interest. The ship itself couldn't have stopped an invasion. What mattered was what withdrawing it seemed to say. In 1982, Argentina invaded. Britain had a powerful navy. Argentina's own inquiry later found that the junta had thought a British military response almost impossible.

Deterrence needs two things. The power to strike back, and an opponent convinced you will. Both sides are judging that with incomplete information. Sometimes, in a matter of minutes.

5:26September 1983

In September 1983, a Soviet early-warning system reported a missile launched from the United States. Then four more. The officer on duty, Stanislav Petrov, had to judge whether the warning was real. He reported it as a false alarm. He was right. A satellite had mistaken sunlight off high clouds for rocket engines. A machine built to remove all doubt had got it wrong. It took a human's doubt to say so.

5:59A narrow line

That leaves countries walking a narrow line. Strong enough that attacking them looks irrational. Not so threatening that a neighbour decides to strike first. From the outside, defensive and offensive preparations can look almost exactly the same.

6:18The hotline

After the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962, the two superpowers built something new. Not a weapon. A direct line between Washington and Moscow. During the crisis, one Soviet letter took nearly twelve hours to reach the White House. In 1963, the hotline went live. It wasn't the famous red telephone. It was a teleprinter. It meant the two sides could talk before anyone had to shoot.

6:49So, does it keep the peace?

So, does preparing for war keep the peace? Sometimes, yes. A country that can defend itself is harder to bully, and harder to invade. Weapons don't create peace on their own. They also create fear, arms races, and room for catastrophic mistakes. Deterrence only works when the other side believes the threat, understands it, and doesn't panic. The best deterrent may be a weapon that is never fired. And the proof that it worked may be nothing happening at all.

Do you want peace? Then prepare for war. But perhaps… prepare even harder for peace.
