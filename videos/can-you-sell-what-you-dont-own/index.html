Short Selling: Selling Before Buying

10 min2026-09-10

Short selling looks backwards because it is: you sell first and buy later. But follow that idea far enough and it stops being about oil at all. Most futures contracts never deliver a single barrel. Some refer to oil that hasn't been produced yet. And in April 2020 the price of a major oil contract briefly fell below zero, because holding it meant being obliged to take delivery of something you had nowhere to put.

From barrels to contracts, from contracts to risk, and finally to markets that trade nothing but the probability of an event. This is how a market can work without ever needing the thing it is named after.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The offer

Imagine I offer to sell you a barrel of oil. There's just one problem. I don't own the oil. In fact, I don't even want the oil. I just think its price is going down. So I sell it to you today… and I plan to buy it back later, hopefully for less. If that sounds backwards, that's because it is. Welcome to short selling.

0:25Sell first, buy later

Normally, making money is pretty intuitive. You buy something for ten dollars. You sell it for twenty. Congratulations. You made ten dollars. Short selling flips the whole thing upside down. You sell first. And only later do you buy. The idea is simple. Suppose oil is trading at eighty dollars a barrel. I think the price is going to fall. So I sell an oil contract for eighty dollars. Then the price falls to sixty. I buy it back for sixty. And I keep the twenty-dollar difference. At least in theory.

1:06The loss with no ceiling

Because there's a rather important detail. What happens if I'm wrong? If oil goes from eighty to one hundred dollars, I'm in trouble. I sold something for eighty… and now I have to buy it back for one hundred. I lose twenty dollars. And if oil goes to two hundred? I lose one hundred and twenty. If it goes to five hundred? Well… you get the idea. When you buy something normally, the most you can lose is what you paid for it. With a short position, there is theoretically no upper limit to how much you can lose. Because there is no upper limit to how high the price can go. And this creates a very strange asymmetry.

If I buy oil for eighty dollars and it falls to zero, I lose eighty. But if I short oil at eighty and it goes to one hundred and sixty… I've lost eighty. If it goes to eight hundred… I've lost seven hundred and twenty. The upside is limited. The downside isn't. So why would anyone do this?

2:14Why anyone would do this

Because markets aren't just about making money. Sometimes you want to get rid of risk. Imagine you're an airline. Fuel is one of your biggest costs. And you're worried that oil prices might suddenly rise. So you can buy futures contracts that effectively lock in a price for oil. If oil goes up, your fuel becomes more expensive… but your futures position makes money. You've exchanged an uncertain future price for a more predictable one. Someone else takes the other side. Maybe they're a speculator who thinks oil will fall.

Maybe they're another company with the opposite risk. Maybe they're a market maker. They don't have to believe the exact same thing you do. They just have to be willing to take the other side of the contract. And this is where things start getting weird.

3:10We are not trading oil

Because when we say we're trading oil… we often aren't trading oil. We're trading a contract. A futures contract can say, essentially. At a certain date, under certain conditions, one party will deliver a certain amount of oil… and another party will accept it. But here's the important part. Most futures contracts never result in somebody receiving a truck full of oil. The traders simply exit their positions before the contract expires. So I can sell a futures contract for oil… and later buy the same contract back.

No barrel needs to travel anywhere. The gain or loss is settled financially. And suddenly our original question gets even stranger.

3:58Does the oil have to exist?

Does the oil actually have to exist? Not necessarily. A futures contract can refer to oil that will be produced in the future. The oil doesn't have to be sitting in a warehouse with your name on it. And if everyone closes their positions before delivery… the physical oil may never enter the transaction at all. What we're really trading is a promise. A standardised promise that has a price. And once you realise that… you start seeing these contracts everywhere.

4:32The anchor in the real world

But surely there has to be some connection to the real world. And there is. There are companies that actually produce oil. There are refineries that actually consume it. There are pipelines, ships, storage tanks and millions of barrels of physical crude moving around the planet. The financial market doesn't float completely free of reality. If the futures price becomes wildly different from the physical price, traders can exploit the difference. They can buy the cheap thing and sell the expensive thing. That's called arbitrage.

So physical reality still acts as an anchor. Usually.

5:16April 2020: below zero

But what happens when the physical world suddenly can't keep up with the financial one? Well… we actually got to see that happen. In April twenty twenty, during the COVID nineteen pandemic, oil demand collapsed. People stopped driving. Airlines stopped flying. Factories shut down. But oil production couldn't simply stop overnight. And suddenly there was a problem. There was too much oil… and not enough storage space. The oil wasn't imaginary. It was very real. There was just nowhere to put it. And something absolutely bizarre happened.

The price of a major oil futures contract briefly fell below zero. Yes. Below zero.

Traders were effectively willing to pay someone else to take the contract. Why? Because if you were still holding the contract when it reached physical delivery… you could end up with an obligation to take delivery of oil. And if you had nowhere to put it… that was a very expensive problem. For a moment, the question wasn't. How much will you pay me for my oil? It was. How much will I pay you to take this problem away from me?

6:37Trading obligations

And this is where finance starts feeling less like buying and selling things… and more like trading obligations. But we can go even further. Because if you can trade a contract on oil… you don't necessarily need oil. So what else can we put into a contract? Interest rates. Currencies. Stock indexes. Inflation. Credit risk. Even weather. And eventually… something even more abstract. The probability of an event happening.

7:10A market in probabilities

Imagine a market asking. Will candidate X win the election? A contract might pay one dollar if the answer is yes… and zero dollars if the answer is no. If that contract trades for seventy cents, the market is roughly saying. We think there's about a seventy percent chance. You can buy it if you think the real probability is higher. You can sell it if you think it's lower. Now you're not trading oil. You're not trading gold. You're not even trading a company. You're trading a belief about the future. Platforms such as Polymarket have made this kind of prediction market highly visible. And suddenly the progression looks almost ridiculous.

First, we traded things. Then we traded contracts for things. Then we traded contracts for things that didn't exist yet. Then we traded risk. And now… we can trade probabilities.

8:12The answer

So let's go back to our original question. How can you sell something you don't have? The answer is. You don't necessarily have to be selling the thing. You're selling a contract. A contract that represents an obligation, a future transaction, a risk… or sometimes just a bet on what will happen. And that is one of the strangest things about modern finance. A financial market doesn't necessarily need a physical object. It needs rules. It needs counterparties. It needs a way to settle the contract. And most importantly… it needs people who believe the contract is worth something.

8:55So what are we trading?

Which leads to a rather uncomfortable question. When you strip away the barrels of oil, the warehouses, the stock certificates and the banknotes… what exactly are we trading?

Maybe the answer is. Expectations. Risk. Promises. And beliefs about the future. We built an enormous global machine for trading those things. And somehow… it works. Most of the time. Until everyone wants the same thing at the same time. And then you might suddenly discover that the barrel of oil you were trading… was never really the interesting part.
