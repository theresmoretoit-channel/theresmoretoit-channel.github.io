Why Is Getting to Orbit So Hard?

9 min2026-10-29

This video works through it step by step: why adding fuel to a rocket stops helping at some point, the equation every rocket still has to obey, how fast you actually need to go to stay in orbit, why rockets throw parts of themselves away on the way up, and why we can't simply build a better engine.

Along the way: a schoolteacher from Kaluga, a British mathematician working on military rockets in 1813, a Falcon 9 that delivers about four percent of its launch mass, an ion engine whose push is about the weight of a sheet of paper resting on your hand, and what the same calculation says about a planet ten times heavier than Earth, and about Mars.

The full narration of the video.

0:00One part in six hundred

In July 1969, the rocket that carried the crew of the first Moon landing weighed about two thousand nine hundred tonnes on the launch pad. Eight days later, what came back to Earth was a capsule with three people inside. It weighed just under five tonnes. About one part in six hundred of that rocket came home. So where did the rest go? Most of it was propellant: fuel, and the oxygen to burn it. More than two thousand tonnes of it burnt in under three minutes. And here's the strange part. Much of it wasn't there to lift the spacecraft. It was there to lift more propellant. To keep things simple, we'll call all of it fuel from here on.

0:46Fuel that has to carry fuel

Imagine you want a rocket to go faster. The obvious answer is more fuel. But fuel has mass. Before the rocket can burn it, it has to carry it, and speed it up along with everything else. So the extra fuel needs fuel of its own. And that fuel needs fuel too. At some point, adding fuel stops solving the problem… and starts becoming the problem.

1:13A century-old equation

This problem has an equation, and it's more than a century old. In 1903, a schoolteacher from the Russian town of Kaluga, Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, published the formula that every rocket still has to obey. He wasn't the first to do the maths. In 1813, a British mathematician, William Moore, had worked out how a rocket speeds up as it loses mass, for military rockets. Tsiolkovsky used it to work out how we might reach space.

1:44Reading the equation

Here it is. On the left is delta-v: the change in speed the rocket can achieve. On the right, first, the exhaust speed. A rocket has nothing to push against. It moves by throwing mass backwards, and the faster it throws it, the more momentum each kilogram carries off, and the more the rocket gets back. Then the ratio of two masses: the rocket full of fuel at the start, and what's left when the fuel is gone. Engines, empty tanks, structure… and the payload you actually wanted to send. And wrapped around that ratio, a logarithm. That's the part that makes it so hard.

2:25More fuel, less speed

Take two rockets with the same engine. The first starts four times heavier than it will be with its tanks empty. The second, eight times heavier. Twice the mass ratio. You'd expect twice the speed. It gets about fifty percent more. Double it again, from eight to sixteen, and you get only about a third more. Every extra tonne of fuel buys less speed than the tonne before it. Turn that around, and it looks worse. Each extra kilometre per second doesn't add to the rocket's starting mass. It multiplies it.

3:04What it takes to reach orbit

So how much speed does orbit take? In low Earth orbit, a spacecraft travels at about seven point eight kilometres per second, sideways. On the way up, the rocket also fights gravity and the air. Altogether, it needs around nine point four kilometres per second. Now give it the best chemical engine, burning hydrogen and oxygen. And let's be generous: assume it performs as it would in a vacuum, with exhaust at four and a half kilometres per second. Put those numbers into the equation, and the rocket has to start about eight times heavier than it ends.

A hundred tonnes on the launch pad… and only about twelve tonnes allowed to reach orbit. And those twelve tonnes aren't payload. They're the engines, the empty tanks, the whole structure that held the fuel. Whatever's left after that is the satellite. In real rockets, that leftover is small. A Falcon 9 weighs about five hundred and fifty tonnes at launch. Its maximum payload to low orbit is around twenty-three tonnes. Up to about four percent.

4:14Throwing the rocket away

So rockets that reach orbit shed stages or other hardware on the way up. That's where the rest of the Saturn V went. Its first stage burned for less than three minutes, then fell away into the Atlantic. Five engines and enormous empty tanks… that the rest of the flight no longer had to carry. The second stage took it almost to orbit, and fell away too. The third finished the job, and later pushed the spacecraft towards the Moon. Each stage does part of the work, and then stops being dead weight. The stages above it never have to carry its empty tanks and engines.

That's why so many rockets look like several rockets stacked on top of each other. They are. But staging doesn't beat the rocket equation. Every stage obeys it on its own. It just stops us paying for the same empty tank over and over again.

Apollo kept doing it all the way. The lander left its lower half on the Moon. Its upper half brought the astronauts back up, and was cast off in lunar orbit. The service module was dropped just before re-entry. What came home was the one piece that had to.

5:32Why not a better engine?

So why not just build a better engine? Remember the exhaust speed in the equation. The faster the exhaust, the less fuel you need. Chemical rockets can't go much faster. Their exhaust speed is limited by the energy released in chemical reactions. Ion engines throw their propellant out several times faster. NASA's Dawn probe used them to change its own speed by more than eleven kilometres per second. But their push was tiny. About the weight of a sheet of paper resting on your hand. At full power, Dawn would have needed four days to reach sixty miles an hour. An engine like that can't lift anything off the ground.

To leave the ground, you need brute force. To travel efficiently, you need fast exhaust. No engine we've ever flown is good at both.

6:26A heavier planet

Which leaves one more question. We've been treating that nine point four kilometres per second as a fixed price. But the price is set by Earth. A heavier planet pulls harder. Escaping it takes more speed… and in the rocket equation, more speed doesn't add to the bill. It multiplies it. In 2018, the German researcher Michael Hippke did the calculation for a rocky planet ten times Earth's mass, and about one point seven times as wide. To escape Earth, a rocket needs about eleven kilometres per second. There, it would need about twenty-seven.

In his simplified single-stage rocket, the mass ratio jumps from about twenty-six to about two thousand seven hundred. A mission like Apollo, he estimated, would need a rocket of around four hundred thousand tonnes. More than a hundred Saturn Vs. His calculation suggests that chemical rockets could still escape a planet like this. But on heavier rocky worlds, a chemical rocket's fuel needs could become prohibitive. It works the other way, too. Mars has about a tenth of Earth's mass. To escape it, a rocket needs only about five kilometres per second.

In the same simplified calculation, the mass ratio drops from about twenty-six to about four.

7:54The price

The rocket equation never forbids anything. It only names the price.

On Earth, that price is a rocket that throws away almost all of itself to bring one small capsule home. On a heavier world, it might be a price nobody would ever choose to pay.
