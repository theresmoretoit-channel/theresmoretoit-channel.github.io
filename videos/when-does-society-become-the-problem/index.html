Trans People

When Does Society Become the Problem?

14 min2026-10-25

This video looks at what research says about suicide among transgender people, and what it can and cannot tell us. A Danish study that followed an entire national population over four decades. The difference between gender dysphoria and transgender identity, and how ICD-11 and the American psychiatric manual treat each. Minority stress, and how hundreds of small experiences can add up. A large survey of LGBTQ+ young people in the United States, and what it found among those who felt accepted. And the question of what society is responsible for, when we cannot remove every source of someone's pain, but can choose not to add to it.

This is the fifth and final video in a series about transgender people. The earlier ones asked why there suddenly seem to be so many trans people, how you know you're trans, why trans people change their bodies, and why it is so hard to be trans.

The full narration of the video.

0:00What if the world saw you as a problem?

Imagine waking up tomorrow and discovering that something about the way the world sees you has become a problem. Not because you have hurt anyone. Not because you have done anything wrong. But because the way you look, the way you sound, or the person you know yourself to be does not fit the expectations of the people around you. You still have to get dressed. Go to work. Buy groceries. Talk to your family. Answer questions. And all the while, you have to think about things most people never need to consider. Will I have to show an ID that no longer matches me? Will a form make me tick a box that doesn't fit? Will someone call me by a name that hurts?

Will I get to simply be myself today? For some transgender people, these are not hypothetical questions. They are part of everyday life. In Denmark, researchers used national records to identify transgender people and compare their suicide rates with those of the rest of the population over four decades. Their rate of death by suicide was about three and a half times higher. But a number like that is only one part of the story. Because transgender people are not all alike. Some are happy and comfortable with themselves.

Others struggle for years to reconcile their identity, their bodies, and the way the world treats them. There is a difference between the pain a person experiences within themselves and the pain that other people add to it. Sometimes, the two become almost impossible to separate.

1:38When your own body feels wrong

When your own body feels wrong. We need to talk about something that is harder to see from the outside. Gender dysphoria. For some transgender people, the relationship between their experienced gender and their bodies can cause profound distress. It may involve physical characteristics that feel deeply wrong. It may involve the changes brought by puberty. It may involve being perceived as a gender that does not match their identity. For others, the distress may be less about their bodies and more about how they are treated or recognised by the world.

Stand in front of a mirror. Anyone looking over your shoulder would see nothing unusual. Just a face. A body. A person getting ready for the day. But for you, that reflection may bring a sense of alienation, grief, or distress that is difficult to explain. You might try to ignore it. You might even believe that if you simply tried harder to fit in, the feeling would disappear. Sometimes people spend years trying to make that happen. And when it does not, they may begin to wonder whether something is wrong with them. But transgender identity itself is not classified as a mental disorder in the World Health Organization's ICD-11. It uses its own term, gender incongruence, and lists it separately from mental and behavioural disorders.

The American psychiatric manual does use the term gender dysphoria. But there, it describes the distress, not the identity itself. That distinction matters. It means we should not automatically treat a person's identity as the illness that needs to be cured. At the same time, real distress deserves real care. Some people benefit from psychological support. Some pursue social or medical transition. And we should never assume that we understand someone's suffering simply because we can see their body.

3:43The world adds its own weight

The world adds its own weight. Now imagine that the distress you already experience is only the beginning. You speak, and someone laughs at your voice. You go online, and a stranger who has never met you explains what you really are. At school, the teacher calls the register and pauses at your name. You phone your bank, and the person on the line doesn't believe the account is yours. And perhaps nothing particularly dramatic happens on any single day. That is part of the problem. Stress does not always arrive as one terrible event. Sometimes it builds through hundreds of small experiences.

A remark you cannot forget. A conversation you rehearse in advance. A place you avoid. A part of yourself you keep hidden because you do not know how people will react. These moments do not reset every morning. They add up, over months and years. In psychology, one framework for understanding this is called minority stress. It describes the extra burden of stigma, discrimination, expected rejection, and navigating a world where your identity may not be accepted. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis examined eighty-five studies. It found that several minority-stress factors were associated with depression, suicidal thoughts, and suicide attempts.

A separate review of European research, published in 2024, found discrimination among the most consistently documented risk factors. The environment in which people live is not irrelevant to their mental health. And that raises an uncomfortable question. What happens when someone who is already struggling must also spend their life defending their right to exist as themselves?

5:39The numbers behind the pain

The numbers behind the pain. Let's look at some numbers carefully, starting with Denmark. Out of more than six and a half million people in national records, from 1980 to 2021, researchers identified almost three thousand eight hundred as transgender. Compared with everyone else, their rate of recorded suicide attempts was nearly eight times higher. Their rate of death by suicide, the number we started with, was about three and a half times higher. But the number of deaths was small. Twelve. So the true difference could lie anywhere from about two to about six times.

In more recent decades, the gap narrowed. But it did not disappear. The study could only find people who had received hospital care related to their gender identity, or who had legally changed their gender. Trans people who never did either are not in the data. Still, it compares an entire population, not just people who chose to answer a survey. What a number like this shows is a difference. It does not show the cause. A suicide attempt can have many contributing factors. Mental health conditions, family conflict, isolation, trauma, financial insecurity, difficulties accessing care, and experiences of discrimination can overlap.

We cannot look at a statistic and determine the cause of an individual person's suffering. But the question is not whether every problem has one cause. It is whether we are willing to take every relevant cause seriously. For young people, one of the largest recent sources is The Trevor Project. In 2024, it published a survey of more than eighteen thousand LGBTQ+ young people in the United States. Among the transgender and non-binary respondents, forty-six percent had seriously considered suicide in the previous year. Fourteen percent had attempted it.

It was an online survey, not a random sample, so the exact figures cannot be applied to every young trans person. But behind every percentage is someone who had to live through the experience being counted. Someone who may have needed help. If you're struggling yourself, you can find links to free, confidential support services in the description, including options in many countries.

8:10What happens when people are accepted?

What happens when people are accepted? There is another side to this story. People are not only vulnerable to their environments. They can also find strength in them. A supportive family cannot magically eliminate every form of gender dysphoria. And acceptance is not a substitute for professional mental health care when that care is needed. The same survey asked how accepting young people felt their local communities were. Twenty percent of those who described their communities as very unaccepting reported a suicide attempt in the previous year.

Among those who described them as very accepting, it was eight percent. Transgender and non-binary young people also reported lower rates of suicide attempts when everyone they lived with respected their pronouns, or when they had access to a gender-neutral bathroom at school. These are correlations, not proof. But they challenge the idea that the social environment makes no difference. We cannot remove every source of suffering from another person's life. We can, however, choose not to make that suffering worse. We can listen without ridicule. We can respect someone's privacy.

We can allow a person to seek appropriate medical and psychological care without treating their existence as a public spectacle. We can disagree about difficult questions without turning another human being into a target. These things may sound small. To someone who spends every day expecting rejection, they may not be small at all.

9:50Who is responsible?

Who is responsible? At this point, someone might object. Why should society be held responsible for another person's mental health? Nobody gets through life without pain. We cannot make everyone happy, and we cannot force everyone to agree with everyone else. All of that is true. But it misses the difference between the pain a person carries within and the pain that others add to it. We can still ask whether our words and actions make discrimination, misunderstanding, or conflict more common. And we can ask why a person's dignity should depend on whether the majority finds their identity easy to understand.

You do not need to agree with every decision a transgender person makes to treat them with basic respect. And you do not need to abandon your questions to reject cruelty. These are not the same thing. Sometimes, when someone does not fit neatly into the categories we understand, we make that person's life harder simply because their existence makes us uncomfortable. We may call it tradition, or common sense. But we should still ask what our actions accomplish. Do they help someone who is struggling? Or do they simply make another person's life more painful?

11:11The mirror

The mirror. Let's return to the person from the beginning. They wake up tomorrow morning. They get dressed. They leave their home. They go to work, or to school, or to meet a friend. They still have their own doubts, their own struggles, and their own complicated relationship with the world. We may never understand exactly what it feels like to live their life. And perhaps we do not need to. This series was meant to look beyond the surface. To see the person behind the appearance. The person behind the arguments. The person behind the statistics.

And to recognise that whatever difficulties someone faces, they do not become less human because their life is different from ours. We often ask transgender people to explain themselves. To justify their identity. To defend their bodies. To prove that their suffering is real. But perhaps there is another question we should ask ourselves. What does our response to their suffering reveal about us? When someone is already struggling, do we offer understanding, even if we do not fully understand? Or do we demand that they make us comfortable before we are willing to treat them with dignity?

A society is revealed in how it treats people who have less power. People who are misunderstood. People whose lives challenge our assumptions about what is normal. We cannot promise that every person will be free from pain. But we can choose whether our society becomes another source of that pain. And perhaps that is the final lesson of this series. The question is not only how a person learns to live in a world that does not understand them. The question is what kind of world we are choosing to build. Because sometimes, when we look at the suffering of another person, we are not only looking at their life.

We are looking into a mirror. And what we see there is partly our own reflection.
