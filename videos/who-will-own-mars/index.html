Who Will Own Mars?

11 min2026-09-26

So who will own Mars? Nobody can own the land. But you can own a city, a reactor and everything you have built — and once safety zones appear around them, it starts looking suspiciously like property.

And the bigger question comes later. What happens when millions of people live on Mars, most of them born there, and a government on Earth still tells them they are its citizens?

The full narration of the video.

0:00A city on another planet

Imagine it's 2045. The tenth Starship has just landed on Mars. There are no astronauts inside planning to go home in a few months. These people have just moved there. And they brought construction equipment, reactors, solar panels, factories… everything they need to build the first real city on another planet. Now here's the question. Who owns Mars? Does it belong to Elon Musk? Or to the United States? Does SpaceX own it? Or does nobody own it at all? And what happens when, fifty years later, there are millions of people living on Mars? Will they still answer to Earth?

Or will they eventually say: "Thanks for the help. But from now on, we govern ourselves."

0:49The Outer Space Treaty

In 1967, the Outer Space Treaty came into force. Among other things, it says that no country can claim the Moon, Mars, or any other celestial body as its territory. So if Elon Musk lands on Mars and plants a SpaceX flag, he can't say: "I hereby declare Mars to be part of the United States." Mars isn't America. And SpaceX isn't a country.

1:22A sentence buried in Starlink's terms

SpaceX, however, seems to see it a little differently. In 2020, it published the terms of service for Starlink, its satellite internet. And buried in them is a sentence about Mars. Both parties, it says, recognise Mars as a free planet… and that no Earth-based government has authority or sovereignty over Martian activities. A satellite internet contract that already tells Earth to stay out. But the treaty has an answer to that, too.

1:54Who supervises a company on Mars?

It says that private space activities have to be authorised and continuously supervised by the appropriate state. So if SpaceX is doing something on Mars, the United States still has international responsibilities connected to those activities. But what exactly does "supervise" mean when the company is, most of the time, hundreds of millions of kilometres away? There is no lawyer from Washington standing on Mars. There is no American police force. And you definitely can't just send a sheriff over.

2:30Islands of civilisation

So let's imagine the first few decades. SpaceX builds the first base. Then another. China builds one. Europe builds another. India builds another. Maybe international partnerships and private companies join in too. And because Mars is really, really big… nobody is going to occupy the whole planet. Instead, there will be individual islands of civilisation. One city here. A mining operation two thousand kilometres away. A Chinese settlement somewhere else. And between them… nothing. Thousands of kilometres of Martian wilderness.

3:12You own the city, not the land

Here's another problem. Suppose someone lands on Mars and builds a city. You own the city. You own the buildings. You own the reactor. You own everything you've built. But you don't own the land it's sitting on. Now imagine you have a settlement on Mars. There are a hundred thousand people living there. You have a nuclear reactor. You have a water supply. And one day, another company arrives and says: "Hi. We're going to build our base right here." Right next to you. And you say: "Uh… we'd rather you didn't." They say: "Why not? Nobody owns this land." And technically, they have a point.

But their landing could throw enormous amounts of dust into the air. They could contaminate your water source. They could damage your equipment.

4:03Safety zones

So we start getting into the idea of safety zones. Not: "This territory belongs to us." But: "Before you operate here, talk to us first, because you might interfere with our activities." In 2020, the United States and seven other countries signed the Artemis Accords… principles for exploring the Moon, Mars, comets and asteroids. Safety zones are written right into them. Legally, that's something very different from owning the land. Practically… it starts looking suspiciously like property.

4:40How colonies become societies

When European powers colonised other parts of the world, they encountered enormous areas that, from their perspective, were simply… available. Never mind that people already lived there. Then someone arrived. They built a settlement. More people arrived. Governments appeared. And eventually it became rather difficult to say: "It's just a few people living somewhere far away." Because it had become a society.

Take the American colonies. Britain could say: "You are our subjects." And legally, that made sense. But eventually the colonies had their own cities, their own economy, their own political institutions, their own military, and their own interests. And eventually came the question: why should we be governed by a government thousands of kilometres away?

5:35Now put that on Mars

Now put that on Mars. Imagine the year 2150. There are ten million people living on Mars. Most of them were born there. They have never been to Earth. Earth is something they know from history. Like we know the Roman Empire today. And then some government on Earth tells them: "Under our laws, you are still our citizens." The Martian government replies: "No, we're not." Earth insists: "You have to obey our laws." Mars asks: "And why would we do that?" "Because we founded you." "That was a hundred years ago."

6:12What a state really needs

Now we reach a problem that no law can completely solve. A state isn't just a legal document. It's also the ability to actually exercise power. If Washington gives an order to a Martian settlement… how exactly does it enforce it? Do you send the police? You have to transport them to another planet. Do you send the military? Sure. But an interplanetary war is slightly more complicated than sending tanks across a border. And if Mars already has its own industry, energy infrastructure and defences… then the situation starts looking a lot like any other independent power.

6:56Could Mars become a country?

Could Mars itself become a country? And here's the surprising part. Current space law doesn't really provide a clear answer. The Outer Space Treaty says: "You cannot claim Mars as your territory." But it doesn't provide a particularly elegant answer to this question: what happens if, after several generations, a society develops on Mars and decides to create its own state?

7:24Spoilers: For All Mankind and The Expanse

Two science-fiction shows illustrate this beautifully.

First, For All Mankind. Mars is still primarily a place where Earth powers compete. The United States. The Soviet Union. Later, other players. Everyone wants a base. Everyone wants resources. Everyone wants to be first. Mars is essentially an extension of Earth's geopolitics. Then there's The Expanse. And there, the situation is completely different. Mars isn't just a colony any more. It's a civilisation of its own. It has its own government. Its own military. Its own economy. Its own culture. And its own interests. And that may be what eventually happens in reality. Not in twenty years. Probably not in a hundred.

But if people stay on Mars for several generations… it's hard to imagine that it would remain nothing more than a distant colony of Earth forever.

8:30Back to Elon

Let's go back to Elon. Because that's where we started. Imagine SpaceX really does establish the first Martian colony. The first Martian city might even be named Musk City. But a hundred and fifty years later? Millions of people are living there. Elon Musk has been dead for generations. And some teacher is telling students: "The first Martian settlement was established by a company called SpaceX." And some student asks: "Wait, what was SpaceX?"

9:04The paradox

So here's the paradox. The Outer Space Treaty may prevent Elon Musk from saying in 2045: "Mars is mine." But it may not prevent Mars from saying, in 2200: "Mars is ours." And I think that's the much more interesting question.

9:24Two stages of colonisation

The first stage of colonisation will probably look something like For All Mankind. Countries and corporations will compete for bases, resources and prestige. But if colonisation actually succeeds… we might eventually end up in something much closer to The Expanse.

9:43The question that comes next

And then the important question won't be "Who owns Mars?" It will be: who gets to decide how the people living on Mars are governed?

Mars may never belong to a single country. It may never belong to SpaceX. It may not even belong to humanity as a whole. Maybe one day it will have new countries, new nations, and new political identities.

10:09Where the history of Mars begins

And hundreds of years from now, someone on Mars might look at Earth and ask: "So our ancestors really came from there?" And Earth will be to them what Europe is to many people in the Americas today: the place their ancestors once came from. Perhaps that is where the real history of Mars will begin. Not when the first Starship lands. But when the first Martians look at Earth and say the sentence humanity has heard many times before: "We are not your colony."
