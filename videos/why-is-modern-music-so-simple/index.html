Why Is Modern Music So Simple?

13 min2026-08-27

Modern pop is simpler in some ways and far more complicated in others. This is about where the complexity went, what streaming actually rewards, and the measurement problem underneath the whole question: we compare today's average music with the best music that survived sixty years of forgetting.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00The question

Have you ever listened to a modern pop song and thought: That's it? Four chords. A simple beat. A short melody. The same chorus repeated over and over again. And yet somehow… it has hundreds of millions of streams. Meanwhile, you might listen to older music and hear complicated chord changes, long instrumental sections, unexpected key changes, and melodies that seem to go somewhere. So what happened? Did musicians simply become less talented? Did streaming services turn every song into a three-minute advertisement for the chorus?

Or is something more interesting happening? Because modern music may be simpler in some ways and far more complicated in others. And there's another problem. When we compare modern music with music from the past, we may not actually be comparing the same thing. Why does modern music often sound so simple? And perhaps more importantly: Is it actually simpler at all?

1:03What "simple" actually means

First, we need to define what we mean by simple. Because music can be simple in one dimension and incredibly complicated in another. Take harmony. A huge number of popular songs are built around just a few chords. Sometimes four. Sometimes three. The same chord progression may repeat for almost the entire track. The melody might also be relatively simple. Short phrases. Repeated patterns. And a chorus designed to get stuck in your head after hearing it once. The rhythm is often predictable too. A steady beat. A regular tempo. Verse. Chorus. Bridge. Final chorus.

None of this means the song is bad. Writing something complicated is relatively easy. Writing something simple that millions of people remember is much harder.

1:56The production side

But there is another side to modern music. The composition may be simple. The production often isn't. A modern pop song can contain dozens, or even hundreds, of individual sounds and layers. Synthesisers. Samples. Vocal harmonies. Effects. Compression. Tiny sounds appearing for only a fraction of a second. The listener may not consciously notice most of them. But they are there. So perhaps the complexity hasn't disappeared. Perhaps it has moved. From the notes themselves… to the sound of the recording.

Still, there is something real happening. And here's where it gets complicated. Some aspects of popular music have become simpler, or more restricted. Certain harmonic patterns have become less common, while others have become increasingly familiar. But that doesn't mean music as a whole became less diverse. In fact, some measures of musical diversity tell a very different story. We'll come back to that.

3:03The four chords

The famous four-chord progression is almost a cliché at this point. You can hear similar harmonic patterns across pop, rock, country and dance music. It sounds like this.

Why? Well, one reason is simple. They work. Certain chord progressions create a satisfying feeling of tension and release. They are familiar. They are emotionally flexible. And they leave plenty of room for something else. The hook. Think of it like the background of a painting. Nobody complains that the sky is too simple if the important thing is happening in the foreground.

3:49Streaming changes the incentives

But technology has changed something fundamental. Imagine buying an album in nineteen seventy-five. You spend money on it. You take it home. You are probably willing to give the music some time. Maybe the interesting part starts after a minute. Maybe the strange intro only makes sense when you hear the entire album. Now imagine a streaming service. You have access to millions of songs. And if you don't like what you hear… you can skip. Immediately. There is almost no cost. And that changes the incentives. A song now has to compete with virtually every other song ever recorded.

The first few seconds matter. A memorable sound. A recognisable voice. A beat. Something that says: Don't skip me. And this is one reason why modern songs often get to the point faster. Long instrumental introductions have become less common.

4:48What the researchers measured

Researchers measured more than three hundred American top ten singles, released between nineteen eighty-six and twenty fifteen. The wait before the first vocal had fallen from over twenty seconds to about five seconds. A drop of nearly eighty per cent. And over the same period, the average tempo rose by about eight per cent.

The same researchers also measured the titles. Song names got shorter. More and more of them are a single word. And in pop, the title is usually the hook — the line you actually sing. After the vocals start, it now arrives about eighteen per cent sooner.

5:28The charts became vocal

And there is something else. Sixty years ago, an instrumental could top the charts. Today that almost never happens. Almost everything in the charts is sung. And the singing is about anything at all — love, money, grief, a Tuesday afternoon. The song is not necessarily trying to be lazy. It is competing in an environment where the listener can leave at any moment. And now music exists inside an enormous system of data. Streaming platforms can measure what people skip, what they replay, and where they stop listening. This doesn't mean an algorithm tells musicians which chords to use. But data changes the environment. This is not a conspiracy. It's evolution.

6:16Fifteen seconds that matter

Then social media added something else. Sometimes a song doesn't become popular because people love the whole song. Sometimes they love fifteen seconds of it. One particular line. One drop. One strange sound. One moment that works perfectly in a short video. If one section can spread across millions of videos, that section becomes incredibly valuable. Almost like a tiny musical logo.

6:46Survivorship bias

Now we come to the most important problem. Was old music actually better? Or more complicated? Well… we have a very serious case of survivorship bias. When we think about the music of the nineteen sixties, we remember The Beatles. The Rolling Stones. Pink Floyd. Led Zeppelin. But those decades also produced thousands of songs that nobody remembers. Simple songs. Repetitive songs. Terrible songs. Songs that were probably annoying even when they were new. They simply disappeared. Time acted like a filter. The music that survives tends to be the music that people continued to value.

So when we compare today's charts with the past, we are often comparing: the average music of today with the best music that survived sixty years. That's not exactly a fair fight. Imagine someone from the year twenty seventy-six listening only to the greatest songs from our time. They may conclude that the twenty-twenties were an incredible golden age of music. They might never even discover half of the songs we currently complain about.

8:00Diversity didn't decline

And complicated music never disappeared. It may just not be sitting at the top of the charts. When researchers analysed the chords and textures of fifty years of American chart music, they found that variety did not steadily decline. It fell to a low point in the mid-eighties, and then rose again — to a peak in the early two-thousands.

8:23Where the complexity went

In classical music, complexity often lived in harmony. In progressive rock, in unusual time signatures. In modern electronic music, it may live somewhere completely different. Sound design. Rhythmic layering. The construction of a beat. The manipulation of a human voice. A producer might spend hours creating a sound that lasts two seconds. The final song may use only four chords.

8:52The studio in a laptop

And this kind of craft is no longer locked inside a studio. Today, almost anyone can make music. A computer can contain an entire virtual orchestra. A studio that once filled a room now fits inside a laptop. Anyone with a computer can make a record. And that is one reason why a single streaming service now carries over a hundred million tracks. And enough software to keep you busy until the heat death of the universe. But much of that software already contains accumulated knowledge. Presets, drum samples, mastering chains — decades of studio techniques, packaged into tools.

So the songwriter no longer has to carry all the complexity. The song can be simple because the sound around it doesn't have to be. Those same four chords, on their own.

And the same four chords, produced.

10:01A watch and a smartphone

It's a bit like comparing an old mechanical watch with a modern smartphone. The watch may be mechanically fascinating. The smartphone may look like a simple black rectangle. But simplicity on the surface doesn't necessarily mean simplicity underneath.

Perhaps the final answer is much older than streaming or artificial intelligence. Humans like patterns. We like familiarity. We like repetition. When the brain can predict what comes next, it receives a small reward. But if the music is completely predictable, it becomes boring. So successful music often lives in a very interesting zone. Familiar enough to understand. Different enough to remain interesting.

10:48So why is modern music so simple?

So… why is modern music so simple? Partly because streaming rewards immediacy, and partly because simple music has always worked. But perhaps mainstream music isn't becoming simpler. Perhaps it is becoming more optimised. Optimised for attention. Optimised for memorability. Optimised for replay. And optimised for surviving in a world where millions of other songs are only one click away. Meanwhile, somewhere on the internet, someone is probably writing a seventeen-minute song in eleven-eight time… and putting it online for twelve people to discover.

And honestly? That's probably fine too. Because music has never been just one thing.

11:33Simple compared to what?

The real question may not be: "Why is modern music so simple?" Maybe the better question is: "Simple compared to what?"

Because if we compare today's average pop song with the greatest music of the past, modern music can look disappointingly simple. But if we compare average music from every era… we might discover something slightly uncomfortable. Maybe people have always been writing catchy, repetitive songs. We just forgot most of them.
