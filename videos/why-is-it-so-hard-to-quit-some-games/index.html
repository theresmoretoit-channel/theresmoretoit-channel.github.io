Why Is It So Hard to Quit Some Games?

11 min2026-08-30

An unfinished thing does pull you back. That much has been measured: people return to interrupted tasks about two thirds of the time. But on its own that pull is weak. You put down a half-read book and the world carries on.

What makes it strong is that a game does not leave it on its own. It puts the unfinished thing next to a reward, next to a deadline, next to a routine you already have — and then it shows you a number. At that point you are not really playing the game any more. You are maintaining it.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Three hours, and no fun

Maybe you know this feeling. You come home from work in the evening. And you are tired. You don't really feel like doing anything complicated. So you turn on your computer. And you start a game. Not because you desperately want to play. It's just the easiest thing to do. You already know the game. You know how everything works. You don't have to learn anything new. You don't have to figure out what to play. You just log in. And then you start doing things. Daily quests. Collecting rewards. Levelling your character. Upgrading your equipment.

Farming resources. Completing achievements. And, most importantly, moving progress bars. And suddenly, three hours have passed. And you ask yourself: Did I actually have fun? Maybe. Maybe not. Because sometimes you realise that you spent the entire evening doing things you didn't really want to do. You did them because they were there. Because they were unfinished. Because they were part of your routine. Because if you didn't do them today, you would lose something. And this raises an interesting question: Why do we keep playing games even when we're no longer enjoying the actual playing?

1:20A game that never ends

Traditional games have a fairly simple structure. You start the game. You play through it. And eventually, you finish it. There is an ending. You can play it again if you want, but there is still a point where you can say: I'm done. Online games can work very differently. They don't really have an ending. Instead, they give you an enormous number of smaller goals. Levels. Gear. Collections. Achievements. Skills. Reputation. Ranks. Quests. Seasonal rewards. And when you finish one thing, there is another one waiting. And another. And another. You never really reach the end.

2:00Progress is not the problem

And there's nothing inherently wrong with that. Progress is a fantastic game mechanic. Starting at zero and gradually getting better feels good. Getting a new ability feels good. Completing a collection feels good. Finally defeating a boss you couldn't defeat before feels good. The problem begins somewhere else. It begins when you stop doing something because you enjoy doing it… and start doing it because it's unfinished.

2:32Seventy-three per cent

Imagine a progress bar at seventy-three per cent. What does that number actually mean? Nothing particularly important. But your brain immediately sees: Twenty-seven per cent is missing. And suddenly there is something to fix. You play a little more. Seventy-three becomes eighty-two. Eighty-two becomes ninety-four. And finally: One hundred per cent. Done. And that feels good. But if the game is designed around permanent progression, something else happens immediately. Another progress bar appears. Empty. And behind it, more. And the whole thing starts again.

3:12Four pulls, not one

We often talk about rewards. Experience points. Loot. Virtual currency. Cosmetics. But a reward is only one of the things pulling at you. There is something quieter sitting next to it: unfinished things. When you leave something half-done, it often pulls you back. An unfinished task at work. An unread message. A half-finished project. On its own, that pull is fairly weak. You put down a half-read book and the world carries on. A game doesn't leave it on its own. It puts the unfinished thing next to a reward. Next to a deadline. Next to a routine you already have. And then it shows you a number.

It doesn't have to tell you: Keep playing. It can simply show you: Eighty-seven per cent. And suddenly you want to make it one hundred.

4:06Playing, or maintaining?

This creates a strange situation. You stop asking: What do I want to do tonight? And start asking: What am I supposed to do tonight? That's a very important difference. Because now you're not necessarily playing the game. You're maintaining it.

You're doing your dailies. You're keeping up with the current meta. You're maintaining your character. You're collecting resources. You're making sure you don't fall behind. The game has become something you have to take care of.

4:41A world that keeps growing

But I want to finish everything. Of course you do. That's the trap. Some games are built around goals that can never really be finished. You can collect everything that exists today. And tomorrow there is a new update. New items. New achievements. New levels. New systems. New rewards. So if your goal is one hundred per cent completion, the game can give you an impossible task: Finish everything in a world that keeps expanding. And you keep trying.

5:13Why willpower is bad advice

So how do you actually stop? The obvious answer is: Just use more self-control. I don't think that's very good advice. Because when you're tired after work, you're already dealing with the worst possible combination. Low energy. A familiar habit. And a game that gives you an immediate reward. Telling yourself: I'm not going to play tonight. can be surprisingly difficult. So instead of relying entirely on willpower, you can change the environment around the habit.

5:471. Break the automatic start

The important moment isn't necessarily two hours into the gaming session. It's the moment when you decide what to do after work. If your routine is: come home, sit at the computer, launch the game, then you are already playing before you've really made a decision. So make the first step less automatic. Remove the shortcut. Turn off notifications. Log out. Don't let the game constantly remind you that something is waiting for you. And, most importantly, prepare alternatives. Not one alternative. Several. A game you've already played. A short game. A book. A movie. Something you've been meaning to do.

The goal isn't to find a new perfect hobby. The goal is simply to make sure that the familiar game isn't the only effortless option.

6:382. Try a real break

If you really want to know how much a game has taken over your routine, sometimes it helps to stop completely for a while. Not as a punishment. Not as a permanent decision. Just an experiment. Try two weeks. And pay attention to what happens. The first few days may feel strange. You may feel that something is missing. But eventually you may discover something important: You don't actually miss the game. You miss the routine. And those are not the same thing.

7:133. Don't let FOMO decide

Live-service games are very good at creating the feeling that something is happening right now. A reward expires. An event ends. A season is almost over. A daily bonus is waiting. If you don't log in today, you'll miss something. But ask yourself: What exactly am I going to lose? Some virtual currency? An item? A daily reward? A position on a leaderboard? And what do I get in exchange for not logging in? Time. Time to do something else. Because the real cost of these games isn't necessarily what you spend inside the game. It may be what you don't spend your time on outside it.

7:544. Leave things unfinished

Learn to leave things unfinished. This may be the hardest part. Leave the progress bar at seventy-three per cent. Leave the achievement unfinished. Leave the collection incomplete. Leave the quest unfinished. Log out. And see what happens. Probably nothing. The world doesn't end. Your character doesn't disappear. The unfinished thing simply remains unfinished. And you are somewhere else. If unfinished progress makes you uncomfortable, one way to weaken that feeling is to sometimes let something remain unfinished on purpose.

You don't have to win against the game. You just have to prove to yourself that you can walk away.

8:405. One uncomfortable question

Before you continue doing something in the game, ask yourself: If there were no progress for this, would I still want to do it? No experience points. No loot. No achievement. No increase in a percentage. Would I still want to do it? If the answer is yes: Great. You're playing. If the answer is no: Maybe you're not playing anymore. Maybe you're just completing.

9:05Games are not the problem

None of this means that games are bad. And it doesn't mean that progress is bad. Progress is one of the reasons games are fun. Collecting things can be fun. Getting better can be fun. Completing difficult goals can be incredibly satisfying. The problem isn't: I want to finish something. The problem is: I can't stop even though I don't want to continue. That's the line worth paying attention to. And maybe the solution isn't to stop playing games. Maybe it's to learn how to play them differently.

9:42Playing differently

Sometimes you can play towards a goal. Sometimes you can chase a difficult achievement. And sometimes you can simply wander around and do something completely pointless. No progress. No optimisation. No reward. Just because it's fun. Because perhaps that's something we forget when games become systems of permanent progression. We forget what it feels like to play a game without having to accomplish anything.

10:11You don't have to

You don't have to reach one hundred per cent. You don't have to collect everything. You don't have to claim every reward. You don't have to finish every quest. You don't have to keep up with every update. And you don't have to play every day. A game can offer you something valuable. But you don't have to give it all of your free time in return. Because in the end, the most important question isn't: What is left to complete? It's: What do I actually want to do?

And sometimes the best way to win a game… is simply to turn it off.
