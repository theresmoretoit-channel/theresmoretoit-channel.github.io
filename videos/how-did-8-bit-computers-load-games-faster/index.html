How Did 8-Bit Computers Load Games Faster?

12 min2026-11-02

This video looks at turbo loaders on 8-bit home computers: how a ZX Spectrum game could load at twice the normal speed, why the Spectrum didn't do that by itself, and how a computer that only understands the slow format loads the program that teaches it the fast one. Along the way: Speedlock and why speed wasn't its only job, a Commodore 64 cassette beating a floppy disk, Atari owners in Czechoslovakia opening up their tape recorders, and a Sharp MZ-800 program that could load games the computer itself refused.

The loading sounds in this video were generated from the real loading routines, a copy of the Spectrum's ROM loader with the constants Skool Daze used, and checked in an emulator. The whole turbo tape plays at the end.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Same tape, half the time

This is a ZX Spectrum loading a program.

And this is the same program, on the same computer, from the same kind of cassette.

It finishes in half the time. Nothing in the hardware changed. No faster tape, no faster recorder. The only thing that changed was the way the computer listened. So how did 8-bit computers learn to load games faster? And if it was that easy, why didn't they do it from the start?

0:37Make every pulse shorter

In an earlier video, we saw how the Spectrum stores bits as pulses on the tape. A short pair of pulses means zero. A pair twice as long means one. That gives about fourteen hundred bits every second. A game that fills the 48K memory takes around five minutes. Now make every pulse half as long. The computer still tells short from long. But twice as many bits pass the tape head every second. That's the whole trick behind a turbo loader. And it isn't just a thought experiment. In 1984, the game Skool Daze loaded its data at twice the Spectrum's normal speed.

1:22Why the Spectrum didn't do it itself

So why didn't the Spectrum load that fast by itself? The built-in loader is stored in the ROM, the memory chip the computer starts from. It doesn't measure a pulse in seconds. It counts. After each edge of the signal, it waits a moment, then counts how long until the next one. One single number in the ROM decides where zero ends and one begins. And that number leaves a lot of room. A zero can stretch by more than forty per cent and still be read as a zero. That margin is what lets a worn tape on a cheap recorder still load.

And the computer wasn't the limit. The Spectrum checks the tape signal about sixty thousand times a second. The cassette was the limit. Shorter pulses mean higher tones. At double speed, a zero moves from two kilohertz to four. And high tones are exactly what a cheap recorder, or a slightly misaligned head, loses first. A Spectrum magazine in 1986 reckoned most people could manage at least twice the normal speed. How much further depended on the recorder.

2:34The loader that loads the loader

But turbo had a stranger problem. If the computer only knows the slow format, how does it load the program that teaches it the fast one? The answer is at the start of the tape. The first part is recorded in the ordinary, slow format. It's short, and the ROM loads it like any other program. Then that little program takes over. In the simplest case, it's a copy of the ROM's own loading routine, with a few numbers changed. In Skool Daze, the changes sit in three places. A shorter wait, a new starting count, a new dividing line.

So the ROM loads the program that does the ROM's job, only faster. It's a bit like downloading a faster downloader. The slow step gets you the tool. The tool does the rest.

3:26Speedlock

Commercial games used the same idea. One well-known example was Speedlock, first used by Ocean in 1984. One of its authors said he wrote it because he was fed up with how long the standard loader took. But here's the surprise. Speedlock was only about one and a half times as fast. The first day of Daley Thompson's Decathlon took three minutes twenty seconds, where the same data at normal speed would take four minutes forty-seven. Later versions were only about a fifth faster than the ROM. Because speed wasn't the only job.

From 1983, copying programs could read a game's header and copy the whole game onto another tape. A custom format gave those programs nothing to read. Early Speedlock even chopped its lead-in tone into short bursts, so the ROM's own loader wouldn't recognise it at all. And you could hear it. A Speedlock tape loaded with clicks, and jerking stripes around the screen.

It didn't stop anyone copying a tape from one deck to another. But it did give publishers a reason to write their own loaders.

4:42Commodore 64: a cassette beats a floppy

On the Commodore 64, the reason to switch was much bigger. Its built-in routine recorded everything twice, as a safety copy. A forty-kilobyte game took about twelve and a half minutes. And here, the recorder did almost nothing clever. It just turned the sound into clean on and off pulses. The computer timed every pulse itself, in software. So a faster loader was just a different program. In 1985, Compute! magazine tested one called TurboTape. Once it had found the program on the tape, it loaded twelve kilobytes in twenty-eight seconds.

Commodore's own floppy disk drive needed thirty-four seconds. A cassette, beating a floppy disk.

And because the computer was doing all the listening itself, it could do other things in between. Loaders such as Freeload gave reading the tape priority. Whatever processor time was left went to music, smooth scrolling, even small games. With Mastertronic's Invade-a-Load, you played a Space Invaders clone while the real game loaded.

The Atari 8-bit computers were a different story.

5:55Atari: open the recorder

Here, the recorder did the listening. Inside it were two filters, one for each tone, and a circuit that handed the computer finished ones and zeros. The standard was six hundred bits a second, and the filters limited how much faster it could go. Clever software could nudge that up a little, at best to about twelve hundred. But to go much faster, you had to open the recorder. And in Czechoslovakia, people did. A floppy drive there cost two to three times as much as the computer itself. So most Atari owners there stayed with tape.

In 1987, a Prague student from the local Atari club designed Turbo 2000. It added one new path for the signal. A wire inside the recorder took the sound straight from its amplifier, before the filters, through one small chip, and onto the line to the computer. A switch, flipped by the computer, chose between the old path and the new. The computer now timed the pulses itself. Around two thousand three hundred bits a second. Almost four times as fast.

7:09The price of speed

Turbo wasn't one universal format. Sometimes you changed the program. Sometimes, you had to change the machine.

And faster wasn't free. Compute! warned that a TurboTape recording would sometimes refuse to load on a different recorder, with its head aligned slightly differently. The standard save, it said, was much slower, but extremely reliable. Recording everything twice wasn't Commodore being lazy. It was the price of loading reliably.

7:45The program that repaired games

But a different loader could also do something unexpected. It could read a tape that the built-in one refused. On the Sharp MZ-800, a computer that found its way to Czechoslovakia in the late 1980s, owners knew a program called Turbo Copy. A game would fail to load, with a checksum error. Owners found Turbo Copy could often load it anyway. Then you saved it at normal speed onto a fresh cassette, and the computer loaded it normally again. It felt like repairing a broken game.

8:19A stopwatch set in advance

So how could one loader read what the other couldn't? The Sharp's ROM recorded every block twice, at about twelve hundred bits a second. And it never measured a pulse. After each pulse began, it waited a fixed moment, and looked once. Still high? That's a one. Already low? That's a zero. A stopwatch, set in advance. On an old tape, or a different recorder, the pulses came back a little rounder. Then that single look could see the wrong level. One wrong bit spoils the checksum. If both copies fail, the load fails. Turbo Copy listened differently. It timed every pulse with the computer's timer chip.

And before each block, it listened to the lead-in tone, and set its dividing line from the pulses it was actually hearing. Its stopwatch was set to the tape in front of it. And the repair wasn't a copy of the sound. Turbo Copy kept the bytes, and wrote a brand new, cleanly timed signal. The data hadn't been lost. The usual way of reading it just wasn't good enough. Turbo Copy could also save the game with turbo, at two or three times the normal speed, with its own small loader in front.

9:42What they all have in common

And that's what all of these have in common. The cassette stayed the same. What changed was the way it was read.

Today, we expect games to load instantly. In the 1980s, loading was part of the game. You listened to the tape. You watched the stripes. You waited. And sometimes, a different loader made the same cassette behave like a completely different machine. The computer didn't need a faster tape. It needed a better way to listen.

10:17A question for you

So here's a question for you. Did you use turbo loaders back then? How do you remember them? Did they fail more often? Let me know in the comments.

10:27The whole turbo tape

And finally, the whole turbo tape, start to finish. First the part at normal speed, then the turbo. For a program this small, turbo doesn't pay off. The loader takes longer than the time it saves. For a whole game, it could save minutes.
