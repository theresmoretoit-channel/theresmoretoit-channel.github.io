Your Body, and How Long It Lasts

Why We May Not Be Able to Live Forever

11 min2026-08-30

So we would have solved ageing. Could we live forever?

Maybe not. A recent mathematical model asked what would be left if every reversible cause of ageing were fixed, and landed on a median human lifespan of about 156 years. The bottleneck it points to is not the body in general. It is the tissue we cannot replace without replacing what it holds.

The full narration of the video.

0:00Could we solve ageing?

Imagine we had a technology that could repair the human body. We could remove damaged cells. Replace worn-out tissue. Repair damaged DNA. Rejuvenate mitochondria. We could even make some cells biologically young again. An eighty-year-old could have the body of a forty-year-old. So… we've solved ageing. Could we live forever? Maybe not. Because there is one part of the body that makes this much more complicated. The brain. And one recent mathematical model took a perfectly maintained human body and did the maths. It might eventually reach somewhere around a hundred and fifty years.

But is that really a hard limit? And why would the brain be the problem?

0:49Why we age

Our bodies are constantly repairing themselves. Cells die. New cells replace them. Our skin renews itself. Our blood cells are constantly replaced. The lining of our intestines can renew itself incredibly quickly. So why can't the whole body just keep doing this forever? Because the repair process isn't perfect. DNA gets damaged. Mitochondria wear out. Waste products accumulate inside cells. Some cells stop working properly, but don't die. These are called senescent cells. And over time, cells also change the way they switch genes on and off. These are epigenetic changes.

It's a little like an operating system that has been running for decades. It still works. But over time, bugs accumulate. Old configurations remain. Files become corrupted. And the system becomes less reliable. Our bodies aren't computers. But the analogy is not completely wrong.

1:57What if we fixed all of it?

Now imagine that science gets really good at this. We can remove senescent cells. Repair damaged DNA. Rejuvenate mitochondria. Restore stem cells. And reset some of the epigenetic changes associated with ageing. We wouldn't just be treating individual diseases anymore. We could potentially keep the body in a younger state. Instead of letting the body deteriorate for eighty years and then trying to repair it, we'd maintain it continuously. Like servicing a machine. Replace worn-out parts. Repair damage. Keep everything working. So why couldn't we do the same thing with a human?

Maybe a body is a machine. But not every part of a machine can simply be swapped. Some are easy to replace. Others are not.

2:50The one part we cannot swap

And the most difficult one is the brain. The human brain contains roughly eighty-six billion neurons. And those neurons are connected by an enormous network of synapses. That network contains a large part of what makes you you. Your memories. Your skills. Your personality. Everything you've learned during your life. So we can't simply say: "This neuron is old. Let's replace it." With a skin cell, that's relatively simple. With a neuron, it could be a very big deal. Because the new neuron might not have exactly the same connections. And if you change enough neurons and synapses, you may change the structure of the brain itself.

3:35Damage that piles up in a neuron

Most neurons in our brains stay with us for decades. And while they're there, damage slowly accumulates. Including mutations in their DNA. Our cells have remarkably good systems for repairing DNA. But they're not perfect. A cell that survives for a hundred or a hundred and fifty years has a lot more time to accumulate mistakes than one that survives for only a few months. And eventually, enough damage could affect how the cell works. And this is where that number comes in.

4:10Where 156 comes from

A recent mathematical model asked a fascinating question. What happens if we remove most of the ordinary causes of ageing? The answer was surprising. The model estimated a median human lifespan of roughly a hundred and fifty-six years. Median, not maximum. In the model, half of the population would reach beyond a hundred and fifty-six years. But this does not mean there is a biological timer that kills us at a hundred and fifty-six. And it doesn't prove that humans can't live longer. It's a mathematical model based on a particular set of assumptions.

But what happens when we fix almost everything else? Some cells are extremely difficult to replace without changing the information they contain.

5:01A heart is a pump. A brain is not.

The model points at two of them. The heart. And the brain. A heart is a pump. If we ever learn to grow one, we could simply fit a new one and lose nothing. We have already done a version of this. People have lived with an artificial heart that has no pulse at all, its speed set from outside the body. Not for long. Weeks, sometimes months. And not without complications. But the principle held. Their blood kept moving, and they were still themselves. The brain is the one place where replacing the part means replacing the information.

Let's imagine that science gets even better. We can repair the DNA inside every neuron. Remove damaged proteins. Restore mitochondria. Fix every problem we know how to fix. Maybe we could keep the brain biologically young too.

5:56What does repairing a brain even mean?

But that leads to a much stranger question. What does it actually mean to repair a brain? Think about a computer. If you replace a damaged transistor, the data stays intact. Replace the hard drive with an exact copy, and the data is still there. But what happens if you slowly replace every component? At some point, none of the original hardware remains. Is it still the same computer? And what about a human being?

6:29Replace every neuron, slowly

Let's take this to an extreme. Imagine replacing a different one percent of your neurons every year. The new neurons are perfect copies. Same function. Same connections. Same information. After one hundred years, none of your original neurons would remain. But the process happened so gradually that you never noticed. Would you still be the same person? Most people would probably say yes. There was never a moment when you suddenly disappeared. Now change the thought experiment. Do exactly the same thing… but in one second. Is the result different?

7:10And what about a copy?

And what if we could create a perfect copy of your entire brain? It would have your memories. Your personality. Your experiences. And it would look at you and say: "I'm still me." Would that copy actually be you? Or would it simply be a new person who happens to have all your memories? At this point, we're no longer talking only about biology. We're talking about personal identity.

7:38Healthspan, not lifespan

And that's why I think extending healthspan is much more interesting than simply extending lifespan. We don't need to live for a thousand years. We don't even need to live for two hundred. Imagine something much simpler. You reach forty. You're still physically capable. Then fifty. Still healthy. Sixty. Still active. Seventy. Still independent. Eighty. Still capable of living your own life. What if we could slow biological ageing enough to make that normal? That could matter far more than simply pushing human lifespan higher.

8:16What age actually gives us

Because age gives us something valuable. Something we don't have at twenty. Experience. A twenty-year-old may have plenty of energy. But they are still working out what to do with it. At forty, you've accumulated twenty years of experience. At sixty, perhaps forty. And that experience can be incredibly valuable. You may understand your profession better. You may understand people better. You may understand yourself better. You may simply make better decisions. But that's exactly when the biological hardware starts slowing down. If we could keep the hardware working… we could keep the experience.

Modern life doesn't always fit very well with human biology. We often spend decades learning, building careers, and figuring out what we want. And by the time we're ready, biology may already be running out of time. Slowing biological ageing could give us something more important than extra years. We'd gain freedom over the order of our lives.

9:26Where this leaves us

So how long could we actually keep living? Maybe the future isn't a world where humans become immortal. Maybe it's something more interesting. A world where the boundaries between youth, middle age and old age start to blur. A hundred-year-old person might not resemble today's hundred-year-old at all. And if we eventually learn how to repair the brain itself, even a hundred and fifty-six years might not be a meaningful limit. Because that number depends on what we believe we can repair. And technology has a strange habit. When we don't know how to fix something, we tend to think it's a fundamental limit. Until someone figures out how to fix it.

So maybe the real question isn't: "How long can a human life last?"

Maybe it's: "How long can we keep a human being human?"

And that may be a much more interesting question than immortality.
