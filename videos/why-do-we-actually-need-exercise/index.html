Your Body, and How Long It Lasts

Why Do We Actually Need Exercise?

9 min2026-09-19

In 1966, five healthy twenty-year-olds spent three weeks in bed. Not ill, not injured — just horizontal. When the researchers measured how much oxygen their bodies could use at full effort, it had fallen by 27 per cent. Thirty years later the same five men came back, and it turned out that three weeks in bed at twenty had done more damage than three decades of ordinary ageing.

That number is really what this video is about, because it tells you what kind of thing a body is. Not a lump of matter that keeps its shape until something breaks it, but a system that keeps asking one question: how much am I being asked to do?

The full narration of the video.

0:00Exercise is good for you

People say that exercise is good for you. But have you ever stopped to ask why? Why does your body actually need you to move? We do not have to walk to get water. We do not have to go looking for food. We do not have to walk miles to work. We do not even have to stand up to change the channel. And yet doctors keep saying the same thing. Move more.

0:25So what goes wrong when we don't?

So what exactly goes wrong when we don't?

In 1966, five healthy young men agreed to an experiment. They were twenty years old. For three weeks, they stayed in bed. Not ill. Not injured. Just horizontal. Then the researchers measured how much oxygen their bodies could use at full effort.

0:49Twenty-seven per cent gone

It had fallen by twenty-seven per cent. In three weeks. The heart was the main reason. Every beat was moving about a quarter less blood than it had three weeks earlier.

1:01The study waited thirty years

And then the study did something almost no study does. It waited. The same five men came back thirty years later. They were fifty now. Three decades of ordinary ageing had cost them twelve per cent.

1:16Three weeks versus thirty years

Three weeks in bed at twenty had done more damage than thirty years of getting older.

That number is really the whole video. Because it tells you what kind of thing a body is. A body is not a lump of matter that holds its shape until something breaks it. It is a system that keeps asking one question. How much am I being asked to do?

1:42Muscle is the obvious case

Muscle is the obvious case. Stop loading it, and it does not simply stay as it was. It gets taken apart. In bed rest studies, leg muscle can go at roughly half a per cent a day.

1:56Bone, and where it goes first

Bone does the same thing, more slowly. Astronauts in orbit lose bone density at something like one to two per cent a month. And here is the detail that gives the game away. They do not lose it evenly. The arms, which never carried their own weight anyway, lose much less of it. The hips and the spine, which used to, lose much more.

2:19Not decaying — measuring

The body is not decaying. It is measuring. And it is measuring where the load actually goes.

And you do not need a hospital bed or a spacecraft to watch this happen. Ten healthy young men were asked to do one thing. Walk less.

2:38Ten thousand steps to thirteen hundred

They went from about ten thousand steps a day to roughly thirteen hundred. For two weeks. By the end of it, their muscles had become measurably worse at pulling sugar out of the blood. About a sixth worse, on the measure the researchers used. Their fitness had dropped as well, and so had the lean mass in their legs. Nobody was ill. Nobody was in bed. They had simply stopped walking to things.

3:06So why is a body built like that?

So why would a body be built like that? Because for almost all of our history, there was no way to opt out. If they wanted food, they had to get to it. If they wanted to be somewhere else, they walked. They carried things. They worked with their hands. They walked, ran and climbed. And this is the part worth holding on to. Movement was not exercise. It was just what a day looked like.

3:35The Hadza

And we can put a number on that. The Hadza are a hunting and gathering people in Tanzania. Forty-six of them wore heart rate monitors. They spent about two and a quarter hours of every day at moderate to vigorous effort. The health guidelines most countries publish ask for a hundred and fifty minutes of that. Per week. The Hadza pass the weekly target in two days.

4:02Meanwhile, the rest of us

Meanwhile, a study that counted steps on seven hundred thousand phones put the global average at about five thousand a day. Now think about an ordinary working day. You get up. You sit in the car. You sit at work. You sit at a screen. You sit down for lunch. You sit in the car going home. You sit in front of another screen in the evening. Not one moment of it told your body that it needed to be capable of anything.

4:33Where this story usually goes wrong

Now, this is the point where this kind of story usually goes wrong. Because it is very easy to turn it into: everything old was good, everything new is bad. It was not. Hunter-gatherers were not healthier at everything. Infections, injuries and childbirth were dangerous in ways they are not for us. And there is one result that complicates the story even further.

4:58They burn the same as us

When researchers measured how much energy the Hadza actually burn in a day, it came out about the same as ours. Same body size, same daily total. All that walking did not show up as a bigger number. So this is not a story about burning more. The body seems to spend a similar budget either way. It is a story about what it is being asked to do with it.

5:25Why does it not feel like it?

Which brings up the strangest part. If movement matters that much, why does it not feel like it? You know when you are hungry. You know when you are thirsty. You know when you have not slept. All of those come with an alarm, because in each case the trouble arrives within days.

5:44There is no alarm

There is no equivalent alarm for not moving. What your body does instead is quietly lower the target.

Capacity you are not using is expensive to keep. So it is let go. Which means everything still feels fine. Because fine is now measured against a day of sitting down.

6:05Two flights of stairs

You only find out when you ask for something you have not asked for in years. Two flights of stairs. A run for a bus.

6:15The second half

And then there is the second half of it, which is almost unfair. We did not only evolve to move. We evolved to avoid moving when we did not have to. Energy was scarce. Anything you spent walking for no reason was energy not spent on staying alive. So the voice that says not today is not laziness that arrived with the sofa. It is an old instinct that used to be right.

6:44A thought experiment

Now for a question that is pure speculation. And it is worth saying so out loud. What if sitting had always been normal? Not for decades. Not for a few generations. But for hundreds of thousands of years. Food always within reach. Transport everywhere. Survival depending on how well you handled information. Given that much time, evolution would have something to work with. Maybe the muscle would go. Maybe the skeleton would be built lighter. Maybe the whole way we store and spend energy would shift. We do not know what that human would look like. Anyone who tells you they do is guessing.

7:29Three generations

But the guess is not the point. The point is the timescale. Our surroundings changed in about three generations. Evolution does not work at that speed. So we ended up with bodies shaped by a world where movement was unavoidable, living in a world where it is optional. And then we act surprised that we have to go to a gym, or run, or ride a bike, just to keep the thing working.

7:57The wrong shape

Which is why the sentence exercise leads to health is slightly the wrong shape. When you go for a run, you may not be adding a bonus on top of a normal body. You may be handing your body back the one input it was built around.

8:11Perhaps the question was never that

So perhaps the question was never why is exercise healthy? Perhaps it is: why would we expect a body built for movement to work properly without it? Movement is not something we added to the human body. Movement is part of the human body.

And exercise is good for you might just be a long way of saying: this is what normal feels like.
