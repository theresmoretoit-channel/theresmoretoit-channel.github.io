How Do You Know You're Trans?

11 min2026-10-22

This video looks at how people come to understand that they're trans. Why it is often a slow process rather than a single dramatic moment. Why some people start asking these questions in childhood or puberty, and others at thirty, forty or sixty. Why it is hard to recognise something you don't have a word for. And why not fitting gender stereotypes, or being attracted to people of the same sex, is not the same thing as being trans.

It also looks at doubt, at people who suppress those feelings, at people who realise they're trans but decide not to transition, and at detransition: what it does and doesn't tell us.

The full narration of the video.

0:00How do you know you're a man or a woman?

Imagine someone asking you: "How do you know you're a man?" Or: "How do you know you're a woman?" You might have to think about it for a moment. And then you might answer: "I just know." And that's actually a rather interesting answer. Because most people never have to examine their gender identity. Nobody asks them to prove it. Nobody says: "Explain why you consider yourself a man." It's simply part of their life. But what if that sense of certainty isn't there? What if you start wondering: "Am I really who everyone thinks I am?"

And even more importantly: how do you know if the answer is no?

0:42There isn't always a single moment

There isn't always a single moment. When we imagine someone discovering that they're trans, it's easy to picture a dramatic moment. As if one day they suddenly realise: "Oh. I've actually been someone else all along." Sometimes the realisation really can be that powerful. But not always. For many people, it's a much slower process. It might start with nothing more than a vague feeling: "Something about this doesn't feel right." But what exactly? Often, the person doesn't know. Maybe they don't feel comfortable in their body. Maybe they don't like the way other people address them.

Maybe being seen as a man or a woman feels strangely uncomfortable. Or maybe they can't explain it at all.

1:39Childhood, puberty, adulthood

Childhood, puberty, adulthood. Some trans people say they questioned their gender from an early age. Others didn't notice anything like that. For some, puberty becomes the turning point. Their body suddenly starts changing. And they may discover that changes which are a completely normal part of growing up for everyone else feel deeply uncomfortable to them. But even then, they may not think: "I'm trans." They may not even know the word transgender. They may simply think: "I hate what's happening to my body." And some people don't start asking these questions until much later. They may be thirty, forty, or sixty years old.

They may have a partner, children, a job, and an entire life that looks completely ordinary from the outside. And yet they may eventually start wondering: "Why have I felt this way for so long?"

2:36But why didn't you know earlier?

"But why didn't you know earlier?" That's actually a reasonable question. If someone is trans, why didn't they simply know from the beginning? But in order to recognise and describe something, you need words for it. Imagine someone who has never heard the word left-handed. They only know that writing with their right hand feels strangely difficult. They might spend years thinking they're simply bad at writing. Then one day they discover that some people naturally use their left hand. And they think: "Wait. Maybe that's me."

Gender, of course, is not the same thing as handedness. But the process of putting a name to something you've experienced for a long time can be similar.

3:22Maybe I'm not man enough

"Maybe I'm not man enough." There's another complication. Our ideas about being a man or a woman aren't just about sex. Society adds a whole collection of expectations. Men are supposed to be strong. Women are supposed to be feminine. Men are supposed to like certain things. Women are supposed to like others. And when someone doesn't fit these expectations, they may start wondering: "Maybe I'm just not masculine enough." Or: "Maybe I'm not feminine enough." A trans person can have an additional question: "Maybe I'm not trans. Maybe I'm just a man who doesn't like traditional masculinity."

And that's an important question. Because not fitting gender stereotypes is not the same thing as being trans. A man can love pink. A woman can hate dresses. A man can be gentle and sensitive. A woman can be aggressive, technically minded, or completely uninterested in her appearance. Gender identity is not a list of personality traits.

4:32And what about sexuality?

And what about sexuality? Another source of confusion can be sexual orientation. Someone might realise they're attracted to people of the same sex. And they might ask: "So maybe I'm not a man?" But sexual orientation and gender identity are two different things. A man can be gay. A man can be straight. A man can be bisexual. And a trans person can have any sexual orientation. So being attracted to certain people doesn't, by itself, tell you whether you're trans.

5:07What if I'm just imagining it?

"What if I'm just imagining it?" And now we get to something that may be much less visible from the outside. Doubt. Someone may start asking: "Am I really trans?" "Or am I just unhappy with my body?" "Or do I simply hate traditional gender roles?" "Or am I trying to explain something else entirely?" "What if I'm wrong?" And sometimes these questions keep coming back. There is no simple test that gives you a result: Trans: yes. Or: Trans: no.

5:42Suppressing it

Suppressing it. Some people decide they simply won't think about it. Maybe they tell themselves: "This is nonsense." "I just need to be normal." "It'll go away." "I'm not going to deal with this." And sometimes people continue living their original lives. Not everyone who questions their gender starts transitioning. And not everyone who discovers that they're trans decides to make changes. There can be many reasons. Someone may not want to change their life. Someone may not want to change their body. Someone may not want to tell other people.

And someone may simply reach the conclusion: "Yes, this is my identity. But I don't want to transition." That's not a contradiction. Identity and what you decide to do about it are not the same thing.

6:38So do you have to transition?

"So do you have to transition?" No. And that's an important point. In the previous video, we distinguished between social, legal, and medical transition. But none of them automatically follows from discovering that you're trans. Some people change the way they dress. Some use a different name or pronouns. Some take hormones. Some undergo surgery. Some do only some of these things. And some do none of them. Right now, we're interested in something else: how does someone actually come to understand their own identity?

7:16Telling someone else

Telling someone else. And then there may be another step. Coming out. Someone who has spent months or years wondering: "Am I trans?" may eventually have to tell someone else: "I think I'm trans." And that can be a strange moment. Because inside that person's head, something may have been happening for years. But for the other person, this may be completely new information. And they may ask: "How do you know?" Which brings us right back to where we started.

7:49How do you know?

How do you know? Maybe the person doesn't have a simple answer. Maybe they can list dozens of memories. Some from childhood. Some from adolescence. Some from the last few months. But none of them necessarily proves anything on its own. And perhaps that's exactly why the idea of a universal "trans test" doesn't really work. Because gender identity isn't something most people discover through a single measurement. It's something people come to understand through their own experience.

8:25What if you're wrong?

There's one more uncomfortable question we should ask. What if you're wrong? Some people eventually stop or change their transition. This is often described as detransition. The reasons can be different. Some people change their gender identity. Others discover that some parts of transition aren't right for them. And for some, the circumstances in which they live may play an important role. The existence of detransition doesn't prove either of the two simple conclusions people sometimes draw from it. It doesn't mean that everyone who transitions will eventually discover they were "really cisgender" all along. And it doesn't mean that nobody ever changes their mind or stops transitioning.

People can make mistakes. People can change their opinions. And people can understand themselves differently as they grow older. That is true far beyond questions of gender.

9:27So how do you know you're trans?

So we're back to the original question. How do you know you're trans? Some people describe it as something they knew from a very early age. Others aren't sure for years. Some first have to figure out that their problem isn't simply with gender stereotypes. Others don't question their gender until adulthood. Some suppress those feelings. Some don't transition. And some eventually change their minds. There isn't one universal story.

Perhaps the most honest answer to the question: "How do you know you're trans?" is simply: "Because I tried to understand why I've felt the way I've felt for so long."

For one person, the answer may come very early. For another, it may take years. And someone else may never find a completely certain answer. But we already know one thing: trans people don't have to look a certain way. They don't have to behave a certain way. They don't have to have a particular sexual orientation. And they don't have to undergo any particular treatment. But if someone comes to believe that their body and their gender identity don't fit together, another question appears: what can they actually do about it? And that's the story we'll explore next.
