What Would It Take for Russia to Change?

9 min2026-10-19

Russia has tried changing the face at the top. So what would it actually take for the system itself to change, and how long would it take? This video looks at four conditions, from courts and elections to oil money, borders and the people who leave, and at the one thing nobody can predict: when change starts, and which way it goes.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A new president in 2008

In May 2008, Russia got a new president. He was forty-two, and a lawyer by training. A few months before taking office, he told an economic forum in Siberia that freedom is better than unfreedom. He talked about fighting corruption, independent courts, and modernising the economy. Three months after he took office, Russian troops were fighting in Georgia. And in 2011, he announced that he would step aside, so that the man he had replaced could return to the job. His name was Dmitry Medvedev. The man before him and after him was Vladimir Putin.

Russia has already tried changing the face at the top. On its own, that did not change the system.

0:47Where we left off

In the previous video, we looked at why Russia keeps choosing confrontation, even when it makes the country weaker. The short version: the pattern is not Russia's destiny. It keeps returning because it serves a political system that needs an enemy abroad. So what would it actually take to change that system? And how long would it take?

1:18Waiting for a new leader

Start with the most tempting answer. Wait for a new leader. The trouble is that a new leader inherits the old tools. In October 1993, President Yeltsin, the man who had faced down the Soviet hardliners, sent tanks to fire on his own parliament. Two months later, Russia adopted a new constitution. It gave the president enormous power. It was written for a reformer who wanted to push reforms through. It works just as well for anyone who comes after him. In 2020, it was amended again. The change allows Putin to stay in office until 2036.

1:59Change from above

That is the deeper Russian pattern. Change has usually come from the top. And what the top gives, the top can take back. Alexander the Second freed the serfs in 1861. After he was assassinated, his son rolled back many of his other reforms. Khrushchev's thaw was followed by Brezhnev's freeze. Gorbachev's openness ended with the collapse of the state. So Russia is not a country that has never tried to change. It is a country where change has rarely been built to last.

2:35Condition one: power that can lose

What would make it last? The first condition sounds dull. Power would have to be able to lose. In Russian criminal courts, fewer than one defendant in a hundred is acquitted. That number alone proves little. But Russian courts also rarely rule against the state in political cases. The judge looks less like a referee, and more like part of the state's team. Since the Soviet Union collapsed, Russia has never had a national government lose a genuinely competitive election and peacefully hand over power to its political opponents.

That is the real test. Not whether elections exist, but whether the people in charge can lose one and go home. Some former dictatorships have passed that test. How they got there is the subject of an earlier video. Why does this matter for war and peace? Because a leader who can lose power has to think about the cost of mistakes. A leader who cannot only has to think about loyalty.

3:40Condition two: money

The second condition is about money. Norway also lives off oil and gas. But its oil money goes into a fund now worth more than two trillion dollars. Over time, the budget is meant to spend roughly what the fund is expected to earn in real terms, while leaving room for bad years. Oil alone does not make a country authoritarian. What matters is who controls the money, and whether anyone can check them. In Russia, the same small circle controls the pipes, the budget, and the courts that could question them. Breaking that link would mean independent courts, real property rights, and businesses that can say no to the state.

4:24Condition three: borders

The third condition is the one closest to the war. Russia would have to treat its neighbours' borders as final. Not as a new principle. As one it has already signed. In 1994, as Ukraine gave up its nuclear weapons, Russia gave it security assurances. Among them, a promise to respect its independence and existing borders. In 1997, the two countries signed a treaty that confirmed those borders again. There is a precedent for a former great power letting go of lost territory. After the Second World War, Germany lost about a quarter of its pre-war land.

In 1970, West Germany signed a treaty accepting its border with Poland. In 1990, a reunited Germany confirmed that it was final. Today, Germany is Poland's largest trading partner. It gave up a claim and gained a neighbour.

5:25Condition four: people

The fourth condition is people. Since 2022, hundreds of thousands of Russians have left, many of them programmers, scientists, and engineers. A modern economy cannot be ordered into existence from the Kremlin. It needs people who want to stay and can build without permission.

5:46How long would it take?

So how long would all this take? Some things could change in months. A new government could stop the war, release more than a thousand people recognised as political prisoners, and let independent media work again. Other things would take years. Independent courts need judges who were not trained to obey. Real elections need parties that have had time to grow. And some things take a generation. In a 2018 survey, two thirds of Russians said they regretted the collapse of the Soviet Union. Expectations about power do not change with a new law. They change when people see that the new rules hold.

Trust between neighbours is slower still. In 1970, on the day of that treaty, the West German chancellor knelt at the memorial to the Warsaw Ghetto. Twenty years later, after reunification, the border was confirmed for good. Fourteen years after that, Poland joined the European Union. Thirty-four years from the first gesture. And that was with a democratic Germany on one side.

6:58What nobody can predict

And there is something we cannot know at all. When change will begin, or what direction it will take. The next leader might not be a reformer. In June 2023, a private army marched towards Moscow and got within a few hundred kilometres before turning back. Its leader was not trying to make Russia more peaceful. He wanted the generals replaced and the war fought harder. A weaker regime does not automatically produce a better one. But history is full of surprises in the other direction too.

7:33A question from 1969

In 1969, a Soviet dissident wrote an essay with a question for a title. Will the Soviet Union survive until 1984? Few experts took the prediction seriously. The Soviet Union looked permanent. It lasted until 1991.

7:54A prisoner of its history?

So is Russia a prisoner of its own history? Its history explains why the old pattern keeps coming back. Power at the top. Reforms that can be taken away. But the same history also contains the freed serfs, the thaw, and the open years. Moments when Russia tried something different. What it has rarely contained are institutions strong enough to keep those choices alive. That is what would have to be different this time. Not a better man at the top. A system that does not depend on one. Nobody can say when that might happen, or whether it will.

The past can explain why a pattern keeps returning. It cannot decide whether it returns again.
