Ordinary Things, Awkward Questions

What If 9/11 Never Happened?

11 min2026-09-11

Now fast-forward 25 years. How much of the world you live in today exists because of something that happened that morning?

This is not a video about the attack. It is a video about the world we might have had instead — and about how quickly that question stops having answers. No invasion of Afghanistan. Probably a very different decision about Iraq. An airport that still annoys you, but not like this. A surveillance apparatus that was already growing before anyone had heard of the Patriot Act.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The morning nothing happened

Imagine waking up on the morning of September 11, 2001. You get up. You go to work. You turn on the television. And nothing happened. No planes crashed into the World Trade Center. No plane hit the Pentagon. No plane crashed in Pennsylvania. New York looks completely normal. It is just another Tuesday. At first, almost nothing seems different. But now imagine something much harder. Fast-forward 25 years. It is 2026.

0:36A world where it never happened

You are living in a world where September 11 never happened. What would that world look like? And more importantly. How much of our world today exists because of something that happened that morning?

0:51Afghanistan

Let's start with the obvious. There would probably be no invasion of Afghanistan in October 2001. The Taliban would still control most of the country. Osama bin Laden would still be wanted by the United States. But the enormous military campaign that followed would probably never happen in the same form. Thousands of American soldiers would never be sent there. And the war would have taken a very different human toll.

1:21History has no undo button

But even here, we have to be careful. History doesn't come with an undo button. The United States already had reasons to be concerned about terrorism. Al-Qaeda already existed. The Taliban already sheltered bin Laden. And the Middle East was already politically unstable. So removing September 11 doesn't mean terrorism disappears. It means one particular chain of events never begins. And that distinction is extremely important.

1:54Iraq

Now it gets harder. In 2003, the United States invaded Iraq. Officially, the invasion was not a response to September 11. Iraq was not responsible for the attacks. But politically, that morning completely changed the environment in which the decision was made. The American public had just experienced the largest terrorist attack in the country's history. The government was operating under a new sense of threat. And the idea that another catastrophic attack could happen at any moment was no longer hypothetical. Without September 11, would George Bush still have invaded Iraq?

Maybe. Maybe not.

2:39The arguments were already there

There were already arguments for regime change in Iraq. There were already concerns about Saddam Hussein. There were already people in Washington advocating military action. But the political landscape would have been radically different. And that means one of the biggest geopolitical events of this century becomes uncertain. No invasion? Perhaps no occupation. Perhaps no insurgency in the same form. Perhaps no chain of events that eventually helped create the environment in which Islamic State emerged. But notice the wording. Perhaps.

3:18An educated guess

We are not watching an alternate universe through a window. We are making an educated guess. And history becomes much harder the further we go.

3:30The airport

So let's bring the thought experiment closer to home. Imagine going to an airport in 2026, without that morning ever having happened. You arrive at the terminal. You check in. And airport security is still annoying. But probably not this annoying. Before that morning, security procedures were very different. Knives and other objects that are now prohibited could sometimes be taken through security. Cockpit doors were not designed around the assumption that passengers might try to take over the aircraft. And the enormous security infrastructure we now take for granted had not yet become part of everyday life.

The Transportation Security Administration did not even exist. Today, we simply accept all of this. Take out your laptop. Put your liquids into a tiny plastic bag. Walk through a scanner. And wait.

4:29The rule that quietly ended

And for 25 years, you took off your shoes. Until, in 2025, that rule quietly ended. Because the scanners got better. Not because the fear did.

It feels normal. But it isn't inevitable. It is a response to history. And this is where the consequences become much bigger.

4:53Surveillance

After September 11, the United States dramatically expanded its ability to collect and analyse information. The Patriot Act gave the government new powers. Intelligence agencies received greater resources. Mass surveillance became a central issue in national security. And eventually, Edward Snowden revealed just how extensive some of these programmes had become. But here is the interesting question. Would mass surveillance have happened anyway?

5:25It was already growing

Probably to some degree. The technology was already developing. The internet was growing rapidly. Computers were becoming more powerful. Governments were already interested in communications intelligence. So perhaps the surveillance state would still have grown. But maybe more slowly. Maybe under different laws. Maybe with stronger restrictions. Maybe the public would have reacted differently. Again, we don't know. And this uncertainty is the real problem with our experiment.

6:02The Middle East

Because now things become almost impossible to predict. Remove September 11. Remove the invasion of Afghanistan. Possibly remove the Iraq War. And you have changed the political history of the Middle East. But that doesn't mean you get peace. Iran is still there. Saudi Arabia is still there. Israel and Palestine still have their conflict. Syria still has its internal problems. Saddam Hussein is still ruling Iraq. And governments still have their own interests. There would still be wars. Still be terrorism. Still be revolutions. Still be political disasters.

History doesn't become peaceful because one disaster disappears. It simply becomes a different history.

6:54Would 2026 be better?

So would 2026 be better? Maybe. Maybe not. There are things we can reasonably expect to be different. But there is no reliable way to calculate the entire alternate world. And perhaps that tells us something important about our own world. We often look at history as if it was inevitable. As if the present was always waiting for us. But it wasn't. The world we live in is the result of billions of decisions, accidents, mistakes, inventions, deaths, coincidences and completely unpredictable events. Change just one of them, and the present might look completely different.

7:38Through individual lives

And there is one way to think about this that we have not used yet. Not through governments. Not through wars. Through individual lives. Nearly 3,000 people who died on September 11 would still be alive. Their children would grow up with parents. Their parents would grow old with their children. Some people would marry. Some would have children. Some would start businesses. Some would become grandparents. There would be entire families in 2026 that simply do not exist in our timeline.

8:15Millions of private histories

And there is something almost impossible to comprehend about that. A single morning didn't just change history. It changed millions of private histories. People met different people. Moved to different cities. Joined the military. Changed careers. Lost the people they loved. They made decisions they would never otherwise have made. And those decisions affected other people. And those people affected others. A historical event doesn't only create headlines. It creates an enormous tree of consequences.

8:53September 10

So let's go back to the beginning. September 10, 2001. Imagine that day. People went to work. Planes flew across the United States. People complained about traffic. Someone was late for a meeting. Someone bought a coffee. Someone made plans for the weekend. Nobody knew that history was about to split in two.

9:18Nothing happens

Except in our version of history, it doesn't. September 11 arrives. Nothing happens. And the strange thing is, nobody notices.

There is no breaking news. No emergency broadcast. No moment when everyone remembers exactly where they were. The day simply passes. And 25 years later, people live in a world shaped by events that never happened. They probably don't spend much time wondering about them. Because you don't miss a history you never lived.

9:55Not the only world

And maybe that is the most unsettling thing about history. The world we know is not the only world that could have existed. It is simply the one we happened to get. And somewhere in the enormous space of all the possible histories, there are countless versions of our world that were only one event away. Including one where September 11, 2001, was just another Tuesday.
