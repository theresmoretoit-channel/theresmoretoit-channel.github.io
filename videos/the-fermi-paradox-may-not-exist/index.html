The Fermi Paradox May Not Exist

13 min2026-09-08

This video takes that list apart, one assumption at a time. Aliens should be common. They should become intelligent. They should survive. They should want to expand. And they should leave traces we can recognise. Break any one of them and the paradox stops being paradoxical.

Along the way: why a sample size of one supports almost no conclusion at all, why an advanced civilisation might have no reason to cross a hundred light-years, why the galaxy is not a snapshot but a four-dimensional history, and why searching for loud civilisations might be like searching for teenagers by looking only for abandoned toys.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The question

The universe is thirteen point eight billion years old. And humans appeared here only a tiny fraction of a second ago, on the cosmic timescale. So here's the famous question. Where is everybody?

If intelligent life is common, shouldn't somebody have colonised the galaxy by now? Shouldn't we have seen something? A signal. A spacecraft. A megastructure. Anything. This is the basic idea behind the Fermi paradox. And it sounds like a very simple argument. So… where are the aliens? But there's a problem. The Fermi paradox isn't really a conflict between two observations. We observe one thing. We haven't detected extraterrestrial civilisation. And then we combine it with a large collection of assumptions. They should be common. They should become intelligent. They should develop advanced technology.

They should survive. They should want to expand. And they should leave traces that we can recognise. If even one of those assumptions is wrong, the paradox becomes much less paradoxical. So let's take them apart.

1:19Maybe intelligence is rare

The simplest possibility is that technological intelligence is rare. Maybe life itself is common. But perhaps getting from a primitive cell to a civilisation capable of building radio telescopes is extraordinarily unlikely. And we have one inconvenient problem here. We have exactly one confirmed example of a technological civilisation. Us. That's it. We don't have ten planets with intelligent life. We don't have a thousand. We have one. So when someone says this. "Intelligent life probably evolves like this…" there is a rather obvious question.

Based on what?

Earth could be typical. Or Earth could be a freak accident. We simply don't have enough data to know.

2:11Why would anyone expand?

But let's assume civilisations are common. Would that mean the galaxy should be full of aliens? Not at all. Because intelligence isn't the same thing as expansion. Imagine a civilisation with abundant energy, advanced computers, and virtual reality indistinguishable from the real thing. And then somebody says this. "We should send people to another star." The trip takes centuries. It costs enormous amounts of energy. The destination may contain nothing useful. And even if it does, there is no obvious reason why anyone has to go there. Why?

We assume that an advanced civilisation will automatically become expansionist. But that's a very human assumption. Humans have always expanded. We spread across continents. We crossed oceans. So perhaps we assume every civilisation will do the same on a larger scale. But maybe that's wrong. If your civilisation lives inside computers, why travel hundreds of light-years? Why build cities on another planet when you can build another universe at home? The classic Fermi argument needs something much stronger than this. "Aliens exist."

It needs this. "Aliens exist, and they want to expand." Those are very different claims.

3:40The Great Filter

There's a darker possibility. Perhaps some step on the way from matter to a civilisation is almost impossible to pass. It's often called the Great Filter. Maybe technological civilisations almost always destroy themselves. But we have no evidence that this is what happens. The Great Filter is a possible explanation. It is not an observation.

4:07Maybe we're searching badly

There's another possibility that's almost embarrassingly simple. Maybe we're searching badly. We talk about SETI as though humanity had searched the universe and found nothing. But that's not what happened. We've searched tiny fractions of the possible parameter space. We don't know what frequency an alien civilisation would use, or whether it would transmit at all. We don't even know what technology it would use. For most of human history, we didn't know radio existed.

4:40A Roman with a radio telescope

So imagine giving a radio telescope to an ancient Roman. He points it at the sky. It detects thousands of signals. What does he conclude? Probably nothing. The signals aren't part of his conceptual model of the universe. We could stare directly at an alien civilisation's technological activity and have no idea what we're looking at. The absence of a signal isn't the absence of a civilisation. It's the absence of a signal we know how to recognise.

5:15The galaxy is not one moment

And even if they are out there, the galaxy is enormous. The Milky Way is roughly a hundred thousand light-years across. A civilisation could exist somewhere in the galaxy right now… and its light wouldn't reach us for thousands of years. There could be civilisations that existed millions of years ago, and civilisations that haven't even evolved yet. The galaxy isn't one moment. It's a huge four-dimensional history. We aren't looking at the galaxy as it is. We're looking at different parts of it at different points in their past.

5:53Civilisations may get quieter

And when we imagine aliens, we imagine something remarkably human. They build machines. They communicate. They colonise. But why?

Perhaps technological advancement eventually makes a civilisation less visible. Perhaps the loudest phase of a civilisation is also its shortest. Searching for noisy civilisations could be like searching for teenagers by looking only for abandoned toys.

6:25The Dark Forest

Now we get to one of the most famous ideas. The Dark Forest.

Imagine the galaxy as a dark forest. Every civilisation is a hunter hiding among the trees. You don't know who else is there. You don't know their intentions. And you don't know whether they are stronger than you. So the safest strategy is this. Don't make noise.

If another civilisation discovers you, it might decide to destroy you before you become a threat. Therefore everyone stays silent. And that would neatly explain the Fermi paradox. It's an elegant idea. But there's a problem. A surprisingly big one. How exactly do you hide a planet?

7:17But Earth is not quiet

We don't broadcast deliberate messages to the galaxy all the time. But Earth itself is extremely interesting. We have been modifying our atmosphere, producing artificial chemicals, and generating enormous amounts of radio emission. And all of this is happening on a planet that is already naturally observable. You don't have to detect an alien radio message saying this. "Hello. We are humans." You can look at the planet's atmosphere. You can study its spectrum. You can ask what gases are present. A sufficiently advanced telescope observing Earth as an exoplanet could detect the chemicals in our atmosphere.

Water vapour. Carbon dioxide. Oxygen. Ozone. Of course, detecting oxygen does not automatically mean this. "There are humans here." It could simply mean this. "This planet is interesting." But that's already enough to create a problem for the Dark Forest. The argument doesn't require them to know exactly what we are. It only requires them to notice that something unusual is happening here. And Earth is not merely a rock with a little bit of life. Its atmosphere has been dramatically shaped by biology. And increasingly… by technology.

8:43The really loud part

And atmospheric chemistry isn't even the only problem. Some of our strongest transmissions are not intended for extraterrestrials at all. Powerful radar systems can produce signals that are vastly stronger than ordinary radio leakage. Researchers have investigated how detectable Earth's technological activity could be from other stars. Under favourable circumstances, some of these signals could be detected across thousands of light-years. That does not mean somebody could casually look across the galaxy and say this. "There are humans on Earth."

That's not how astronomy works. Detection depends on distance, direction and sensitivity. But it does mean something much more modest… and much more interesting. Earth is not perfectly hidden.

We are already producing technosignatures. And we're not even trying very hard to hide them.

9:45So does that kill it?

So does that kill the Dark Forest? Not completely. A sufficiently advanced civilisation could perhaps hide much better than we can. But now the Dark Forest has a new problem. It isn't enough for civilisations to be afraid. They also have to be extremely good at hiding. And every one of them has to reach that conclusion independently. Because if even one civilisation decides this. "We don't care. Let's expand." then the galaxy might eventually become much less quiet. The Dark Forest therefore doesn't solve the Fermi paradox. It adds another assumption. Almost everyone must be paranoid.

10:30The wrong question

We started with this. "Where is everybody?" But perhaps that isn't the right question. Maybe the correct question is this. "What exactly should we expect an alien civilisation to look like?" Do we expect it to expand? Why? Do we expect it to remain detectable for millions of years? Why? And most importantly. Do we expect our instruments to recognise it? We have one example of a technological civilisation. One.

And we've existed for an absurdly short time. So perhaps we're making an enormous extrapolation from a sample size of one.

11:17We are difficult to hide

Perhaps somewhere in the galaxy, somebody has already noticed our planet. Not because we sent them a message. But because… we are difficult to hide.

11:31Perhaps there is no paradox

So where does that leave us? Maybe intelligent life is rare. Maybe it's common. Maybe they don't want to expand. Maybe they're hiding. Every one of these possibilities is conceivable. And that's precisely the problem. The Fermi paradox is presented as if we knew one side of the equation. "The universe should be full of civilisations." But we don't. So perhaps there is simply a huge amount of uncertainty… wrapped inside a very catchy question. Where is everybody?

Maybe someone is looking at Earth right now and wondering what the hell is happening on that strange little blue planet. And perhaps the most important question isn't this. "Why haven't we found aliens?" It's this. "If they exist… would we even recognise them when we see them?"
