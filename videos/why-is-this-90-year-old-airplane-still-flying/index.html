Why Is This 90-Year-Old Airplane Still Flying?

10 min2026-10-04

This video goes back to the days when airlines struggled to make money carrying passengers alone, and looks at what the DC-3 changed. It follows what pilots say about the way it flies, what a mechanic can do with it on a gravel strip hundreds of kilometres from the nearest hangar, and what the Second World War did to its future.

It also asks why we aren't still flying them everywhere, and what the Basler BT-67, a DC-3 airframe with turboprop engines, says about the way technology really moves forward.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A 90-year-old airplane at work

Imagine you are standing on a runway somewhere in the Canadian Arctic. It is freezing. There is no major airport nearby. No repair centre. No endless supply of spare parts. And then an airplane arrives. It has two giant radial engines. Its cockpit is full of round mechanical instruments. No screens. No fly-by-wire. No computers. This is the Douglas DC-3. It first flew in 1935. During the Second World War, more than ten thousand military versions were built. And more than ninety years after that first flight, some of them are still working.

Not in a museum. Hauling cargo and supplies to remote places. Most airliners are retired after about twenty-five years. Some DC-3s still at work are more than three times that age. So what did Douglas get so incredibly right?

1:01Flying before the DC-3

To understand that, we need to go back to the early days of passenger flying. Airliners were noisy. They were slow. They were uncomfortable. And many airlines couldn't make money carrying passengers alone. They depended on mail contracts and subsidies.

1:19From the DC-2 to the DC-3

Then Douglas built the DC-2. All-metal construction. Retractable landing gear. A streamlined fuselage. Twin engines. And then Douglas took what worked… and made it bigger, more comfortable and more capable. The result was the DC-3. And suddenly, passenger flying made economic sense. It was fast enough. It carried enough people. It had enough range. And it was reliable enough. For the first time, an airline could make money carrying passengers alone. The DC-3 didn't just become a successful airplane. It helped turn air travel into a real industry.

2:07So what was the secret?

So what was the secret? There wasn't one. And that's the interesting part. The DC-3 didn't have one magical technology. Almost everything about it was good at the same time. And above all, it was good at the one thing an airliner has to do. Make money. Douglas made the cabin wider. Twenty-one seats instead of fourteen. Fifty percent more seats, on every flight. It wasn't the fastest airliner. It wasn't the biggest. It didn't have the longest range. But it was good enough at almost everything that mattered.

2:33Good enough at almost everything

Its wings were relatively large for its weight. That let it fly slowly and safely, and use short, rough runways. Its structure was strong. Its systems were simple. And with two engines, if one failed, the other could keep it flying. Not indefinitely. But long enough to give the pilots options. That matters enormously when the nearest airport is hundreds of kilometres away.

3:10The way it flies

And then there was the way it flew. Pilots describe the DC-3 as stable and predictable. If you were slightly too slow, the airplane warned you before things got really bad. And a small movement of the controls gave you a small, smooth response. Never a sudden lurch. It even talked to you. Some veteran airline pilots claimed they could feel a stewardess walking up the aisle with coffee. Her weight shifted the balance, and they felt it in the controls. Landing was gentle, too. Those big wings settled softly as the airplane neared the ground, and it had little tendency to bounce. Some pilots say it's easier to land than many small private airplanes.

3:55Forgiving

Pilots have a word for that. Forgiving. And think about where the DC-3 spent its working life. Bad weather. Short gravel strips. Long days. Pilots who were tired, or still new to the job. An airplane that punishes every small mistake doesn't last long in that kind of life. One that forgives them might. It doesn't mean the DC-3 couldn't crash. It absolutely could. It means small mistakes didn't immediately turn into catastrophic ones. And in aviation, that room to recover can be priceless. As one DC-3 pilot put it, it's slow to get you into trouble… but even slower to get you out of it.

4:42Nobody planned for ninety years

But here's the really weird part. Nobody in 1935 set out to build an airplane that would still be flying in 2026. The goal was simply a really good airliner. But some of the designers' choices aged remarkably well. An aluminium structure. Mechanical controls. Mechanical engines. A mechanic could understand most of the machine just by looking at it. And that becomes extremely important when your airplane is more than eighty years old.

5:14Fixing it far from a hangar

When something breaks on a modern aircraft, you may need special diagnostic equipment, a replacement electronic module, software, and someone certified to work on it. Now imagine doing that in the Arctic. That's where the DC-3's old-fashioned design becomes an advantage. Picture one on a gravel strip, hundreds of kilometres from the nearest hangar. Something isn't working. A mechanic can often open a panel and actually see the problem. A cable. A pulley. A leaking line. No error code. Just the part itself. And generations of mechanics have learned how to keep these airplanes alive.

That doesn't make it easy. The problem is no longer understanding the machine. It's finding parts. Finding specialists. And satisfying modern aviation rules. The airplane may be simple. The world around it isn't.

6:15The right moment, and a war

The DC-3 also arrived at exactly the right moment. Aerodynamics, aluminium construction and aircraft engines had all matured at the same time. And then history gave it an enormous push. During the war, the military version was called the C-47. That's where most of those ten thousand came from. They carried troops, cargo, paratroopers and medical supplies, across enormous distances, in brutal conditions. And suddenly there were thousands of people who knew how to fly them… and thousands more who knew how to fix them.

6:51From an airplane to an ecosystem

After the war, many of those aircraft were sold for civilian use. And the DC-3 stopped being just an airplane. It became an ecosystem. Parts. Mechanics. Pilots. Training. Experience. And airplanes everywhere.

Sometimes a machine survives not because it is perfect… but because so many people know how to keep it alive.

7:18Why aren't we still using them?

So why aren't we still using them everywhere? Because being durable isn't the same as being efficient. A modern turboprop carries more, burns less fuel for every passenger, and has far more capable avionics. And fuel isn't free. Neither is maintenance. So why would anyone still use one? Because efficiency isn't everything. Remote areas. Rough airfields. Specialised cargo work. Places where being able to operate independently matters more than squeezing out the last percent of efficiency.

7:56Old airframe, new engines

Some DC-3s have even been rebuilt. The radial engines replaced with turboprops. The old cockpit with modern avionics. One famous example is the Basler BT-67. It starts with a DC-3 airframe, and replaces much of what's inside. It's like taking an eighty-year-old classic car… and fitting it with a modern engine and modern electronics. The body survives. The concept survives. The technology around it changes.

8:30What the DC-3 says about progress

We often imagine technological progress as a straight line. New technology replaces the old, and the old stuff disappears. But sometimes, an old design gets something fundamentally right. And then newer technology doesn't destroy it. It gets added to it. The DC-3 doesn't have to compete with a modern airliner. It just has to keep doing the job it was exceptionally good at. And apparently, it is very difficult to convince this particular airplane to retire.

So somewhere in the Arctic, more than ninety years after the first DC-3 took to the sky… one of them may be starting its engines. And flying to work, like it has for generations.
