Why Would Anyone Build a Radio You Can't Tune?

9 min2026-10-13

From Soviet "radio points" and Cold War planning to Swiss mountain valleys, classical music down Italian telephone lines and loudspeakers rented out in British towns: the story of wired radio, and of the night it carried the news someone had tried to silence.

Watch on YouTubeSubscribe on YouTube

The full narration of the video.

0:00One night in 1968

It's almost two in the morning, on the twenty-first of August, 1968. In Prague, a radio announcer begins to read a statement. Foreign armies have crossed the border during the night. And the statement says it happened without the knowledge of the country's own leaders. As he reads, the country's medium-wave transmitters go silent. Someone has switched them off. Across the country, radios hiss. But in kitchens, offices and workshops, a small box on the wall keeps talking. It carries the statement to the very last word. That box had no tuning dial. No antenna. No buttons for other stations.

One cable, one volume knob, and one programme… whatever came through the wall. So how did the one radio that couldn't choose anything end up carrying the news everyone else had lost?

0:55Not really a radio

Because it wasn't really a radio. It never picked up a single radio wave. In Czech its name simply meant broadcasting by wire. Most people shortened that to a nickname: the wire box. Not the loudspeakers on poles in the village square. This one lived indoors. The programme travelled from a central station, through cables, into the home. Like a telephone call that never ended. At the amplifier, the signal was stepped up to a hundred and twenty volts, so it could run for kilometres along the line. Near the listener, a transformer brought it back down to thirty volts.

And inside the box, there was almost nothing. A transformer, a loudspeaker, and a knob that clicked between volume steps. Nothing to tune… because there was nothing to tune into. And switching off a transmitter does nothing to a cable. That night in 1968, the wire simply kept going.

2:02Why build a second network?

But that only makes it stranger. Why would a country build a whole second radio network, cable by cable, into people's homes? The first Czechoslovak lines went live in 1953. And the idea came from the Soviet Union. The Soviets began building wired radio networks in the 1920s. A simple loudspeaker was far cheaper than a conventional radio receiver. For millions of families, it was their only way to listen to the radio. They called it the radio point. In its simplest form, one socket in the wall, one programme from the central service, or from a local station.

For a government, it solved two problems at once. It was cheap to put a voice into every home… and it was the government's voice. And the reasoning was military. In a war, a radio transmitter is a big, obvious target. A major transmitter could serve an entire region. If it went down, a huge area could lose its signal. A cable network doesn't depend on one big mast. It has its own amplifiers, its own lines, its own route. Knock out the transmitter, and the wire can still talk. It wasn't invincible. The amplifiers needed electricity. Cables could be cut. It was a spare route, not a fortress.

And it had everyday advantages, too. No need to fight a weak indoor signal. No radio interference to contend with. And a receiver so simple that it cost very little.

3:41One cable, one voice

But the design had one more consequence. And a one-party state could hardly have asked for a better one. The listener couldn't choose. The programme was decided for you. One cable, one voice. Not all of it was propaganda. There was music, stories for children, radio plays, the weather. Plenty of people left it on all day, just for company. But all of it arrived through a channel the state owned from end to end. There was no dial to turn to Vienna, or to Radio Free Europe. What the state couldn't control was the off switch. Or whether anyone was actually listening.

And many homes had an ordinary radio as well. The box for the kitchen, the real radio for the evening… and, discreetly, for stations from the West.

4:30Democracies did it too

So it's tempting to call it a control device dressed up as a radio. Except that democracies wired up radio too. Switzerland started its own version in 1931. In deep mountain valleys, reception was often poor, so programmes went down the telephone line instead. Italy launched a similar service in 1958, also over telephone lines, with several channels… including one of nothing but classical music. And in British towns, from the late 1920s, companies rented out loudspeakers connected by wire. The same idea, doing very different jobs.

In the Alps, it beat the mountains. In Italy, it offered choice. In Czechoslovakia, it was a spare route for war… with one channel, chosen by the state. The technology didn't decide what it was for. That depended on who controlled the network… and the programme. And in August 1968, the people controlling the transmitters turned out not to control every route.

5:37Back to that night

Back to that night. Armies of five Warsaw Pact countries, led by the Soviet Union, had crossed the border to end the Prague Spring… months in which the Communist Party itself had tried to loosen its grip. The announcer, Vladimír Fišer, was reading a statement from the party's own leadership. The troops, it said, had come without the knowledge of the president, parliament, the government, or the party's first secretary. And it called the invasion a denial of the basic norms of international law. But the man in charge of the country's communications, Karel Hoffmann, was a hardliner who backed the invasion. He had the medium-wave transmitters shut down just as those words went out.

Here's the irony. A network built in case foreign armies came. And on the night they came, the transmitters went dark… but the wired network stayed on. So the statement reached listeners in full. Only by wire. The wire didn't stop a single tank. It was cables and amplifiers, nothing more. But for those first hours, the box that couldn't choose anything at all was the one that told people what was happening.

And it wasn't the end of the story. Later that morning, the broadcast fell silent again. Around eleven, it came back… first from a bunker-like emergency studio elsewhere in Prague. Then from a studio the troops had missed… hidden inside the radio building, with soldiers already in its corridors. In the days that followed, studios across the country took turns keeping the broadcast going, like a relay. The wire was one backup. The people running the radio turned out to be another.

7:26What happened to Hoffmann

And Karel Hoffmann? He didn't fall. He rose. For the next two decades, he sat near the top of the Communist Party. The regime ended in 1989. And in 2003, thirty-five years after that night, a Czech appeals court convicted him of sabotage, for the order to switch off the transmitters. He went to prison in 2004, at the age of eighty… and was released less than a month later, on health grounds.

The wire outlived his career, but not by much.

8:01The end of the wire

Cheap radios got better. FM brought clearer sound and several stations, with no cable to every building. Next to that, a radio with one programme had nothing left to offer. After 1989, the networks kept running for a while. Then, one by one, they were switched off. In 1999, the last one went silent.

8:27Who holds the other end?

It's easy to remember the box as a propaganda speaker. And it was built inside a system that decided what people heard. But the same idea carried radio through the Swiss mountains, and classical music across Italy. And for one night in 1968, the wire carried the very news someone had tried to silence. A wire doesn't care what it carries. What matters is who holds the other end… and whether there's another radio in the house.
