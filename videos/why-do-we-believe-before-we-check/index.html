Why We Do What We Do

Why Do We Believe Before We Check?

10 min2026-09-21

That isn't a problem with the internet. It starts with something your brain does before the headline even lands — and the reason it's still there is that fear used to be cheap and being wrong about fear used to be fatal.

We go from a noise in the bushes to a street full of frightened people who never received a single piece of new information, to an election, to a 2005 study in which one second of looking at a face predicted the winner of roughly seven out of ten Senate races.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A headline, and then a question

You see a headline. You get angry. You share it. And only later do you ask yourself whether it was actually true. Why? Why do we believe before we check? It would be comforting to blame the internet for that. It isn't the internet. It starts with something your brain does before the headline even lands.

0:22A brain that doesn't wait

Imagine hearing a noise in the bushes. You could stop and work out the probability that it wants to eat you. Or you could run. Evolution never waited for the results. If there was a predator, the one who ran survived. If there wasn't, almost nothing was lost. Fear was cheap. Being wrong about fear was expensive. So we ended up with a brain that reacts first and checks afterwards. Only the bushes have changed. Instead of a tiger, a headline. Instead of thirty people in a valley, a few billion, all within reach.

1:00Fear spreads without information

And that last change does something the first one doesn't. One person becomes frightened. Their neighbour sees it. "What's going on?" Now the neighbour is nervous too. And somebody else sees the nervous people. Within a few minutes, a street full of people is afraid. Notice what nobody in that street got. New information. They saw other people being afraid, and that was enough. It works the other way just as well. Excitement spreads. So does certainty. A crowd doesn't only panic. It can also decide together that something is going to be wonderful. And the more people believe it, the more convincing it looks.

1:45Now think about an election

Now hold that thought, and think about an election. We like to picture it as a rational process. Several parties, each with a programme. The voter studies the proposals, weighs up the consequences, and picks the best one. How many voters have ever finished a whole programme? How many check the arithmetic behind the promises? And how many simply think: "I like her." "I trust him." "That one seems confident." "I can't stand that man."

2:17One second and a face

In 2005, a group of researchers tested how far that goes. They showed people photographs of two candidates they had never seen before. No names. No parties. No policies. One second of looking, and one question: which of these two faces looks more competent? Those snap judgements picked the winner in roughly seven out of ten American Senate races. One second. A face. Nothing else. We don't even need to hear someone speak before we start judging them.

Which means the same sentence, delivered calmly and steadily… …or delivered nervously, is not really the same sentence. The information can be identical. Our reaction isn't. And now put that machinery in front of something built to use it.

3:10Why a false story wins

We could say that people believe nonsense because they're stupid. That explanation is far too easy. A false story doesn't have to win by having better arguments. It can win by reaching the right feeling first. Somebody is frightened? They are coming for what you have. Somebody is angry? Here is exactly who did it to you. Somebody feels ignored? Everyone lies to you. We are the only ones who don't. And a story that lands on a feeling gets clicked, and shared, and put in front of more people. Which pays whoever published it. So more of it gets made.

So that's our answer, isn't it. Emotions are faster than reason, and the modern world knows it.

4:00But wait

But wait.

If our feelings push us around this easily, what are we supposed to do about it? Stop trusting our own judgement? Check everything ourselves? That sounds right until you try it exactly once.

4:16Try checking one thing

Pick one claim about the economy. Just one. Find the original data. Find out who collected it, and how. Understand the statistics well enough to know what that number cannot say. Find the studies that came out the other way. And then work out whether the people who study this actually disagree with each other, or only sound as if they do. That is one claim. Now do medicine. Energy. Law. Climate. History. Finance. Nobody has that many lifetimes.

So the real question was never how to check everything. It's how to decide who to trust when we can't.

4:58Needing to trust isn't weakness

And needing to trust somebody is not a weakness. When your car stops working, you don't become a mechanic first. When the power goes out, you don't sit down and study the national grid. You rely on somebody who has spent years on it. Which is why the sentence "I don't trust scientists" deserves a proper answer, and not an eye roll.

5:20Science gets things wrong

Because the worry behind it is fair. Scientists are not infallible. Studies get overturned. Whole fields have been wrong for decades at a time. And when better evidence arrives, the answer changes. That isn't science failing. That is the only thing that makes it worth anything. It also tells you what we are really trusting. Not a person who cannot be wrong. A process that is built to catch the people who are.

But watch what usually happens next. Somebody says they don't trust the experts… …and then believes a video instead. No evidence was examined. No field was studied. No better source was found. One authority was simply swapped for a different one. Usually for the one that says what we hoped to hear. That isn't critical thinking. Critical thinking never meant "I believe nothing." It means "I want a good reason to believe it."

6:25Four questions anyone can ask

And here is the useful part. You usually can't judge the claim. But you can nearly always judge the way it is being made. Does the source show you where the information came from? Does it correct itself when it turns out to be wrong? Does it tell you what would change its mind? Does it ever say "this part we don't know yet"? Try it on the next thing that crosses your screen. A new study proves something. You can't evaluate the study. But you can ask whether anybody has shown you the study at all, or only a screenshot of a number with nobody's name on it.

And a source that has never once told you it got something wrong isn't more reliable than the others. It just isn't checking. None of those questions require you to be an expert. They only require you to look. It isn't proof. It is a far better bet than how somebody made you feel.

7:33Being clever doesn't protect you

And now the uncomfortable part. You might expect that being clever protects you from all of this. It doesn't.

Researchers gave people a genuinely difficult maths problem about some data. For half of them, the data was about a skin cream. For the other half, exactly the same numbers were about a law on guns. On the skin cream, the people who were best with numbers did best. Obviously. On the gun law, they didn't. They split along political lines harder than everybody else did. Being good at reasoning had not made them better at finding the truth. It made them better at defending what they already thought. That is the trap. Not stupidity. Skill, pointed the wrong way.

It is also why "do your own research" can leave you worse off than when you started. You go looking… and you find what you hoped to find.

8:35And this includes us

And notice how easy it is to enjoy that sentence. Those people. The ones who fall for propaganda. The ones who share nonsense. Same brain.

We also have opinions we would prefer to be true. We also have people we believe automatically. We can also be furious at a headline before we have reached the end of it. So perhaps the question isn't whether people are rational or emotional. We are obviously both. We built mathematics and rockets. And a single moment of fear can still move us in under a second.

9:17Ten seconds

Which leaves one small habit that is worth the effort. When something makes you feel strongly and immediately… …notice that the feeling arrived first. Then ask who is telling you this. And how they could possibly know. And then the hardest one of all. Do I have a reason to believe this, or do I just want to?

It costs about ten seconds. And sometimes, ten seconds is enough.

Because "I don't know" is a complete answer. It is a much better answer than "I know, because I believe it."
