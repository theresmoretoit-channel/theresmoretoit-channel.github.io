How Society Actually Runs

Was There More Nonsense Before the Internet?

10 min2026-08-26

So were people back then more gullible than we are? This is the story of six technologies that each made it faster to spread knowledge, and exactly as much faster to spread nonsense: the spoken rumour, the printing press, the newspaper, radio, the internet, and now AI. Along the way: a massacre justified by a lie the Pope had already refuted, the most famous mass panic in history that never actually happened, and a study of 126,000 stories that found the problem is not the bots.

The full narration of the video.

0:00A monster in the north

Imagine you live in the fourteenth century. No internet. No television. No newspapers. Most of the people around you cannot read. One day a traveller arrives, and he says: "In the north, a monster has appeared. It has the body of a man, the head of a dog, and it eats children." How do you find out whether that is true? You don't. There is no way. Maybe you don't believe him. But maybe, that evening, you mention it to your neighbour. He tells a few more people. And within a week, half the country knows there is a monster in the north.

0:36Were people back then more stupid?

Today we would laugh at that. But were people in the past really so much more stupid than we are? And did more nonsense actually spread back then than it does now?

People have always believed strange things. A comet could be read as a warning that something terrible was coming. And when the plague arrived, the best medical minds in Europe concluded that the air itself had gone rotten. That was not a fringe theory. That was the official answer. But most of those people were not stupid. They simply did not have the information we have. And with no way to check anything, a story changes every time it is retold, until it has almost nothing to do with whatever actually happened.

1:24Lies with a purpose - Strasbourg, 1349

Of course, it was not only ignorance. Deliberate lies are as old as we are. Rulers needed propaganda. Politicians needed to justify wars. People spread rumours about their enemies. And during an epidemic, somebody always had to be blamed. On the fourteenth of February, thirteen forty-nine, the city of Strasbourg burned its entire Jewish community. About two thousand people, according to the city's own chronicler. The accusation was that they had poisoned the wells, and caused the plague. It was a lie. The Pope had written, months earlier, that the plague was killing people in places where no Jews lived at all.

It spread anyway. City after city, for another two years. So, no. Disinformation is not an invention of the internet. The internet only changed its speed, and its reach.

2:23Print, newspapers, radio: the multiplier

And then came new technology. The printing press could make hundreds of copies of the same text. Historians estimate that around twenty-eight thousand different editions from the first decades of printing still survive today. Even at a conservative few hundred copies each, printing had already produced millions of books. Newspapers could inform a whole region. Radio and television could reach millions of people at once. And the pattern kept repeating. The new technology made it faster to spread knowledge. And just as much faster to spread nonsense and propaganda.

So the technology was not good, and it was not bad. It was a multiplier. It multiplied truth and lies alike.

3:12The panic that never happened

And here is my favourite example. In nineteen thirty-eight, Orson Welles put a radio play about an invasion from Mars on the air. And it supposedly sent the whole of America into a panic. You have probably heard the story. Millions of terrified listeners. People running out of their homes. Except that it didn't happen.

The service that measured radio audiences rang five thousand households that night and asked what they were listening to. Two per cent said the Welles programme. And not one newspaper ever verified a single death caused by the panic. The panic was reported by newspapers, which at that moment were losing their advertising money to radio. A story about the new medium driving listeners out of their minds was extremely convenient. So the most famous example of a medium spreading mass hysteria is itself a piece of disinformation. Spread by the older medium. About the newer one.

4:20The internet: the library and the pub

And then came the internet, and that changed the rules completely. Somebody makes a claim? You can look it up. Checking a fact has never in history been as easy as it is right now. But the internet also let everybody publish. Reaching a million people used to need a newspaper, a radio station, or a television channel. Now it needs a phone and an account. So we got the largest library in history. And also the largest pub in history, where anybody can shout across the room. And if it is shocking enough, a million people hear it by the evening.

5:00Why nonsense travels further

And it doesn't even have to be a lie. Our brains love information that is surprising, frightening or outrageous. So sensational nonsense can travel further than boring truth. "Researchers analysed the data and found…" Boring. "SCIENTISTS DISCOVER THE SHOCKING TRUTH THEY ARE HIDING FROM YOU!" Click. And that is not just a feeling. It has been measured. A study published in Science followed a hundred and twenty-six thousand stories spreading across Twitter. A false story was seventy per cent more likely to be passed on than a true one. The truth took about six times as long to reach fifteen hundred people.

True stories almost never got past about a thousand people. The most successful false ones reached up to a hundred thousand. And then the researchers did something that surprised everybody. They took the bots out of the data. The bots had been spreading true stories and false stories at the same rate. The difference was not the machines. The difference was us.

6:08Too much information

So here is where it gets interesting. The problem in the Middle Ages was not having enough information. Our problem is the opposite. We have far too much.

6:22What AI changes

And now another large change is arriving. Artificial intelligence. The internet let us search for information. AI can produce it. You can ask about a historical event, a scientific problem, or anything you don't understand. And within a few seconds you get a perfectly worded answer. But a perfectly worded answer does not have to be a correct one. A model can produce a very convincing claim that is simply wrong. And that is not all. AI can now also make images. And video. And voices. So a false claim no longer has to be a sentence typed somewhere on the internet.

It can have a photograph. It can have footage. It can have the voice of somebody who supposedly witnessed the whole thing. It can even arrive with a very persuasive assistant, ready to explain it all to you. And that is a new kind of problem. Not only: is this true? But also: who, or what, made it?

7:26So, were they more gullible?

So were people back then more stupid? Probably not. A medieval person had far less knowledge, and far fewer ways to check anything. We have the opposite problem. An enormous amount of knowledge, an enormous amount of nonsense, and tools that manufacture nonsense at scale. So perhaps we are asking the wrong question. "Were there more lies in the past?" A better question is: "How hard was it to find out that something was false?" And there, the difference is enormous. In the Middle Ages you heard a story from a traveller, and you had no way to check it.

Today you can find the sources, compare the accounts, and track down the original data. In minutes. So perhaps we are not living in an age with more lies in it than ever before. We are living in an age that holds the best tools ever built for finding truth, and the best tools ever built for manufacturing nonsense.

8:25The skill that actually matters

Printing let us copy information. Radio and television broadcast it. The internet handed it to everybody. And AI has started producing it. Every one of those gave us something. And every one of them created a new way for information to mislead us. So maybe the most important skill now is not knowing everything. That isn't possible anyway. It is being able to say: "I don't know. Let's check."

Because when somebody tells us today that there is a monster in the north with the head of a dog… we don't have to believe it, and we don't have to disbelieve it. We can just open the internet. And look.
