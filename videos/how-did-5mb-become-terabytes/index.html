How Did 5 MB Become Terabytes?

11 min2026-09-15

The obvious explanation is that we got better at building small machines. That explanation is true and it is the boring half. The machine got about a thousand times lighter. The storage got about a billion times denser. Everything interesting about storage is hiding in that gap.

On the disk IBM shipped in 1956, one square centimetre held three hundred bits. A modern drive puts nearly three hundred billion into the same square centimetre. This video is about how that happened: the head that had to fly without touching, the compressor that disappeared when someone shaped the head like a wing, the physical effect two laboratories found independently in 1988 that made weak signals readable, and the wall that arrived when a magnetic grain became small enough for room temperature to flip it on its own.

The full narration of the video.

0:00The machine that weighed a ton

In 1956, IBM shipped the first hard drive. It held about five megabytes. The machine was taller than the person operating it, and with the compressor that kept it running, it weighed more than a ton. Five megabytes is one photograph from a modern phone. Today you can hold a drive in one hand that stores nearly ten million times more. So what happened in between?

0:27A thousand against a billion

The obvious answer is that we got better at building small machines. That answer isn't wrong. It's just the boring half. Because the machine got about a thousand times lighter. The storage got about a billion times denser. A thousand against a billion. Everything interesting about storage is hiding in that gap.

To see where the billion came from, you have to start with what a hard drive was actually for.

0:57Tape is a queue

Before it, computers kept their data on punched cards and on magnetic tape. Tape works. But tape is a queue. If the thing you want sits at the end, you travel through everything before it. A machine could spend its afternoon winding backwards and forwards. The new machine could go straight to it. That was the whole point of it, and it is what the name says. Random access.

1:24Fifty disks, twenty times a second

Fifty metal disks, sixty centimetres across, spinning twenty times a second. An arm moved between them and put a magnetic head over the right track. It could find any piece of data in under a second. In 1956, that bordered on magic.

1:42The head must not touch

And it came with a problem that has never gone away. The head must not touch the disk. Touch it, and you scrape away the data and the head together. But it has to stay near enough to feel one magnetic patch. IBM's answer was to float it on compressed air. The head hovered twenty micrometres above the surface. Which is why the machine needed its own air compressor. Two hundred kilograms of that ton was a pump, blowing air under a head.

2:14What five megabytes really was

And that five megabytes needs a footnote, because it wasn't five. It was five million characters of six bits each. In today's money, three and three quarter megabytes. But here is the number that matters, and nobody ever quotes it.

2:32Three hundred bits

On that disk, one square centimetre held three hundred bits. Three hundred. A modern drive puts nearly three hundred billion into the same square centimetre. That is where the billion came from. Not from the box. From the bit.

2:50The compressor goes

The box did shrink, of course, and the first thing to go was that compressor. In 1961, IBM shaped the head like a tiny wing, so that the spinning disk made its own cushion of air. No pump. No plumbing. And the head could now fly three times nearer the surface, which bought thirteen times the density. In 1973 came a drive nicknamed Winchester. It sealed the disks and the heads into one clean assembly, so that nothing could get in.

3:24What a bit actually is

What made storage grow was never the box. It was making the bit smaller. So what is a bit, physically? The disk is coated with a magnetic film made of tiny grains. A bit is a patch of those grains, magnetised the same way. Point them one way for a zero, and the other way for a one. Want more capacity? Then use fewer grains for each bit. Every generation did exactly that. Smaller grains. Narrower tracks. Arms that could aim more precisely. But there is a price.

3:59The signal gets too weak

A smaller patch makes a weaker signal. Halve the patch, and the head has half as much to listen to. By the late eighties, it was getting thin.

Then physics handed the industry something it had not asked for.

4:14Fert and Grünberg

In 1988, Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg independently found the same strange effect. Stack thin magnetic layers with a sliver of metal between them, and the resistance of the stack depends on how the layers line up. A small magnetic field. A large change in resistance. They called it giant magnetoresistance. Which is a read head. A magnetic patch goes past the sensor, and the sensor's resistance jumps. Small signals stop being a problem. IBM shipped the first one in 1997. The Nobel Prize for the discovery came in 2007. By then the effect had been sitting inside ordinary computers for a decade.

5:02A billion times

And the numbers it unlocked are absurd. Three hundred bits per square centimetre in 1956. Nearly three hundred billion now. Roughly a billion times more data on the same piece of surface. That is how five megabytes became terabytes.

5:30The wall

Except that isn't the end of the story. It's the point where the story hits a wall. Because you cannot keep taking grains away from a bit. A grain holds its direction because flipping it costs energy. Shrink the grain, and that cost falls. And a disk is not a quiet place. Room temperature is atoms shoving each other about. Make the grain small enough, and an ordinary thermal nudge is enough to turn it over. The bit flips. On its own. Nobody wrote anything.

6:04Running out of atoms

Physicists call it the superparamagnetic limit. Engineers had a blunter way to put it. They were running out of atoms. Around the turn of the century, the old way of recording ran into it, somewhere near fifteen billion bits per square centimetre. For the first time in forty years, the curve was about to stop. So the industry cheated. Twice.

6:30Standing the bits upright

The first cheat was to notice that the disk has a dimension nobody had been using. Until then, each bit lay flat, stretched along its track, like a word printed on a page. Flat things get thin when you shrink them. So the magnetisation was turned through ninety degrees, to point down into the disk instead. When there was no more room on the surface, we started standing the bits upright. A standing bit can be narrow and still be deep, so it keeps enough material to hold its direction. Drives started shipping that way in 2005. Every hard drive you have bought since works like this.

7:12A laser in the head

The second cheat is stranger. If the material is too stable to write, heat it first. So they put a laser in the head.

It heats one spot on the disk to about four hundred and fifty degrees for a single nanosecond. Hot, the spot is soft enough to write. A nanosecond later it has cooled, and the bit is frozen where it was put. There is a laser inside the hard drive, melting the problem out of the way a few hundred degrees at a time. This is not a laboratory. You can buy it.

7:50Take the disk out entirely

Meanwhile, somebody else was asking a different question. What if we take the disk out entirely? No platter. No arm. No head. Nothing that has to fly. Nothing that has to spin. Flash memory stores a bit as trapped electric charge, sealed behind an insulator inside a cell. Put charge in, and the cell needs a higher voltage to switch on. Measure that voltage, and you know what was stored. Nothing moves, so nothing has to be found. Every cell is equally near. The seek time the RAMAC was built around simply stops existing.

8:29Flash hits its own wall

And flash ran the same race. Smaller cells. More of them. And it hit the same kind of wall. Because the charge in a cell is not really a quantity. It is a number of electrons. Shrink the cell, and that number falls, until a handful of electrons leaking away is the difference between a one and a zero. And it got worse, because the industry had started storing more than one bit in each cell. Four bits means sixteen different charge levels inside one cell, and the gaps between them are a few hundred millivolts. Smaller cells make those gaps smaller. So the same wall arrived, in a technology with no moving parts, for entirely different reasons.

9:14The same answer

And in 2013 the answer arrived. It was the same answer. Samsung stopped making the cells smaller and started stacking them on top of each other. Twenty-four layers, in the first one. Today the count is in the hundreds, and the roadmaps talk about a thousand. The chip did not get wider. It got taller.

Put the two technologies side by side. The bit cannot get any smaller, so stand it upright. The cell cannot get any smaller, so stack the cells. Different materials. Different physics. Different companies. The same move.

9:57The old technology moved

And the old technology did not die. It moved. More than nine tenths of the storage in cloud data centres is still spinning disks, because per byte it is still far cheaper. So the seventy-year story of storage is not really a story about miniaturisation.

10:15We built upwards

We shrank things. We reached the floor of what matter will put up with. And then we built upwards.

Five million characters once needed fifty disks, a motor, a compressor, and a room to keep them in. The same data now sits in a patch of film you could not see without an electron microscope. What got smaller was never the machine. It was the amount of matter we need in order to remember one thing. And every time physics told us that was as small as it goes, we found another direction to build in.
